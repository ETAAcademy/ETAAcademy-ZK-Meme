# Building Dependable AI Agents: From Conversation to Control

A customer asks an AI agent to return a pair of shoes. The request sounds simple, but completing it requires a chain of decisions: identify the order, verify the customer, interpret the return policy, update the business system, and explain what happens next. If the customer interrupts or an integration fails, the agent must adjust. A fluent confirmation is useful only when the return has actually been recorded.

The same problem appears in less familiar settings. A StarCraft agent must respond to an opponent it cannot fully observe. A robot wiping a whiteboard must coordinate its base, arm, and camera while its movements change what it sees. An email assistant must understand a message without accepting every instruction inside it as permission to act.

These examples share a central engineering challenge: turning a capable model into a system that produces dependable consequences. The model needs useful information and an effective action interface. Its learning process needs experiences that expose weaknesses. Its evaluator needs evidence of success. Its execution environment needs boundaries that remain effective when a decision is wrong.

Together, these perspectives suggest an engineering discipline built around complete interactions: define the intended outcome, provide the information and actions needed to reach it, evaluate actual consequences, and use observed failures to improve the system. Capability, reliability, and authorization require distinct mechanisms even when they meet in the same interaction.

## 1. Delivering a Reliable Customer Experience

A customer returning shoes needs more than an accurate explanation: the agent must identify the order, verify eligibility, execute the return, and confirm the resulting business state. Memory, integrations, voice, evaluation, and security determine whether fluent conversation becomes dependable service. An agent represents a business, and its promises have consequences beyond the conversation.

### Conversation as a business interface

Personal agents assist individuals; role-based agents support professional work; customer-facing agents represent businesses across recommendations, troubleshooting, returns, and renewals. It compares the opportunity for agents in AI with the role of websites in the internet era and apps in mobile computing. Each makes a broad technology accessible through a practical interface. A customer can explain a problem in their own words, while the system translates that request into the steps the business supports.

This contrasts the internet's roughly eleven-year path to 10% weekly active use with ChatGPT reaching that level in under twenty-five months. These figures illustrate the pace of adoption, but the account does not establish comparable population denominators or measurement methods. The engineering opportunity does not depend on treating the comparison as a precise growth-rate estimate.

Different channels can share a definition of what the agent knows, what it can do, and which policies it follows. Phone, messaging, web chat, and email still need distinct interaction designs. A refund decision should remain consistent across them even when its spoken explanation is shorter than its written version. This separation lets a business improve policy without rewriting each channel independently.

Outcome-based pricing connects revenue to completed service: the business pays when the agent successfully resolves an issue. Defining resolution therefore precedes optimization. A recorded return is verifiable, while an adequate explanation needs additional judgment. Success must also include the conditions under which an action is permitted. Completing a transaction for the wrong customer cannot count as successful service merely because an API returned a success response.

Production adds versioning, observability, release management, recovery, latency control, and policy enforcement. A managed platform offers value when its abstractions preserve customization and make failures visible. The business specification can separate three responsibilities: knowledge supplies facts, tools enable actions, and policies define how those actions may be used. Business experts can maintain product information and procedures while engineers implement integrations and enforce access constraints.

These responsibilities still require conventional software engineering. Deterministic tool adapters can have unit and integration tests, while probabilistic behavior requires repeated trials and representative conversations. A change in a model, retrieval system, or upstream service can alter the customer experience even when the high-level business instructions remain identical.

### Memory creates continuity

A returning customer should not need to reconstruct every previous interaction. Interaction memory supplies prior questions and promises, while a customer data platform (CDP) supplies structured account facts. Those sources answer different questions: a conversation explains why a customer is frustrated, while the account record establishes whether a return has already been opened.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) can retrieve either shared knowledge or customer-specific memory. Persistent memory additionally requires storage, updating, retention, deletion, and correct customer association. Relevance alone is insufficient. Older summaries must not override current account records, and one customer's information must not enter another's context. A useful warm start gives the agent the history needed for the current task without treating every past statement as permanently authoritative.

Connecting interactions to outcomes supports policy improvement. Teams can compare support or retention strategies across customers, then refine them through repeated evaluation. Measurements can be noisy and objectives incomplete, so incremental improvement does not establish global optimality. A strategy that increases one measured outcome may still produce unnecessary escalations or a poor experience that the metric fails to capture.

Customer data can also suggest proactive outreach, such as following up when an unresolved issue indicates a risk of departure. A predicted opportunity is only a reason to consider an intervention. Contact preferences, frequency, and relevance determine whether it is welcome. Continuity is valuable when the agent remembers what matters and uses that knowledge appropriately.

### Voice makes timing observable

A modular voice pipeline transcribes incoming speech, reasons over the transcript, and converts the response back into audio. Separating speech-to-text (STT), reasoning, and text-to-speech (TTS) permits independent measurement and control. The preference reflects the talk's context, rather than a permanent limitation of audio-to-audio models.

Response latency begins at the customer's actual end of speech and ends at audible playback. Measuring processed chunks or server output can hide turn-detection and delivery delays. Silence is only evidence of a possible turn boundary: a pause may be mid-sentence, and “uh-huh” may be either acknowledgment or a substantive answer. A practical detector combines voice activity with dialogue context and a confirmation window. Ignoring every acknowledgment would lose answers to the agent's questions.

It describes preparing responses speculatively around possible turn endings, then selecting the response appropriate to the confirmed utterance. This spends extra computation to reduce the wait after a turn finishes. The system must still respond to meaningful interruptions and abandon a prepared answer when the customer changes the request.

Request hedging addresses another source of delay: an inference request that occasionally takes much longer than usual. Several requests can run concurrently, with the earliest acceptable answer used. If independent requests each exceed a latency threshold 10% of the time, two both exceed it only 1% of the time. Requests to the same overloaded service may instead slow down together, weakening that benefit.

The source's hedging example schedules coroutines explicitly, skips failed or unacceptable results, and cleans up outstanding local tasks. The supplied calls should perform inference without business side effects; issuing several refunds would have very different consequences from generating several candidate responses.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
import asyncio
import math

async def first_acceptable(calls, acceptable, timeout_s=2.0):
    calls = tuple(calls)  # Each callable creates an inference coroutine.
    if not calls or not math.isfinite(timeout_s) or timeout_s <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Provide calls and a finite positive timeout")

    async def attempt(call):
        try:
            return True, await call()
        except Exception:
            return False, None

    tasks = [asyncio.create_task(attempt(call)) for call in calls]
    try:
        for completed in asyncio.as_completed(tasks, timeout=timeout_s):
            succeeded, result = await completed
            if succeeded and acceptable(result):
                return result
        raise RuntimeError("No acceptable response")
    finally:
        for task in tasks:
            if not task.done():
                task.cancel()
        await asyncio.gather(*tasks, return_exceptions=True)
```

</details>

The acceptance predicate is part of the design: a nonempty string alone does not establish a correct answer. Exhausting the time budget raises a timeout instead of presenting a failed request as success. Cleanup assumes cooperative asynchronous calls; a coroutine that blocks or suppresses cancellation can delay it. Local cancellation also does not guarantee that remote computation or billing stops. [Python's asyncio reference](https://docs.python.org/3/library/asyncio-task.html#asyncio.wait) explains the underlying task and cancellation behavior.

Hedging therefore needs measurement of latency, correlation, cost, and provider behavior. Unavailable providers should be excluded before routing among acceptable alternatives. Traces across recognition, inference, tool use, synthesis, and playback reveal which stage needs improvement and which work can safely overlap. Short acknowledgments can help while work proceeds, provided they reflect actual progress.

Speech quality also depends on the evaluation target. Word error rate counts substitutions, deletions, and insertions relative to a reference transcript. That reference must reward hearing the customer rather than background television; background words entering the customer transcript count as unwanted insertions. Rare product names, places, or customer names may matter more to the task than their frequency suggests. Agreement among transcription systems does not establish correctness.

Channel adaptation preserves decisions and facts while changing presentation. Spoken service benefits from short turns, clear sequencing, and opportunities to interrupt. Numbers require semantic context: the same digits can describe a price, a year, or an identifier, and converting an identifier to an integer can lose meaningful leading zeros. Locale and business convention affect pronunciation. The talk's “voice sommelier” similarly makes vocal identity a deliberate choice, using attributes such as enunciation, breathiness, and pitch movement. Listener evaluation determines whether those choices suit the interaction.

### Evaluate actions and repeated success

In an early simulation, two agents kept thanking one another after resolving the business question. The missing terminal policy turned a successful exchange into an endless gratitude loop. A practical agent needs both a task-completion condition and a resource limit, with defined behavior when either is reached.

[Tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045) evaluates tool-agent-user interactions against annotated business-state outcomes. State checks establish whether an action happened; separate checks assess explanation, policy adherence, and escalation. The evaluator should compare the task-relevant result rather than require equality across unrelated database fields.

The user can change the environment too. A support agent may ask a customer to restart a device or toggle a setting, then reason about the result. [Tau-squared-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07982) models this shared control in a telecom setting, coupling the simulated user to tools and environment state. Evaluation must consider both the agent's own actions and how effectively it guides the user.

This small evaluation loop adapts the source's stateful example. Callbacks supply the simulated agent and tools; user turns include trusted simulator updates. The latest message reaches the agent before it responds, tool results become visible in the history, and the final score comes from environment state.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
from copy import deepcopy
from itertools import islice

def evaluate_episode(agent_step, tool_step, user_turns,
                     initial_state, goal, max_turns=8):
    if max_turns < 1:
        raise ValueError("max_turns must be positive")
    state = deepcopy(initial_state)
    history = []
    for message, user_update in islice(user_turns, max_turns):
        state.update(deepcopy(user_update))  # Trusted simulation fixture.
        history.append(("user", message))
        reply, commands = agent_step(
            deepcopy(history), deepcopy(state)
        )
        history.append(("assistant", reply))
        for command in commands:
            result = tool_step(command, state)
            history.append(("tool", deepcopy(result)))
        if goal(state):
            return True, history
    return bool(goal(state)), history
```

</details>

An agent that says “the return is complete” without requesting the relevant operation will fail a goal check requiring a recorded return. An agent that performs the operation can pass even when unrelated state differs. Copies prevent an agent callback from changing the evaluator's state directly. This illustrative loop limits the number of user turns; real adapters must also enforce authorization, tool-call limits, and execution deadlines. A separate evaluator must assess the conversation. Tool errors propagate here so the harness can record them as execution failures.

Realistic tests vary customer personas, interruptions, accents, background audio, and API behavior. Simulation remains an approximation, so production observations should continually contribute cases. Repeated evaluation then measures consistency. For a fixed task with independent runs and identical success probability $p$, the probability that all $k$ runs succeed is:

$$
\Pr[\text{all } k \text{ runs succeed}] = p^k
$$

For twenty runs, a 95% individual success rate gives about a 35.8% chance that every run succeeds; at 99%, that chance rises to about 81.8%. These are probabilities of complete consistency, not individual failure rates. Different task difficulties and correlated runs require more careful aggregation; raising an aggregate benchmark score to a power does not generally produce the correct result.

[Tau-bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12045v1) calls this metric $\mathrm{pass}^k$. Conventional pass@k measures whether at least one run succeeds. The distinction also matters commercially: payment for an entire successful sequence differs from payment for each separately resolved interaction. The revenue model should reflect the actual contract.

### Enforce access at the tool boundary

Customer-facing agents combine conventional software vulnerabilities with attacks on instruction interpretation. Input checks detect suspicious requests, supervisors monitor behavior, output checks inspect disclosures, and system controls enforce authorization. An API key alone does not scope an action to the correct customer. Integrations must independently validate the user, resource, and requested operation.

These controls can complement one another, but multiplying their failure probabilities requires independence and an attack that must bypass every layer. Defenses can share blind spots, and some harmful actions occur before output checks become relevant. Deterministic pattern matching misses unmatched attacks; preprocessing cannot handle every encoding or language; an LLM supervisor also interprets adversarial text.

Output scanning can miss paraphrases and partial disclosures while flagging legitimate replies. Terminating a session cannot undo a completed unauthorized action. A deployment must therefore measure both attacks caught and ordinary service disrupted, while placing enforceable checks before tool execution. Section 4 develops this boundary in greater detail.

### Close the loop with operational evidence

Conversation analysis can reveal confusing products, missing procedures, and recurring escalations. Retrieval and aggregation let teams investigate questions across a period's conversations without placing every transcript in one context. Recognition errors and unrepresentative cases require checking apparent patterns against outcomes. Traces help distinguish a knowledge gap from a tool failure or a misunderstanding of the customer's request.

Escalations offer reviewed examples of what the agent should learn. Pairing customer questions with expert answers can improve shared knowledge, though this is knowledge maintenance rather than necessarily model retraining. Experts can make mistakes, apply one-time exceptions, or disclose customer-specific details. Updates need review and may require replacing obsolete information instead of appending another conflicting instruction.

The operational cycle is to observe, diagnose, update, evaluate, and monitor. Improving a knowledge article, revising a policy, and repairing an integration address different causes even when they produce similar conversational symptoms. That cycle leads naturally to the next question: which training interactions produce reliable improvement?

## 2. Learning Through Competition and Cooperation

Human demonstrations established useful behavior; reinforcement learning improved execution; a league of opponents deliberately exposed weaknesses. It connects this design to language agents, whose feedback and human objectives are less explicit than a game's outcome. The central question is how to organize experience so that improving one capability does not quietly erase another.

### Agents, Environments, and Structured Actions

In reinforcement learning, an agent acts on an environment, receives observations and rewards, and adjusts its policy to improve expected cumulative reward. Discounting can give nearer rewards more weight. Partial observability requires history or memory: an opponent's hidden army still matters even when it is absent from the current screen. The optimization objective describes what the learner seeks, without promising that a practical algorithm finds the best possible policy.

Language agents follow a similar loop. The model proposes tool calls, and the harness executes them and returns observations from the digital environment. A compiler error, browser result, or API response becomes evidence for the next decision. Instructions specify goals, while verifiers, human feedback, or learned evaluators supply training signals. An instruction alone does not explain how to measure improvement, especially when qualities such as clarity or usefulness remain implicit.

StarCraft combines resource management, scouting, real-time decisions, hidden information, and long-term planning. AlphaStar achieved Grandmaster ratings across all three races in 2019, exceeding 99.8% of ranked human players ([paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1724-z)). It places this result within a broader exchange of ideas among game-playing systems and later advances in language, scientific, and multimodal models. Games offered demanding environments in which to combine and test those ideas; this history does not imply a single line of descent.

Structured actions choose abilities, units, timing, and coordinates in a sequence, with later choices conditioned on earlier ones:

$$
p(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_K \mid o) = \prod_{k=1}^{K} p(a_k \mid a_1, \ldots, a_{k-1}, o)
$$

Here, $K$ is the number of action components, each $a_k$ is one component, and $o$ represents observations and encoded history. Choosing an ability changes which units or coordinates are relevant. Specialized prediction heads can produce these choices; a language model can instead generate a structured tool call through text. Both approaches require parsing, validation, and legal-action checks.

A textual interface makes it possible to describe a new tool without designing a separate output head for each argument. A typed head can directly encode domain structure. Neither representation supplies competent planning on its own. An agent still needs to understand prerequisites, remember earlier observations, and recognize when a syntactically valid action would be strategically inappropriate.

### Demonstrations and Strategic Conditioning

Sparse terminal rewards make discovering coordinated behavior difficult. Before a sophisticated unit contributes to victory, the agent must gather resources, construct prerequisites, produce it, and deploy it usefully. Random exploration is unlikely to discover a particular long sequence of useful actions, although many sequences may realize a useful strategy. This difficulty motivates demonstrations without proving that learning from scratch is impossible.

In the account, learning from scratch found narrow behavior such as attacking with workers. Human replays supplied a broader repertoire before reinforcement learning refined it. Behavioral cloning learns to predict expert actions from observations, giving the agent a useful starting point. For structured actions, training must account for the relevant components and the earlier choices on which they depend. This resembles language modeling at the level of learning from demonstrated sequences, without making game actions and text tokens equivalent units of information.

Demonstrations also contain different intentions. An early investment may be sensible for an air-unit strategy and wasteful for another opening. If a learner sees the same observation paired with different actions but cannot distinguish those intentions, it may blur several coherent plans together. Conditioning on a replay's strategic features helps it learn those alternatives separately.

A replay condition can identify the intended opening or composition, much as an instruction steers a language model toward a particular approach. AlphaStar's published replay statistics included build-order and cumulative information ([methods](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/research/alphastar/AlphaStar_unformatted.pdf)). Reducing this information to whether each unit or building ever appeared would discard order and counts, obscuring important differences between strategies.

Completed replays provide training labels, but deployment cannot consult future events. A label describes the strategy a demonstration followed; the policy still learns actions appropriate to observations available at each moment. The final evaluated AlphaStar was not conditioned on a specific opening ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphastar-grandmaster-level-in-starcraft-ii-using-multi-agent-reinforcement-learning/)). Strategic conditioning is therefore useful to understand as a training mechanism, with its deployment role specified separately.

### Improving While Retaining the Prior

Reinforcement learning can prematurely specialize, discarding useful behavior learned from demonstrations. A strategy that already wins supplies immediate reinforcement, while another may require additional practice before its value becomes apparent. The learner can become trapped in an increasingly narrow stream of experience. A generic regularized objective balances reward improvement against departure from a supervised reference policy:

$$
\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r_t\right] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\mathrm{SL}}\right)
$$

Here, $\pi_\theta$ is the learned policy with parameters $\theta$, and $\pi_{\mathrm{SL}}$ is the supervised reference. The first term is expected discounted reward over a horizon $T$; $r_t$ is the reward at step $t$, and $\gamma$ controls discounting. The second penalizes changes from the supervised policy, with strength $\beta$. KL divergence measures a directional difference between distributions; here it is shorthand for a policy comparison averaged over relevant observations and strategy conditions. This expression explains the idea without specifying AlphaStar's complete losses.

The reference supplies continuity, but the balance matters. Excessive pressure to imitate can obstruct improvement; weak pressure can allow useful behavior to disappear. Small average divergence can also conceal large changes in rare situations. Regularization therefore guarantees neither strategic diversity nor safety. Its value depends on the reference, the observations used to compare policies, and the quality of the new reward signal.

An actor chooses actions, while a critic estimates future returns to help train it. A critic can use information during training that the acting policy will not receive at deployment. AlphaStar used both players' observations, rather than literal omniscience, to improve its value estimates. The critic was unnecessary at evaluation. Keeping privileged information outside deployed action selection preserves the intended information boundary; this training arrangement differs from a process reward model.

This attributes an approximate 400-point Elo gain to adding KL regularization and a further roughly 150 points to conditioning. These figures illustrate the value of retaining a useful prior while learning, rather than serving as exact ablations or Battle.net ratings. The broader lesson is that demonstrations and experience can remain complementary after imitation training ends.

### Strategic Diversity and Self-Play Cycles

AlphaStar's specialization in Void Rays illustrates how familiar opponents can stop testing neglected skills. An agent may execute its preferred strategy with impressive precision while becoming vulnerable to a rarely encountered alternative. This is a distribution problem as much as an execution problem: capabilities that disappear from training can cease to receive reinforcement.

Competitive strength can also cycle. One strategy defeats a second, the second defeats a third, and the third defeats the first. A single rating can conceal those matchups even when it usefully summarizes average performance. Improving against the latest opponent does not necessarily improve performance against historical ones, and a cycle among some policies does not exclude a stronger policy elsewhere.

Rock-paper-scissors makes this concrete. Choosing each move with equal probability gives zero expected payoff against any opponent under the usual win, loss, and draw payoffs. Repeatedly adapting only to the current opponent can instead chase a moving target. This analogy does not prescribe equal StarCraft unit frequencies, where positioning, numbers, and upgrades also matter, or prove that all self-play fails. Historical opponent mixtures and averaging can help under suitable assumptions.

Strategic conditioning encourages alternatives, but different strategies may still require identical actions in some situations. Likewise, high action entropy does not establish broad strategic coverage: an agent can randomize many small decisions while relying on one overall plan. Useful diversity concerns the openings, responses, and counterstrategies an agent can actually execute and defend against.

The “spinning top” view separates overall playing strength from strategic variation. It does not guarantee a dominant pure strategy at high ratings; strong players can still exhibit cyclic matchups. Language-model specialization alone likewise establishes no competitive cycle without a defined pairwise comparison rule. Being better at mathematics and weaker at writing describes different capabilities, but does not by itself define a game between models.

### A League Turns Weaknesses Into Training

AlphaStar's league gives different agents complementary roles. Main agents optimize broad performance; main exploiters attack their weaknesses; league exploiters challenge the wider population; frozen checkpoints preserve earlier strategies. Roles differ in objectives and matchmaking, rather than necessarily in architecture. The population becomes part of the learning environment because it determines what the main agents must learn to handle.

An exploiter searches against a fixed snapshot of an opponent. Holding that target stable gives the search time to develop a counterstrategy while other training continues. When it discovers a useful weakness, a preserved checkpoint can become part of future training. An exploiter's cannon rush, for example, can repeatedly test scouting and defense. Resetting toward the imitation prior and varying conditions can broaden the search without guaranteeing novelty. Admission schedules also depend on role, training duration, and confidence in evaluation results.

A matchup matrix records estimated win rates between policies, revealing strengths that an average rating can hide. A high average win rate against a uniform pool does not mean victory over every opponent, and finite estimates carry uncertainty. A large archive is useful only when its relevant vulnerabilities return through matchmaking. Storing checkpoints and evaluating every possible pair are separate costs.

This arrangement makes progress easier to interpret. If the main agent defeats a new exploiter while retaining performance against earlier opponents, there is evidence that its repertoire has broadened. If it defeats the new challenge but loses to an old one, the latest improvement may have exchanged one vulnerability for another.

Selecting opponents near a 50% win rate can provide a useful curriculum, but that level does not universally maximize learning. Such a rule can exclude difficult opponents whose strategies expose important weaknesses. Published prioritized fictitious self-play combined hard-opponent weighting with a near-level curriculum. A working league therefore needs explicit sampling and independently frozen parameter snapshots.

Every archived opponent retains some probability of selection, while additional weight favors hard and balanced matchups. Win rates are measured from the learner's perspective.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from math import isfinite
from random import Random

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class ArchivedOpponent:
    checkpoint: str
    learner_win_rate: float

def matchmaking_weights(archive):
    if not archive:
        raise ValueError("The archive must contain an opponent")
    weights = []
    for opponent in archive:
        rate = opponent.learner_win_rate
        if not isfinite(rate) or not 0.0 <= rate <= 1.0:
            raise ValueError("Win rates must be finite and between 0 and 1")
        hard = (1.0 - rate) ** 2
        balanced = 4.0 * rate * (1.0 - rate)
        weights.append(0.1 + 0.5 * hard + 0.5 * balanced)
    return weights

def sample_opponent(archive, rng):
    weights = matchmaking_weights(archive)
    return rng.choices(archive, weights=weights, k=1)[0]

archive = (
    ArchivedOpponent("rush-v1", 0.0),
    ArchivedOpponent("balanced-v2", 0.5),
    ArchivedOpponent("old-opening-v1", 1.0),
)
weights = matchmaking_weights(archive)
assert all(weight > 0 for weight in weights)
assert weights[1] > weights[0] > weights[2]
rng = Random(7)
draws = [sample_opponent(archive, rng).checkpoint for _ in range(100)]
assert set(draws) == {opponent.checkpoint for opponent in archive}
assert sample_opponent(archive[:1], rng) == archive[0]
```

</details>

The tuple and frozen records keep this example's archive metadata unchanged. A real training system must separately preserve the model parameters identified by each checkpoint; freezing a record does not freeze a neural network. Updated estimates can produce new records. Match counts and uncertainty also matter: an estimated win rate from a few games should not be treated as equally reliable as one supported by extensive evaluation.

### Cooperative Tasks and Adversarial Evaluation

Language agents usually cooperate with users, while testers seek weaknesses. A league-inspired design can combine rewards for legitimate task completion with penalties for failures exposed by adversarial testing. This is a conceptual extension of the league idea, rather than a documented universal training objective. Specialized testers can reveal failures that average task performance hides, while an archive preserves useful challenges after the main model changes.

The defender, reference policy, and test opponents need independent model instances or isolated snapshots so that updating one does not silently change the others. Training also needs meaningful feedback from discrete text and tool outcomes; simply attaching a score to a generated response does not provide a complete learning method. The learning algorithm must connect that feedback to updates of the policy that generated the behavior.

Effective testers must expose semantically different failures. Many differently worded prompts can exercise the same underlying behavior. Task selection can consider both difficulty and diversity, but neither a balanced success rate nor varied wording guarantees useful learning. Verifiers must track correctness, tool outcomes, and human requirements. A program can pass superficial checks while failing its actual task; an explanation can sound persuasive while omitting a crucial condition. Optimizing those proxies can produce polished failures.

A finite tester population also defines a limited search. Passing its challenges shows progress against those challenges, while broader claims require additional evidence. The productive transfer from AlphaStar is an ongoing process: discover failures, preserve meaningful tests, improve the agent, and check whether earlier capabilities survive the improvement.

### What Additional Experience Can Teach

Comparing training stages requires accounting for the cost of each kind of experience. The roughly ten billion post-training interaction steps are neither optimizer updates nor a direct compute comparison with pretraining tokens: simulation, rollouts, critics, and evaluators have different costs. Historical figures establish neither current industry allocations nor future growth.

The limiting question is what additional interaction teaches. More episodes against familiar opponents may contribute less than encounters that expose an overlooked weakness. Human priors and experience can be combined through task rewards, regularization toward a reference policy, and continued supervised learning where useful. Demonstrations provide a starting point without imposing a strict performance ceiling. Additional interaction helps when varied tasks, retained failures, and credible feedback continue to teach useful behavior. Physical environments make the requirements for that feedback especially concrete.

## 3. Learning to Act in a Changing World

A whiteboard-wiping robot must coordinate perception, base movement, arm contact, and collision avoidance. Each movement changes what it can observe: moving closer can improve contact while hiding part of the board behind its arm. The research connects this integration problem to teamwork, human feedback, simulation-assisted learning, and competitive racing. Across these settings, the representation of actions, interpretation of feedback, and surrounding control system shape what an agent can learn.

### Closing the Perception–Action Loop

Perception estimates the situation; cognition chooses an intent; action execution converts that intent into commands. Cameras, lidar, inertial sensors, and joint readings provide observations from which the agent maintains a world estimate. That estimate can include its own pose, nearby objects, teammates, and predictions about adversaries. Planning selects a goal or maneuver, while motion control translates that choice into actuator commands.

The loop must account for the consequences of its own actions. Previous commands help perception distinguish self-motion from external change: a camera image shifting after a turn does not necessarily mean that an object moved. New observations correct predictions as the environment evolves. A compact world estimate is useful, but it does not eliminate uncertainty about the underlying physical state. An agent should therefore distinguish what it observed from what it inferred or predicted.

Motor control usually needs faster updates than perception or strategic planning. A planner may still be deciding where to move while a controller maintains contact pressure. The architecture must determine which decisions can wait, when observations have become stale, and how to handle missed deadlines. Torque limits, collision avoidance, and other physical constraints must be enforced by the control system; describing them in a planner’s intent does not implement them.

Other decision-makers also affect returns. A passing decision depends on where a teammate will move, while an overtaking decision depends on an opponent’s response. Learning opponents create a changing environment; hidden intentions create partial observability even when their policies remain fixed. Memory, communication, and opponent modeling address different parts of this difficulty.

### Coordinating Under Partial Information

RoboCup’s 2050 ambition—humanoid robots defeating human World Cup champions—is a research objective. Soccer brings locomotion, ball handling, formation, and adversarial reasoning into one shared task. A cooperative team can be modeled as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process: multiple agents choose actions from limited observations while contributing to a shared objective. The model includes states, transitions, observation processes, available actions, and rewards.

General policies can use local observation histories and messages, rather than only the latest observation. Correlated observations may require a joint observation model. Treating opponents as part of the environment simplifies a cooperative model, but competing objectives require a game formulation. Ad hoc teamwork adds another challenge: agents must infer the conventions of unfamiliar partners during interaction. Sharing a goal does not mean sharing an interpretation of which agent should move first.

A vehicle requests the space it will occupy over time, and the manager grants a reservation only when it does not conflict with another vehicle’s reserved occupancy. Scheduling remains centralized while vehicles execute their trajectories. Time matters because two vehicles can use the same road area safely at different moments.

Reservations must cover whole vehicle footprints, movement between sampled positions, and uncertainty. Localization error, imperfect control, and delayed messages require conservative margins and a response when execution diverges from the schedule. A usable system also needs ownership-aware cancellation, consistent timing, an advancing planning horizon, and conflict checks that cannot be invalidated by simultaneous requests. A conflict-free schedule provides useful coordination only while actual movement remains compatible with its assumptions.

Reliable coordination also requires reliable perception. Sony AI’s consent-based Fair Human-Centric Image Benchmark ([FHIBE datasheet](https://fairnessbenchmark.ai.sony/data-card/fhibe-datasheet)) reports 10,318 images and 1,981 subjects. Comparing average model outputs across groups can reveal disparities: for binary decisions this gives a demographic-parity gap, while continuous outputs produce a score gap. Neither comparison automatically measures accuracy or establishes fairness. Labels, adequate samples, uncertainty estimates, and task-appropriate metrics remain necessary. A robot’s planning quality cannot compensate for consistently failing to detect some people it encounters.

### Learning From Human Evaluation

Trial and error becomes expensive when actions consume a person’s time or risk damaging equipment. It trains an Agent Manually via Evaluative Reinforcement—learns to predict human approval of state–action pairs. The agent then uses those predictions to select actions. A simple predictor combines state and action features with learned weights; richer representations can support more complex tasks. Its prediction concerns human evaluation, which is distinct from discounted environmental return.

Human feedback often arrives after the action it evaluates. A person teaching Tetris might approve a completed placement after the agent has already started its next movement. Temporal credit assignment distributes that feedback across plausible recent actions, accounting for reaction delay. The learning objective must match this interpretation: feedback about an aggregate of recent actions and separate feedback about each action do not imply the same update.

The following example isolates the aggregate interpretation. It averages eligible state–action feature vectors, predicts one evaluation for that average, and updates the weights using that single prediction error. The feature average must appear in both prediction and update for the step to follow the same squared-error objective.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
from math import fsum, isfinite


def credit_update(weights, history, now, feedback,
                  delay=(0.25, 0.75), rate=0.2):
    """One linear-model step for uniformly weighted delayed feedback."""
    low, high = delay
    scalars = (*weights, now, feedback, low, high, rate)
    if not weights or not all(isfinite(x) for x in scalars):
        raise ValueError("Use nonempty weights and finite inputs")
    if not 0 <= low <= high or rate <= 0:
        raise ValueError("Invalid delay window or learning rate")
    eligible = []
    for timestamp, features in history:
        if not isfinite(timestamp):
            raise ValueError("Timestamp must be finite")
        if low <= now - timestamp <= high:
            if len(features) != len(weights):
                raise ValueError("Feature dimensions must match weights")
            if not all(isfinite(x) for x in features):
                raise ValueError("Features must be finite")
            eligible.append(features)
    if not eligible:
        return list(weights), None, 0
    average = [fsum(column) / len(eligible)
               for column in zip(*eligible)]
    prediction = fsum(w * x for w, x in zip(weights, average))
    error = feedback - prediction
    updated = [w + rate * error * x
               for w, x in zip(weights, average)]
    return updated, prediction, len(eligible)


history = [(0.25, (1.0, 0.0)), (0.75, (0.0, 1.0))]
weights, prediction, count = credit_update([0.0, 0.0], history, 1.0, 1.0)
assert weights == [0.1, 0.1] and prediction == 0.0 and count == 2
```

</details>

The two eligible actions sit exactly on the delay-window boundaries. Each contributes half of the aggregate feature vector, so both weights increase by 0.1. The model’s aggregate prediction consequently rises from zero to 0.1. Updating each action independently toward the feedback would implement a different objective. If no action falls in the window, the function leaves the weights unchanged and returns no prediction.

It assumes timestamps from a consistent clock and immutable feature snapshots, as shown by the tuples. An interactive agent also needs history management, action selection, and deliberate exploration. The illustrative delay window and uniform weighting are modeling choices; real trainers have different response patterns. Reliable snapshots matter because changing an observation array after recording it must not rewrite the experience being evaluated.

A nonuniform delay model could assign greater weight to some eligible actions. The same principle would apply: construct the weighted feature aggregate, predict its evaluation, and update through that aggregate. Changing only the update weights while leaving the prediction unchanged would break the correspondence. Choosing the delay model therefore changes the interpretation of the teaching signal, rather than merely tuning how quickly the agent learns.

Trainers also differ in their judgments and attention. Deep TAMER adds neural representations, while EMPATHIC’s inferred emotional feedback adds interpretive uncertainty. These approaches provide useful teaching signals, but the agent must distinguish intended preferences from limitations in how they were expressed or inferred.

Predicted approval can be combined with environmental value estimates. The signals need compatible scales, and their relative influence can change during training. Reducing human guidance may allow more independent learning, but does not guarantee performance beyond the teacher or preserve preferences that should remain binding. A trainer’s instruction to avoid disruptive behavior need not become irrelevant when environmental rewards improve.

[Adaptive Planner Parameter Learning](https://people.cs.gmu.edu/~xiao/Research/APPL/APPL.html) instead learns parameters for an existing planner, such as speed limits or obstacle costs. The planner converts those parameters, the current state, and a navigation goal into control commands. Demonstrations, interventions, evaluation, or reinforcement can guide adaptation. A robot might adjust its navigation style in a narrow corridor while retaining the planner’s geometric reasoning. Parameter ranges must preserve the planner’s constraints; an adjustable interface does not itself guarantee valid configurations.

### Learning an Action Repertoire

A mobile manipulator has many coupled controls. Exploring arbitrary combinations of base motion, arm movement, and camera direction on hardware can waste interactions. Simulation-Pretrained Latent Action Space (**SLAC**) instead learns reusable movements in coarse simulation, then learns real-world tasks through latent commands. A decoder translates those commands into low-level movements, giving the task policy a repertoire to explore.

A wiping simulator can model geometry and contact without accurately reproducing marker removal. Real-world feedback then teaches the task policy which available movements remove the remaining marks. This shifts where the simulation gap matters: the decoder must transfer well enough to supply useful actions, while the policy adapts its choices to their observed effects. Missing behaviors remain unavailable regardless of how intelligently the policy selects among existing ones.

Disentangled Unsupervised Skill Discovery (**DUSDi**) encourages latent factors with distinct effects. A robot might benefit from distinguishing movement of its base from movement of its arm or camera, while still accounting for their physical interactions. If a camera command also unpredictably moves the arm, attributing an improvement to the correct decision becomes harder. A structured interface can make exploration and credit assignment more manageable.

Skills should produce distinguishable states while retaining action diversity within each skill. Its practical discriminator-based objective uses a lower bound on the relevant mutual information. This encourages distinguishability statistically; it does not by itself guarantee independent physical control. Distinguishable behaviors are also not necessarily sufficient for every downstream task.

In [SLAC’s experiments](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04147v1), the learned interface uses multi-discrete actions. Safety must be handled separately from whether skills are useful or distinct. Penalties for collisions or joint-limit violations can guide learning, but finite penalties cannot guarantee safety. Hardware constraints include velocity, torque, and force; bounded command outputs do not automatically bound resulting positions, contact forces, or collisions. Demonstration-free learning still depends on human-selected features, rewards, simulation assumptions, and operating limits.

### Exploiting Reward Structure

A task can combine several objectives: reach a useful position, keep an object visible, and manipulate it successfully. Learning can exploit knowledge of which action factors influence each reward component. Separate estimates can focus learning attention on relevant relationships instead of treating every action component as equally relevant to every outcome.

Conditional mutual information can test whether an action factor predicts a reward component after accounting for the state and other action factors. Such dependence is not automatically causal. If a reward is determined entirely by the current state, conditioning on that state leaves no additional information for the action to explain. Studying action consequences requires subsequent or otherwise action-dependent rewards, or suitable transition features. Data coverage and the assumptions behind the comparison matter as much as the statistic.

Additive rewards support additive returns under a fixed policy, motivating separate value estimates for different reward components. Restricting each estimate to selected action factors requires stronger justification because effects can be delayed. Moving the base can change future camera visibility even when a simplified immediate reward mapping treats those behaviors separately. An apparently irrelevant action can therefore matter through the states it makes reachable later.

SLAC assumes an action–reward mapping; it does not establish automatic discovery of those relationships. Factorized critics use the supplied mapping to focus each value estimate on relevant actions, but the mapping still needs validation. Training requires consistent reward and entropy conventions, correct handling of terminal transitions, and care when action subsets overlap so that exploration incentives are not counted repeatedly. Reported sub-hour real-world learning excludes simulation and setup, and some tasks have nonzero safety violations. Those qualifications affect how the result applies to new hardware and tasks.

### Racing: Control, Competition, and Norms

[Gran Turismo Sophy](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~pstone/Papers/bib2html-links/nature22.pdf) used structured game-state inputs and continuous acceleration/braking and steering in Gran Turismo Sport. Its 10 Hz interaction rate means 100-millisecond cycles, not a 10-millisecond inference deadline. A simplified friction circle captures how lateral and longitudinal forces share the tire’s available grip:

$$
\sqrt{F_{\text{lateral}}^2 + F_{\text{longitudinal}}^2} \le \mu F_{\text{normal}}
$$

Here, $\mu$ is the friction coefficient and $F_{\text{normal}}$ is the normal load; together they determine the simplified force limit. Braking and cornering therefore compete for the same physical resource. Competitive driving adds tactical choices: an agent may sacrifice its immediate racing line for a better corner exit or position itself to benefit from slipstreaming. Those choices depend on opponents as well as track geometry.

GT Sophy used Quantile Regression Soft Actor-Critic (QR-SAC), whose critic predicts multiple quantiles of possible returns instead of only their mean. Quantile regression uses asymmetric prediction errors to learn different parts of that return distribution, with a smooth Huber penalty limiting sensitivity to large errors. Multi-step targets propagate reward information across several transitions, helping connect a later outcome to an earlier decision. They require explicit treatment of entropy, off-policy experience, and termination; longer backups also change variance and off-policy error.

Predicting a return distribution does not itself make a policy risk-averse. The action-selection objective determines how the distribution influences decisions. Two maneuvers could have similar average returns while differing in the frequency and severity of failures. Distributional information can expose that difference, but merely estimating rare bad outcomes does not ensure that the agent avoids them.

Training scenarios expose tactical encounters that solo driving misses. Experience can come from solo laps, slipstreaming, battles through corners, dense traffic, and grid starts. Their proportions determine which situations the learner practices. Sampling must preserve the intended mixture rather than silently changing it when some scenarios have less data. Sophy’s [victory against four leading drivers](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04357-7) establishes performance in the tested simulator setting, not real-road transfer.

Sportsmanship requires rewards that distinguish legitimate competition from harmful contact. Progress and overtaking create useful incentives, but contact penalties need a defensible account of responsibility and severity. Distance alone does not establish contact, a squared velocity change alone is not collision energy, and lane ownership alone cannot resolve fault without motion conventions and incident history. Conversely, excessive penalties for proximity can discourage legitimate passing.

Human evaluation remains valuable where a reward proxy misses the intended norm. Product goals also extend beyond beating experts: an enjoyable opponent must offer suitable difficulty, predictability, and responsiveness across players with different abilities. The quality of the shared experience becomes part of what successful behavior means.

## 4. Enforcing Safety at the Boundary Between Language and Action

An assistant summarizing email must understand a request embedded in that email to forward confidential documents without treating it as authorization. The sentence is relevant to the summary, but its author does not acquire control over the assistant. The agent-security work examines this distinction across models, memory, tools, and execution infrastructure: useful autonomy requires explicit limits on authority.

The difficult case is ordinary work that brings an assistant into contact with hostile material. Reading a repository, reviewing a résumé, or searching workplace documents can expose the same model that chooses tools to instructions written by someone other than its user. Security depends on the entire path from that encounter to its consequences.

### Model the Whole System and the Attacker’s Access

An agent combines an LLM with conventional software that manages state and invokes tools. The host system receives requests, builds context, routes model proposals, and mediates interactions with operating systems, websites, APIs, and data. Security depends on what each component can influence and what the surrounding software permits the model to do.

Safety concerns harmful outcomes; security concerns adversarial compromise. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability apply throughout the system. Confidentiality covers private records and credentials; integrity covers decisions, memory, and downstream state; availability covers resource exhaustion and disabled infrastructure. Read-only access can still enable disclosure through an answer, and system prompts should not store credentials. A restriction on writes does not establish a restriction on information release.

Dynamic workflows, powerful tools, runtime discovery, persistent memory, and unattended execution change exposure. Their significance depends on resource scope, isolation, and how capabilities interact. An arbitrary weighted count of features cannot establish attack probability or identify an actual vulnerability. A carefully isolated execution tool can be less dangerous than a read-only interface with unrestricted private-record access.

A threat model instead specifies attacker-controlled content, encounter timing, observable feedback, and prohibited outcomes. Attackers may influence components such as models, retrieval stores, or tools; submit direct prompts; or place content in the environment. These categories can overlap. Writable knowledge sources and dependencies may permit component influence without administrator access, so assumptions about which components remain trustworthy require justification.

For an email assistant, this means distinguishing the ability to write one incoming message from the ability to modify the contact directory or the sending service. Those are different privileges and produce different defenses. The assessment also needs a legitimate objective, such as summarizing unread messages, so that the evaluator can identify actions outside its scope. Merely producing an action the attacker wanted is insufficient if the user already authorized it.

### Prompt Injection Turns Data Into Apparent Authority

Direct injection enters through user input; indirect injection arrives through documents, webpages, emails, or repositories. System instructions, user requests, retrieval results, and environmental content share an inference process. Role labels help communicate provenance, but model interpretation is not deterministic authorization. A résumé’s instruction to approve an applicant cannot override a requirement for at least three years of PyTorch experience.

The same issue appears when a repository comment asks a coding assistant to retrieve credentials or a retrieved document recommends an unrelated sign-in page. Recognizing the instruction as suspicious may help, but the important boundary concerns its authority. Even persuasive task-related text remains environmental content unless a trustworthy mechanism establishes permission for the resulting action.

Assessment must establish the prohibited outcome and observe whether execution occurred or enforcement blocked it. Considering only the model’s most likely response also misses the variability of sampled behavior. Historical demonstrations identify possible failure paths under particular versions and configurations; they do not establish that every deployment of the named product remains vulnerable.

Leakage can occur through tool arguments, outbound requests, artifacts, or rendering effects despite a refusal in the final answer. A complete evaluation therefore follows information beyond the text shown to the user and into the consequences of tool use. Conversely, a model claiming to have sent a secret does not prove that a send occurred. Both apparent safety and apparent compromise require evidence from execution.

Persistent memory extends the attack window. [AgentPoison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.12784) optimizes triggers to retrieve poisoned demonstrations without retraining the target model. Routine success can therefore coexist with triggered failures. Retrieval relevance alone does not establish trustworthiness: corpus composition, retrieval procedures, and downstream interpretation all affect whether poisoned content changes behavior.

Memory provenance and write authority need governance alongside relevance. A useful memory record should not gain permission to direct tool use simply because it resembles the current task. Testing ordinary queries can reveal whether an attack disrupts routine performance, but success on a finite benign sample cannot establish that the poisoned material is otherwise undetectable or will never be retrieved accidentally.

### Evaluate Consequences Across the Execution

Agent assessment extends model-level work such as DecodingTrust by tracking the interaction of model behavior, prompts, memory, tools, and environment state. These are connected parts of an execution, not independent variables. A tool result can change memory, alter later decisions, or trigger effects that persist after the original exchange.

Evaluation should define concrete violations: disclosure, unauthorized tool use, loss of integrity, and loss of availability. Reaching such a violation need not end the run, and later harmless behavior does not undo an earlier failure. The probability of encountering a violation depends on the evaluation horizon, task distribution, attacker access, randomness, and attack budget. An attacker who can adapt later inputs has different opportunities from one limited to a single initial payload.

An informative trace links the encountered content, proposed action, authorization decision, tool result, and resulting state. This makes it possible to distinguish a susceptible model protected by enforcement from a system that executed an unauthorized action. Both observations matter, but they answer different questions. The first can expose dependence on the monitor; the second demonstrates a failure of the combined system.

Automated red teaming repeatedly proposes attacks, observes execution, and uses scored consequences to improve subsequent candidates. [LeakAgent](https://openreview.net/pdf?id=WIfns41MAb) trains an attacker with reinforcement learning; [AgentVigil](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-emnlp.1258/) combines Monte Carlo Tree Search seed selection with mutation; [AgentXploit](https://billchan226.github.io/) separates source analysis from exploitation.

These methods require clear access assumptions. White-box access can concern agent source and internal execution rather than model weights, and training an attacker does not imply white-box access to its victim.

Scoring must match the violation being measured. Visiting a URL or downloading a file does not by itself establish unauthorized execution, disclosure, or harm. For extraction tasks, semantic similarity can indicate related content without establishing exact secret recovery; an evaluator’s access to reference secrets should also be distinguished from the attacker’s access. Detailed feedback can guide search without making the victim differentiable or proving that simpler success signals are inadequate.

Controlled episodes need isolation and authoritative traces that distinguish blocked proposals from completed actions. They also need well-defined starting state: a previous episode’s memory writes or files can change the next episode’s outcome. Repeated trials cannot establish attack absence. Reports should identify versions, tasks, attacker access, budgets, and legitimate-task performance so that improved resistance can be assessed alongside the agent’s ability to do useful work.

### Preserve the Agent Being Assessed

Replacing a production agent’s planner, memory, or tool routing to fit a benchmark changes the evaluated system. Agentified Agent Assessment (AAA) packages assessment as an assessor agent; [AgentBeats](https://docs.agentbeats.dev/) implements this approach using Agent2Agent (A2A) for task interaction and the Model Context Protocol (MCP) for tools and context. The assessor initializes tasks, exposes relevant resources, observes behavior, and evaluates results while preserving the candidate’s own implementation where possible.

Standard interfaces can reduce the need for a separate integration between every agent and every benchmark. With sufficient interface coverage and comparable integration costs, each agent and benchmark can connect to a shared protocol rather than maintaining every possible pairwise adapter. Testing all agent–benchmark pairs still requires all those evaluations; reducing integration work does not remove the measurement workload.

The purpose is to preserve relevant production behavior, but standardization does not guarantee identical execution. Permissions, data, latency, and external services can still differ. A benchmark that grants broader tool access may reveal a real weakness in its tested configuration while misrepresenting a narrower deployment. A benchmark that removes production memory may miss an attack that depends on persistence. Fidelity should be judged by whether such differences change the trajectories and outcomes that matter.

The assessor also needs its own controls. Candidate agents must be isolated from graders, protected data, and host systems. Evaluation sessions need authentication, argument validation, budgets, and timeouts, while the execution environment needs actual isolation. A directory named “sandbox” or a method returning a success-shaped string does not supply that isolation or demonstrate that a tool ran. Use executable state checks where possible; language-model judges add their own manipulation risk.

### Enforce Authorization Outside the Model

Detection, sanitization, isolation, and output checks provide complementary defenses. Least privilege grants task-specific authority; complete mediation checks every action path; fail-safe defaults deny operations without authorization. Model output becomes a proposal that a runtime evaluates. Missing a malicious instruction during content screening need not become an incident if the proposed consequence exceeds the authority granted to the task.

[Progent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11703) applies tool policies expressed in a domain-specific language, with contextual updates. Baseline rules, authorized task context, and verified execution state determine the permitted actions. The central gate is:

$$
a_t^{\text{executed}} = \begin{cases} a_t^{\text{proposed}}, & \text{if } a_t^{\text{proposed}} \in \mathcal{P}_{\text{valid}}(t) \\ \perp \; (\text{Security Block}), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

Here, $\mathcal{P}_{\text{valid}}(t)$ is the permitted action set at time $t$, and $\perp$ means the action is blocked. The permitted set must account for actual tools, arguments, and resources. For a request to email Alice, the initial policy might allow a contact lookup while withholding permission to send. After trustworthy verification of Alice’s address, the policy can permit a message to that recipient and block a substituted attacker address.

Recipient permission is only one part of authorization. The message must remain within the authorized purpose and data scope, including its content, attachments, additional recipients, and repeated sends. Context updates must come from trusted evidence, and every execution path must honor the policy decision. Logging rejected arguments also needs care because those arguments may contain secrets.

This standard-library example adapts the source’s contextual tool check into a guarded, in-memory execution. The host supplies the permit from an already authorized task and verified contact; model proposals cannot create or update it. An exact approved body binds the purpose to actual content, while attachment identifiers select only host-held data. The demonstration records an event without sending mail.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Permit:
    recipients: tuple[str, ...]
    purpose: str
    body: str
    documents: frozenset[str]

documents = {"agenda": "Discuss the meeting schedule."}
outbox = []

def execute(name, args, permit):
    required = {"recipients", "purpose", "body", "documents"}
    if permit is None or name != "send_email" or type(args) is not dict:
        raise PermissionError("Denied")
    if set(args) != required or outbox:  # One send in this demonstration.
        raise PermissionError("Denied")
    recipients, selected = args["recipients"], args["documents"]
    if (type(recipients) is not tuple or type(selected) is not tuple
            or not all(type(x) is str for x in recipients + selected)):
        raise PermissionError("Denied")
    if (recipients != permit.recipients or args["purpose"] != permit.purpose
            or args["body"] != permit.body or len(set(selected)) != len(selected)
            or not set(selected) <= permit.documents
            or not set(selected) <= documents.keys()):
        raise PermissionError("Denied")
    outbox.append({"recipients": recipients, "body": permit.body,
                   "attachments": tuple(documents[x] for x in selected)})

# Trusted host context; never accept this permit from the model.
permit = Permit(("alice@example.org",), "meeting", "Are you free at 2pm?",
                frozenset({"agenda"}))
proposal = dict(recipients=permit.recipients, purpose="meeting",
                body=permit.body, documents=("agenda",))
for name, args, authority in [
    ("send_email", {**proposal, "recipients": ("other@example.org",)}, permit),
    ("unknown_tool", proposal, permit),
    ("send_email", proposal, None),
]:
    try:
        execute(name, args, authority)
    except PermissionError:
        pass
    else:
        raise AssertionError("Unauthorized action executed")
    assert not outbox
execute("send_email", proposal, permit)
assert len(outbox) == 1
```

</details>

Its host state and entry point must remain outside model control. Real concurrent execution also needs atomic authorization and consumption of permission, durable state, and coverage of alternate sending paths. Matching a purpose label alone would not establish that arbitrary message text served that purpose.

Deterministic enforcement guarantees only correctly implemented policies over mediated actions. Incomplete policies, misleading updates, unmodeled effects, permitted recommendation manipulation, and ordinary text-output attacks remain relevant. [Progent’s scope analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11703v1) explicitly excludes some of these attacks and identifies dependence on policy-generation quality.

Privilege separation limits the authority of components that interpret untrusted content. The [Agents Rule of Two](https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/) discourages unchecked sessions that simultaneously process untrusted input, access sensitive data, and take external actions. Restricting that combination is a useful heuristic, not a safety proof.

Multiple agents provide little separation if one passes untrusted instructions to a privileged executor; valid JSON can still contain unauthorized destinations. Information-flow labels can track confidentiality and integrity, but semantic influence is difficult: conservative labels can spread too broadly, while coarse labels miss dependencies. Restricted access, constrained interfaces, trustworthy authorization, and explicit release rules remain necessary.

### Vulnerability Discovery and Verified Construction

[CyberGym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02548) evaluates vulnerability reproduction across 1,507 historical vulnerabilities in 188 projects using pre-patch code, descriptions, and checks against vulnerable and patched versions. The report records Claude Sonnet 4.5 at 28.9% for one run and 66.7% across 30 trials. The wider study also reported 35 zero-day discoveries and 17 incomplete historical patches. These are experiment-specific results. Crashes, inputs, distinct bugs, CVEs, and deployed patches are different counts.

A reproducing input must be evaluated against the claimed failure. A crash signal alone does not establish its root cause, CVE identity, or exploitability; comparison with the patched version helps test whether the input reproduces the intended vulnerability. Execution needs isolation that addresses filesystem, network, memory, and process access, rather than relying only on a timeout.

The same repository navigation and reproducing-input capabilities can help maintainers repair software or help attackers investigate it. More trials increase both opportunities and costs, including inference, execution, validation, and deduplication. A higher reproduction rate does not establish exponential growth or near-zero marginal cost. Defensive value also depends on whether maintainers can turn findings into tested, deployed repairs.

Comparisons between attack and defense depend on what is counted. Vulnerabilities and failures can be correlated, one successful exploit need not imply full compromise, and avoiding a list of unpatched flaws does not establish overall security. Isolation, monitoring, patch testing, and recovery change outcomes alongside discovery rates. The claim that an attacker needs only one path is most useful when it identifies a specific protected asset and the controls that still stand between a bug and that asset.

Formal verification complements empirical testing: agents propose programs, invariants, or proofs, while checkers validate specified properties. Sound proofs establish statements within a model, but correct programs need not have discoverable proofs. The specification must make the intended obligations clear, including termination or information flow where relevant. Memory safety does not establish business authorization, and verified components still depend on specifications, configuration, and surrounding dependencies.

This division of labor makes generated evidence reviewable: an agent can search for a proof or a reproducing input, while an independent checker applies a defined acceptance condition. The remaining engineering work is to ensure that this condition captures the property users actually require and that the deployed system preserves its assumptions.

## Bringing the System Together

These examples put different pressures on the same perception–action loop. Customer service makes successful completion and continuity visible. Competitive learning reveals weaknesses that a familiar training distribution can conceal. Robotics makes timing, partial observability, and physical constraints unavoidable. Security asks whether a proposed action has legitimate authority, regardless of how convincingly the model explains it.

A practical development process begins with an outcome that can be checked. It then identifies the observations, memory, tools, and permissions required to achieve that outcome. Evaluation must preserve the relevant environment: business state for service tasks, diverse opponents for competition, realistic dynamics for control, and attacker access plus execution traces for security. Repeated trials reveal consistency; retained failures make future testing more informative.

The feedback loop also needs boundaries. Expert answers require review before becoming shared knowledge, learned rewards need scrutiny when they stand in for human preferences, and tool proposals need authorization before execution. Better models expand what an agent can attempt. Carefully designed environments, interfaces, evaluations, and controls determine which attempts become dependable results.
