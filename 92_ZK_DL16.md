# Physical AI Beyond the Demo: Priors, Resilience, Human-Centered Control, and Racing at the Limit

_The next generation of autonomous machines will not be defined by model scale alone. It will emerge from systems that combine mathematical structure, economical representations, constrained reasoning, compliant control, and disciplined testing in the physical world._

Physical AI has a deceptively simple objective: enable a machine to perceive, reason, and act in environments that do not conform to a closed set of rules. Achieving that objective is difficult because every useful robot operates at the intersection of several forms of uncertainty. Its sensors degrade, its models encounter unfamiliar conditions, its body imposes hard dynamic limits, and its actions affect people and objects in ways that cannot be undone by reloading a checkpoint. Four research directions reveal what it takes to build such systems.

First, structure can substitute for part of the data and compute burden. PDE-guided motion planning shows how mathematical priors can reduce or eliminate dependence on expensive expert demonstrations. An eikonal equation can supervise a motion planner without a library of expert trajectories. A ray representation can unify cameras with different optics. A Bézier curve can compress hundreds of waypoint variables into a small, constrained trajectory representation. In each case, the model becomes easier to train or control because the representation already expresses something true about the problem.

Second, useful autonomy depends on economical world models. Resilient autonomy shows how shared representations and diverse sensing can help a robot continue operating after its preferred assumptions fail. A robot does not need a maximally detailed reconstruction of everything it can sense. It needs the geometry, semantics, uncertainty, and temporal context required for the next class of decisions. Shared visual features, sparse voxels, reduced embeddings, local temporal windows, and long-range semantic rays all trade exhaustive reconstruction for actionable information.

Third, high-level intelligence works best when its authority is bounded. Human-centered navigation and humanoid control show why semantic intelligence must be constrained by feasible actions and fast, compliant feedback. A vision-language model can rank feasible paths, interpret a social scene, or propose a reference motion, but geometric planners and fast controllers must still enforce reachability, stability, collision avoidance, and contact behavior. This separation is especially important for humanoids: deliberative reasoning operates on the scale of seconds, while balance and compliance require feedback on the scale of milliseconds.

Fourth, resilience and safety are system properties rather than model outputs. Autonomous racing shows how adversarial testing, incremental risk, and fault tolerance expose the difference between a convincing demonstration and a dependable physical system. A backup sensor matters only if it has been prepared and tested before the primary sensor fails. A learned value function still needs safeguards near singular constraints. A fast race planner still depends on watchdogs, emergency braking, healthy actuation, and an incremental validation program. Comparative retrieval and adversarial falsification can reveal failures more efficiently, but neither replaces real-world validation or establishes an absolute safety guarantee.

Together, these directions support a common thesis: the strongest physical-AI architectures do not ask one model to solve everything. They assign each layer the work it is best equipped to perform, encode known structure wherever possible, and preserve explicit mechanisms for uncertainty, recovery, and safety.

## Part I — Physics as Supervision: Fast, Data-Efficient Robot Motion Planning with PDE Priors

Robot motion planning sits beneath almost every demanding autonomy problem. A humanoid must coordinate its full body while walking, a manipulator must react to disturbances without colliding with its surroundings, and a group of robots must reach separate goals without colliding with one another. In each case, the planner must find a valid trajectory from a start configuration $q_s$ to a goal configuration $q_g$ while respecting the geometry and constraints of the environment.

The difficulty is not simply finding a path. A useful planner must find one quickly, avoid an expensive training pipeline, and remain effective as the robot, task, and environment grow more complex. Existing approaches usually deliver only part of that combination. Classical planners require no training but can be slow at inference. Learned planners can respond quickly after deployment but often depend on costly expert demonstrations or millions of interactions. Local optimization is efficient when it works, but complicated geometry can trap it in poor local minima.

A different approach is to make the governing mathematics part of the learning process. Instead of asking a neural network to rediscover motion-planning structure from expert trajectories, a partial differential equation, or PDE, supplies that structure directly. In the framework examined here, an eikonal PDE provides a self-supervised learning signal; metric structure and Bellman consistency repair the weaknesses of the first neural formulation; and the resulting value function becomes a reusable planning primitive for navigation, manipulation, online mapping, and multi-agent coordination.

The larger thesis is that mathematical structure can substitute for a large amount of labeled data, offering a route toward three properties robot planners rarely achieve together: fast inference, efficient training, and adaptability.

### The Motion-Planning Trilemma

Motion planning is usually formulated in a robot's **configuration space**, or C-space, an abstraction associated with the foundational work of Tomas Lozano-Pérez in the 1980s. A point in C-space specifies a complete robot configuration, so a seven-degree-of-freedom arm becomes a point in a seven-dimensional space. Obstacles become invalid regions in that space. This abstraction turns collision avoidance into a geometric planning problem and allows joint limits and other kinematic constraints to be represented explicitly.

The framework motivates itself with the following qualitative taxonomy. It is an organizing rubric rather than a universal benchmark across every implementation, and the final row summarizes the performance profile reported for the PDE-prior approach:

| Method family                                               | Inference efficiency | Training efficiency |                          Adaptability |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------: | ------------------: | ------------------------------------: |
| Optimization-based                                          |                 High |                High |                 Prone to local minima |
| Sampling/classical, such as RRT and A\*                     |                 Slow |                High | Often limited as dimensionality grows |
| Data-driven, including imitation and reinforcement learning |                 High |            Very low |                                  High |
| **PDE-prior approach**                                      |    **Reported high** |   **Reported high** |                     **Reported high** |

Sampling-based methods such as RRT and RRT\* randomly explore valid configurations and connect them into a tree. Many are training-free and probabilistically complete under standard assumptions, but repeated sampling and collision checking can be too slow for high-degree-of-freedom systems. Imitation learning moves much of that cost offline: a network learns from trajectories generated by a classical planner and then produces plans rapidly at test time. Yet the demonstrations still have to be generated, stored, and learned. Reinforcement learning removes the need for an expert planner but commonly replaces it with a vast number of environment interactions.

The research path behind the framework reflects those tradeoffs. It began with adaptive sampling, moved to data-driven models, and suggested that stochastic mechanisms—from dropout to variational autoencoders and diffusion models—can help represent alternative trajectories. PDE priors address the next bottleneck: the cost of teaching such models from expert-generated plans.

This makes inference time an incomplete measure of efficiency. A planner that answers in milliseconds only after weeks of data generation and multi-GPU training has shifted the cost rather than eliminated it. The PDE-prior approach instead asks whether geometry itself can supervise the model.

Here, a **physics prior** does not mean a physics engine or a numerical simulator. It means using the structure of a physics-derived PDE as a constraint on what the neural network may learn. The network still offers a compact, fast approximation at inference time, but its training signal comes from a mathematical relationship rather than a collection of expert trajectories.

### From Optimal Control to a Learnable Eikonal Equation

The natural starting point is the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. For dynamics $\dot{x}=f(x,u)$, running cost $\ell(x,u)$, and value function $V(x)$, the HJB equation can be written as

$$
\min_{u} \left[ \ell(x, u) + \nabla_x V(x)^\top f(x, u) \right] = 0
$$

HJB expresses Bellman's principle of optimality in continuous form. Under the assumed model, boundary conditions, and regularity conditions, solving it gives a globally optimal value function and corresponding policy. Unfortunately, the equation becomes extremely difficult to solve in high-dimensional state spaces, especially when dynamics, singularities, and multiple motion modes interact.

If the dynamics are simplified to isotropic, unit-speed motion, the HJB structure reduces to the eikonal equation. In the stated derivation, $f(x,u)=u$, $\|u\|=1$, and $\ell=1/S$; minimizing over the unit direction $u$ yields the gradient-magnitude condition:

$$
\|\nabla_{q_s} T(q_s)\| = \frac{1}{S(q_s)}
$$

The unknown $T$ is a travel-time or cost-to-go function. The known function $S$ describes the local speed or constraint geometry. In free-space navigation, $S(q_s)$ can be constructed from the robot's distance to the nearest obstacle or constraint boundary. A small value of $S$ produces a steep travel-time field; a larger value produces a shallower field.

The gradient-magnitude equation alone does not determine $T$; a goal boundary condition, conceptually $T(q_g)=0$, anchors the field. With that condition, the relationship has a useful geometric interpretation. The solution $T$ is a geodesic distance through the valid part of configuration space. Its level sets behave like propagating wavefronts, and its gradient supplies a direction for moving through the field.

Numerical solvers such as Sethian's Fast Marching Method (1996) can approximate this field accurately on a grid. If a grid has $n$ points along each of $d$ dimensions, it contains $n^d$ nodes, and a typical marching cost is $O(n^d\log n^d)$. This exponential growth in the number of grid nodes makes direct numerical methods impractical beyond roughly three or four dimensions in the settings discussed here; that is a practical range, not a hard mathematical limit.

#### Learning the travel-time field without expert trajectories

A neural approximation replaces the grid with a function $T_\theta(q_s,q_g,\text{env})$. The network receives a start configuration, a goal configuration, and an encoding of the environment, then predicts a scalar travel time. There is no direct label for that scalar. Instead, automatic differentiation computes its gradient with respect to $q_s$, and the eikonal equation supplies the target relationship:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \left\| \|\nabla_{q_s} T_\theta(q_s, q_g)\| - \frac{1}{S(q_s)} \right\|^2
$$

The supervision is therefore on the network's derivative rather than its forward output. Random configurations are inexpensive to obtain, and a collision checker or geometric model can calculate their distance to an obstacle. No RRT, trajectory optimizer, or human demonstrator needs to generate complete paths.

```python
Algorithm: Eikonal PDE Training
Input:  robot DOF, obstacle geometry, N sample pairs
Output: trained network T_θ

for each iteration:
    sample q_s, q_g ~ Uniform(C-space)
    predict T = T_θ(q_s, q_g, env)
    compute grad = ∇_{q_s} T   [via autograd]
    S_approx = 1 / ||grad||
    S_true   = distance_to_obstacle(q_s, env)
    loss = (S_approx - S_true)²
    update θ via backprop
```

</details>

The displayed residual and the pseudocode have the same zero-residual condition when $S$ and the gradient norm are positive and nonzero, but they are not the same optimization objective. Their gradients, scaling, and behavior near zero differ.

After training, repeated evaluations of $T_\theta$ and its gradient produce a trajectory. The direction depends on the value function's boundary and sign convention, which becomes important in the Bellman update below.

### Why the First Neural Solver Stopped Scaling

The basic method sounds almost too simple: sample configurations, calculate obstacle distance, enforce the eikonal residual, and follow the learned gradient. In early experiments it worked in three and four dimensions, but it did not deliver the expected advantage beyond that range. Two failures explained why.

#### Multiple geodesics create a multimodal target

An obstacle can create several equally short ways to reach a goal. A path may pass on the left or the right; a symmetric configuration space may contain several equivalent motion modes. A conventional single-output network can blur or collapse those modes instead of representing the piecewise structure of the geodesic field.

It is more precise to view this as a problem of multiple valid geodesics and nonsmooth characteristics than to assume that every eikonal boundary-value problem lacks a unique solution. A viscosity solution can be unique even when multiple shortest paths reach the same point. The learning problem nevertheless remains difficult because the gradient field has to represent those competing routes coherently.

#### Random pointwise training leaves trajectories uncontrolled

The loss constrains the gradient at sampled start-goal pairs, but a robot executes a continuous sequence of nearby configurations. Nothing in the original objective guarantees that the learned field behaves well between independently sampled points. A small interpolation error can alter one step, move the robot into a part of the field the model represents poorly, and then compound over the rest of the trajectory.

A one-dimensional absolute-value function gives the intuition. A network can match the correct derivatives at isolated sample points while inserting bumps between them. Both the correct function and the bumpy function have zero loss at those samples, but only one produces a stable trajectory. In a two-dimensional maze, the same effect appears as clean gradients near the source and progressively worse artifacts farther away.

The problem therefore requires more than additional data. The network must respect the mathematical properties of the solution between samples.

### Restoring the Missing Mathematical Structure

The progression from the first eikonal network to the final framework follows three related ideas: viscosity regularization, metric-aware representation, and Bellman consistency.

#### Viscosity selects a smoother solution

Adding a Laplacian term gives the viscosity eikonal PDE:

$$
\|\nabla_{q_s} T(q_s)\| - \epsilon \, \Delta T(q_s) = \frac{1}{S(q_s)}
$$

The small coefficient $\epsilon$ controls the strength of diffusion, while
$\Delta T=\sum_i \partial^2T/\partial q_{s,i}^2$ measures second-order curvature. The vanishing-viscosity construction selects a stable generalized solution and smooths the learned field. Empirically, this improved narrow-passage planning and allowed the method to reach a six-DOF arm.

The cost is computational. Evaluating a Laplacian through a neural network requires second-order automatic differentiation. That raises the training burden and undermines one of the framework's main goals.

Dirichlet energy offers a related smoothness regularizer:

$$
E_D[T_\theta] = \int_\Omega \|\nabla_{q_s} T_\theta(q_s)\|^2 \, dq_s \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\nabla_{q_s} T_\theta(q_s^{(i)})\|^2
$$

Minimizing this energy encourages a smooth function. Its Euler-Lagrange equation imposes harmonicity under appropriate boundary conditions; it does not numerically approximate the Laplacian point by point. The method avoids explicitly forming the Hessian trace used by the viscosity term, although optimizing a loss that depends on input gradients can still require mixed derivatives with respect to the network parameters. It is cheaper in the reported implementation but may need many iterations to become effective.

#### Metric structure represents geodesic distance

A geodesic distance should satisfy the familiar metric properties:

1. Symmetry: $T(q_s, q_g) = T(q_g, q_s)$.
2. Triangle inequality: $T(q_s, q_g) \leq T(q_s, q_m) + T(q_m, q_g)$.
3. Non-negativity: $T(q_s, q_g) \geq 0$.
4. Identity: $T(q_s, q_s) = 0$.

Rather than hope that a generic multilayer perceptron learns those properties from samples, the proposed approach moves the configurations into a shared latent space and applies a max-based, $\ell^\infty$ construction. A schematic summary is

$$
T_\theta(q_s, q_g) = \left\| \max\!\left(\phi_\theta(q_s),\ \phi_\theta(q_g)\right) \right\|_\infty
$$

Here, $\phi_\theta$ is a shared encoder, the maximum acts element-wise, and the infinity norm selects the largest absolute latent component. The intended geometric effect is a piecewise-linear, polytope-like approximation of distance contours. Where an ordinary $L_2$ representation tends to round or average competing modes, the max-and-infinity-norm structure is meant to preserve distinct faces corresponding to different geodesics.

This is a schematic architecture intended to encode metric structure, not a proof that every metric axiom follows from the displayed expression. The exact construction requires additional conditions.

#### Bellman consistency controls the space between samples

Metric structure addresses the geometry of the distance function. Bellman's 1957 principle of optimality addresses how its values relate along a trajectory. If $q_s'$ is the next configuration, then the travel time at $q_s$ should equal the local step cost plus the remaining travel time:

$$
T(q_s) = T(q_s') + \frac{\epsilon_{\text{step}}}{S(q_s)}
$$

Here, $q_s' = q_s + \epsilon_{\text{step}} \cdot \frac{\nabla_{q_s} T_\theta}{\|\nabla_{q_s} T_\theta\|}$.

This is a continuous analogue of a temporal-difference update. The network supplies its own bootstrapped target $T(q_s')$, while the geometry supplies the local cost $\epsilon_{\text{step}}/S(q_s)$. Training no longer checks only isolated configurations; it also checks whether neighboring values fit together along the induced motion.

With the conventional goal condition $T(q_g)=0$ and travel time increasing away from the goal, motion toward the goal follows the **negative** normalized gradient. The displayed positive step therefore assumes the opposite sign convention or a differently oriented field. An implementation must make that choice consistently.

<details><summary>The resulting objective combines the original PDE residual with a Bellman loss:</summary>

```python
Algorithm: Combined PDE + Bellman Training
Input: robot DOF, obstacle geometry

for each iteration:
    sample q_s, q_g ~ Uniform(C-space)

    # --- Eikonal gradient-matching loss ---
    T_pred   = T_θ(q_s, q_g)
    grad     = ∇_{q_s} T_pred            # first-order autodiff
    L_eikon  = ( ||grad|| - 1/S(q_s) )²

    # --- Bellman / TD Loss ---
    u        = grad / ||grad||            # unit gradient direction
    q_next   = q_s + ε_step * u          # next state (analytically computed)
    T_next   = T_θ(q_next, q_g)          # bootstrapped value
    L_bellman = ( T_pred - (T_next + ε_step / S(q_s)) )²

    loss = L_eikon + λ * L_bellman
    update θ via backprop
```

</details>

These changes are complementary. The metric-aware architecture encodes global properties of geodesic distance. The Bellman term constrains local consistency between consecutive states. Together with gradient matching, they avoid explicitly evaluating the input-space Laplacian or Hessian trace while improving the stability of the learned field.

### What the Experiments Suggest

The experiments evaluate the framework against the same three criteria that motivated it: inference speed, training cost, and adaptability.

#### Reported 70-millisecond planning queries

On a seven-DOF planning setup, the proposed method reportedly produced a plan in about **0.07 seconds**, or 70 milliseconds. Its success rate was described as comparable to MPiNet, a data-driven neural planner trained from a large expert-generated dataset, while its planning time was lower; the reported benchmark summarizes MPiNet at roughly **0.1–0.5 seconds** per plan.

The learned value function can be used in two ways. In standard mode, the planner repeatedly evaluates $T_\theta$, extracts its gradient, and integrates the resulting direction. Model Predictive Path Integral control, or MPPI, can instead treat $T_\theta$ as a terminal cost, sampling candidate trajectories while the network evaluates their remaining cost-to-go. This removes gradient extraction from the execution loop and makes the learned field usable as a plug-in heuristic for an existing controller. It does not reduce total MPPI runtime to a single forward pass: the controller still performs many rollouts, dynamics evaluations, terminal-cost queries, and weighting operations.

The evaluation also includes Lazy PRM, but its reported time excludes roadmap construction and counts only graph-query time. That is reasonable for a pure online-query comparison; a lifecycle-cost comparison would also need to count the offline roadmap construction.

#### Training in minutes rather than weeks

The strongest empirical argument is the reduction in training cost. The reported comparison is:

| Method                                   | Data-gathering time |    Training time | Hardware                |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------: | ---------------: | ----------------------- |
| **PDE-prior, 7 DOF and 50 environments** |    About 50 minutes | About 46 minutes | One consumer GPU        |
| **MPiNet**                               |       Several weeks |   About one week | Eight NVIDIA Tesla GPUs |
| **PDE-prior, Gibson environment**        |          24 seconds |  About 9 minutes | One consumer GPU        |

A rough compute estimate is

$$
\text{Speedup} = \frac{t_{\text{train}}^{\text{MPiNet}}}{t_{\text{train}}^{\text{ours}}} \approx \frac{\text{1 week} \times 8 \text{ GPU}}{46 \text{ min} \times 1 \text{ GPU}} \approx 1{,}700 \times
$$

This is a rough normalization rather than a controlled hardware benchmark, but it conveys the claimed scale of the difference: roughly three orders of magnitude in nominal GPU-time. The GPU models and effective throughput are not normalized.

The trend within the PDE project is also notable. Earlier versions reportedly required about two days of training. Encoding more of the PDE's mathematical properties reduced that to under an hour, and a preliminary 12-DOF model under preparation reportedly trained in under five minutes. The improvement is presented not merely as an engineering speedup but as evidence that a better-structured hypothesis class is easier to train.

#### Scaling to harder configuration spaces

The demonstrations move from seven DOF to 12 and 15 DOF. The 12-DOF environment deliberately contains thin obstacles, a difficult case because the constraint field changes sharply around them. The 15-DOF experiment places a large robot in a confined room. Across those settings, the reported planning time remains low and the success rate remains high.

A two-dimensional maze supplies a diagnostic view of the learned field. The final combination of gradient matching, metric structure, and Bellman learning produces time contours that qualitatively resemble those of Fast Marching. The unregularized neural solver develops severe gradient artifacts away from the source; viscosity improves the result but leaves some inter-state inconsistency; the combined model produces the cleanest field.

The evidence is promising, but its interpretation should remain disciplined. These comparisons have not been re-run here, hardware is not normalized, and several results are described qualitatively. The central empirical conclusion is therefore that PDE supervision appears to preserve learned inference speed while dramatically reducing dependence on expert data—not that every competing planner has been definitively dominated under identical conditions.

### Extending the Same Prior Beyond Free-Space Navigation

The appeal of the formulation is that much of the machinery remains unchanged when the task changes. The network still learns a value field; what changes is the meaning of the constraint function or the way several local fields are composed.

#### Constrained manipulation

For a manipulation task, valid configurations may lie on a lower-dimensional constraint manifold $\mathcal{M}$. A gripper opening a door should remain on the handle manifold; a robot carrying a cup may need to keep it upright. Replacing obstacle distance with distance to that manifold gives the generalized eikonal equation:

$$
\|\nabla_{q_s} T(q_s)\| = \frac{1}{S_{\text{manip}}(q_s)}, \quad S_{\text{manip}}(q_s) = d(q_s, \mathcal{M})
$$

This proposal uses manifold proximity as the geometric training signal and aims to remove the need for CBiRRT demonstrations used by an imitation-learning method such as CoMPNetX. Door opening and upright cup transport are the representative examples, with high success rates and low planning times reported relative to those baselines.

The equation becomes singular when $d(q_s,\mathcal{M})=0$, because $1/S_{\text{manip}}$ diverges. At this point, “speed” and inverse local cost must not be conflated: a concrete implementation needs an explicit convention or regularization near zero. The robust takeaway is that distance to the task manifold replaces distance to an obstacle as the geometric supervision.

#### Multiple task modes and large environments

A single neural field can struggle with a very large environment or a task that switches among several constraints, in part because of neural networks' spectral bias toward low-frequency structure, as discussed by Rahaman et al. (2019). Classical PDE methods address related problems with local basis functions and domain decomposition. This framework adopts the same idea.

For sequential task modes, each $T_k$ can act as a specialist for a subdomain $\Omega_k$ or a constraint manifold. A schematic smooth composition is

$$
T_{\text{global}}(q) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(q) \cdot T_k(q)
$$

where $T_k$ solves the eikonal PDE on subdomain $\Omega_k$, and $w_k(q)$ are smooth partition-of-unity weights.

The weights satisfy $\sum_k w_k(q)=1$ and are intended to blend neighboring fields without creating discontinuities. At a shared boundary, the intended continuity condition is $T_k(q)=T_{k+1}(q)$ for $q\in\partial\Omega_k\cap\partial\Omega_{k+1}$. The available description does not detail the exact blending or stitching construction, so the weighted formula explains the continuity requirement schematically rather than specifying a complete architecture.

The reported benefits are simpler local functions, fewer parameters than a monolithic field in the evaluated setup, and selective retraining when only one region changes. Demonstrations include a campus-scale environment divided into regions and multimodal manipulation that moves among stirring, tilting, hammering, and steering constraints.

#### Online arrival-time-field mapping

Conventional mapping produces occupancy grids or signed distance fields, after which a separate planner converts that representation into a C-space trajectory. An arrival-time-field map attempts to merge the two layers. If every represented state stores a learned travel time, its gradient is already a planning signal.

For an unknown environment, the constraint function must be estimated from streaming perception:

$$
S_t(q_s) \approx \hat{S}(\text{depth}_{t}, q_s)
$$

A separate estimator can learn this mapping online. As depth frames arrive, the robot updates the local constraint estimate and then updates the travel-time network against it. A concrete implementation must define the local geometric target $S_{\text{local}}$ and keep the constraint estimator's parameters distinct from those of $T_\theta$.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
Algorithm: Online Arrival Time Field Mapping
Input: robot, environment (unknown), depth sensor

Initialize: T_θ (travel time network), S_θ_hat (constraint estimator)
while robot is exploring:
    depth_t = get_depth_frame()
    q_s     = get_robot_config()

    # Update constraint function from new observation
    S_approx = S_θ_hat(depth_t, q_s)
    loss_S   = ||S_approx - S_local||²    # local geometry consistency
    update S_θ_hat via loss_S

    # Update travel time network using updated S
    T_pred  = T_θ(q_s, q_g, S_approx)
    grad    = ∇_{q_s} T_pred
    loss_T  = (||grad|| - 1/S_approx)²
    update T_θ via loss_T

    # Execute one step
    u = grad / ||grad||
    move(ε * u)
```

</details>

The first reported system took about twice as long as standard occupancy mapping. Later improvements reduced that time by roughly 40 percent, with the latest model described as approaching sub-second updating or processing per incoming frame. An in-hand camera demonstration also constructs an arrival-time field in the six-dimensional C-space of a robot arm.

This is not yet an unequivocal speed win over occupancy mapping, but it changes what the map contains. The output is not just a record of occupied space; it is a field that can immediately guide motion.

#### Multi-agent coordination

For $N$ agents, each with a $d$-dimensional configuration, the joint state
$\mathbf{q}=(q^1,\ldots,q^N)\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}$ has dimension $Nd$. A schematic centralized or cooperative Hamilton-Jacobi-Reachability formulation is

$$
\min_{u^1,\ldots,u^N} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^N \ell_i(\mathbf{q}, u^i) + \nabla_\mathbf{q} V(\mathbf{q}) \cdot f(\mathbf{q}, u^1,\ldots,u^N) \right] = 0
$$

The exact Hamiltonian depends on whether the agents cooperate or compete; an adversarial reachability game would generally require game-specific min-max structure and boundary conditions.

The eikonal solution serves as a preconditioning prior or warm start for this harder joint problem, with the aim of coordinating multiple robots in environments with complex geometry.

This direction is also where the curse of dimensionality becomes most severe. Two seven-DOF arms already create a 14-dimensional joint configuration space. The available description does not provide enough architectural detail to determine exactly how HJR complexity is controlled at larger scales.

### Beyond Kinematics

The eikonal formulation assumes trivial dynamics, so its direct output is a kinematic plan. It does not by itself enforce velocity, acceleration, torque, contact, or singularity constraints. A kinodynamic planner must return to the full HJB equation introduced above, using a state that would typically include velocity and possibly other variables in addition to $q$.

The proposed strategy is to use the easier eikonal field $T$ as a preconditioner or warm start, then learn the dynamic correction:

$$
V^{(k+1)}(q) = \min_u \left[ \ell(q,u) + V^{(k)}(q + \epsilon f(q,u)) \right]
$$

with $V^{(0)}=T$.

A referenced NeurIPS result addresses contact-rich humanoid locomotion and manipulation, but the available discussion does not spell out the loss, architecture, or convergence behavior of that extension. The important conceptual relationship is clear: the eikonal field captures the spatial structure of obstacle avoidance, and the HJB stage adds the consequences of the robot's actual dynamics.

### Open Problems and Practical Safeguards

The framework's limitations are as informative as its reported successes.

#### The field is singular near constraints

As a configuration approaches an obstacle boundary, $S(q)\to0$ and the eikonal target $1/S(q)$ becomes unbounded. The learned gradient can oscillate, especially when a goal lies close to the constraint. This failure mode is explicitly acknowledged.

Curriculum and adaptive sampling are practical mitigations. The following conceptual schedule begins with easier, broadly distributed configurations and progressively emphasizes sharp near-obstacle regions:

$$
p_t(q_s) = (1 - \lambda_t) \cdot \mathcal{U}(\mathcal{C}) + \lambda_t \cdot p_{\text{near-obstacle}}(q_s)
$$

where $\lambda_t$ increases monotonically from 0 to 1 during training.

Sampling more densely near boundaries gives the network more capacity where the field contains sharp features. It does not remove the underlying singularity; clipping, regularization, or a differently defined speed function are possible engineering remedies rather than established parts of the reported method.

#### MPPI can reduce sensitivity to gradient errors

An approximate value field can contain local artifacts even after training. MPPI reduces dependence on a single local gradient by evaluating stochastic rollouts:

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
Algorithm: MPPI Execution with T_θ as Value Function
Input: q_s (current), q_g (goal), T_θ (trained network), K (samples), H (horizon)

for each control step:
    # Sample K trajectory rollouts
    for k in 1..K:
        trajectory_k = rollout(q_s, dynamics, H, noise_k)
        cost_k = sum(running_cost(traj_k)) + T_θ(traj_k[-1], q_g)

    # Weighted average of controls
    weights = softmax(-cost_k / temperature)
    u_opt   = sum(weights * noise_k)
    execute(u_opt)
```

</details>

The learned field then acts as a global cost-to-go estimate, while stochastic model-predictive control handles local execution and may navigate around an erroneous gradient or shallow local minimum.

#### Details that determine scalability

Three implementation details remain central to evaluating scalability:

- The exact metric architecture needs more detail than the schematic max-and- $\ell^\infty$ formula provides.
- The basis-function method must enforce continuity across subdomain boundaries without introducing singularities or local minima.
- The multi-agent HJR extension must explain how it controls the rapidly growing joint state space.

The online mapping system is also still approaching, rather than clearly exceeding, the speed of conventional mapping. And the primary eikonal method remains kinematic until it is combined with a more detailed dynamic model.

These qualifications do not erase the contribution. They identify the line between a powerful organizing principle and a complete universal planner.

### Where the Research Goes Next

The immediate target is real-time training. The reported progression is two days to less than an hour and then less than five minutes; sub-second adaptation remains the stated goal. Achieving it would make the network genuinely plug-and-play: a robot could enter an unfamiliar environment, build the relevant field, and plan without a separate expert-data pipeline.

Other directions follow naturally:

- **Reactive planning under disturbance.** Fast value evaluation can support rapid replanning when an obstacle or task changes.
- **Unified grasp and motion planning.** Rather than select a grasp and then plan a trajectory in separate stages, a multimodal field could represent the coupled problem.
- **Assistive manipulation.** A biomechanical feasibility manifold could constrain robots that interact directly with a human body, although learning an accurate distance to that manifold is itself a difficult research problem.

The C-space perspective remains central. Workspace methods can be computationally attractive, but a Cartesian end-effector path may not lift to a valid joint-space trajectory. The PDE approach deliberately learns in configuration space so that joint-space feasibility constraints can be represented explicitly rather than applied only as a correction afterward.

Mathematical structure can make planning faster and less dependent on labeled trajectories, but a planner is only as dependable as the perception feeding it. The next challenge is therefore broader than path generation: the robot must preserve useful geometric and semantic understanding when sensors, lighting, communication, or compute resources degrade.

## Part II — Resilient Autonomy: Designing Robot Perception and Planning for Failure

The hardest environments do not let a robot assume that its best sensor will keep working, its radio link will remain available, or its carefully tuned model will encounter familiar conditions. Underground tunnels remove GPS and radio connectivity. Dust degrades LiDAR and cameras. Darkness severely degrades ordinary RGB cues. Narrow passages expose small localization errors, while limited onboard power rules out an unlimited collection of specialist neural networks.

The DARPA Subterranean Challenge made those constraints concrete. Heterogeneous teams of drones, legged robots, and wheeled vehicles had to explore mines, caves, tunnels, and underground facilities with intermittent communication and little opportunity for human intervention. Success required more than peak performance on a benchmark. It required a system that could continue operating when its assumptions failed.

That experience motivates a different organizing principle for autonomy: optimize for **resilience**. This research program combines unified visual geometry, thermal perception, learned inertial sensing, spherical vision, dense correspondence, uncertainty-aware odometry, semantic exploration, and task-conditioned planning. Its individual methods are diverse, but they share one systems goal—extract more uses from each representation, retain fallbacks across sensing modalities, and fit the resulting intelligence within the compute and memory budget of a field robot.

### Resilience Changes the Design Objective

Robotics often treats accuracy, speed, or task success under nominal conditions as the main objective. A field system has to answer a harder question: what happens after lighting changes, geometric features disappear, a sensor drifts, or communication drops?

The framework organizes resilience around three capabilities:

- **Robustness** is the ability to withstand anticipated disturbances without losing function.
- **Redundancy** provides an alternative when a primary component fails.
- **Resourcefulness** lets the system adapt what remains—for example, refining an IMU model while LiDAR works so that inertial odometry becomes a better fallback when LiDAR later degrades.

This definition is qualitative rather than a measurable law, and the three properties overlap. It nevertheless changes architectural decisions. A high-performing LiDAR SLAM system may be brittle in a featureless corridor or heavy dust. Adding more sensors creates redundancy but increases weight, calibration burden, power consumption, and software complexity. A resilient design therefore has to balance peak performance against graceful degradation.

The requirement for **onboard computation** sharpens that tradeoff. A robot operating underground cannot assume cloud inference or a reliable ground station. It must estimate geometry, localize, build maps, choose goals, and avoid obstacles within its own power and memory envelope.

#### From a modular stack to a shared visual front end

Traditional field systems separate SLAM, mapping, semantic perception, and planning into modules, often with different representations. That design can work extremely well, as the SubT stack demonstrated, but it also repeats computation and creates many interfaces that can fail.

The alternative vision is a single “V1 for robotics”: process each pixel once through a shared encoder, then reuse the representation for depth, structure from motion, localization, segmentation, matching, scene flow, and planning. This does not mean that every task becomes one opaque end-to-end policy. It means that expensive perceptual features are shared while task-specific outputs and planners remain explicit where useful.

Unification also creates a tension with resilience. A shared encoder saves compute but can become a common point of failure, while a single-camera platform reduces weight and component count at the cost of hardware redundancy. The “single V1” is therefore an architectural ambition, not evidence that sensor diversity is no longer necessary.

The projects discussed below—MapAnything, AnyThermal, the learned IMU model, UFM, MAC-VO, and RayFronts—are steps toward that ambition, not one already deployed universal network.

### MapAnything: One Front End for Visual Geometry

MapAnything is the clearest expression of this unified-perception goal. It takes one or more video frames, optionally accepts calibration rays, camera poses, or sparse depth, and produces a factorized geometric output from a shared DINOv2 Vision Transformer encoder and prediction head. The 4D extension adds a separate scene-flow head.

```

         ↓

         ↓

    └──────────────────────────────────────┘
```

</details>

For frames $\{I_t\}_{t=1}^T$, the factorized head predicts the per-pixel 3D points, a global scale estimate, ray directions, and camera poses shown in the diagram above. The 4D extension adds scene flow.

The central efficiency idea is not merely multi-task learning. Earlier geometric foundation models such as DUSt3R and VGGT are described as using several prediction heads with redundant computation or inflexible input assumptions. MapAnything instead shares the encoder and factorizes a single output head so that common visual work is performed once.

#### Rays as a common camera interface

An intrinsic matrix is a convenient camera model for a pinhole lens, but robots use fisheye, omnidirectional, stereo, calibrated, and sometimes poorly documented cameras. MapAnything represents the projection at each pixel with a unit ray direction.

For a calibrated pinhole camera,

$$
\hat{r}_i = \frac{K^{-1} [u_i, v_i, 1]^\top}{\|K^{-1} [u_i, v_i, 1]^\top\|}
$$

For a fisheye or unknown camera, the learned alternative is written as

$$
\hat{r}_i = g_\theta([u_i, v_i]^\top,\ I)
$$

The ray answers a simple question: in which 3D direction does this pixel look? Known rays condition the model on an existing calibration. Predicted rays let the model estimate an unknown projection from the image. The same representation can therefore serve very different camera geometries without changing the rest of the network. With images alone, the model performs learned self-calibration and reconstruction; calibrated rays support depth and pose estimation with a known projection; precomputed poses condition geometry estimation; and sparse depth supports dense completion.

Monocular geometry remains scale-ambiguous, which is why the factorized output includes $\hat{s}$. In practice, metric scale still needs a cue or prior, such as a known stereo baseline, sparse range, inertial or known motion, a known object size, or a learned metric prior.

#### Extending geometry into time

Static reconstruction is insufficient around pedestrians, vehicles, and other robots. The 4D extension adds scene flow, defined here as the per-point 3D displacement between frames:

$$
\mathbf{v}_{i,t} = P_{i,t+1} - P_{i,t} \in \mathbb{R}^3
$$

The extended model is summarized as

$$
\hat{P}_{i,t},\ \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{i,t}\ =\ f_\theta^{4D}(I_1, \ldots, I_T,\ \text{Doppler}_t)
$$

A Doppler radar branch contributes direct radial-velocity information, complementing vision's geometric correspondence. The displacement equation assumes that $P_{i,t}$ and $P_{i,t+1}$ refer to the same surface point; establishing that correspondence is part of the actual scene-flow problem.

<details><summary>The complete forward pass is represented schematically as:</summary>

```python

Input:  frames [I_1, ..., I_T], optional [rays | poses | depth]

   # Shared across ALL tasks — compute once

              else: self-supervised mode

4. (4D extension) scene_flow[i,t] = flow_head.decode(
       features[i,t], doppler_input[t]
   )

Output: point_cloud, scale_s, rays, (optionally scene_flow)
```

Reported operation is approximately 15–16 Hz for two images on moderate hardware. That is usable for some robots but remains below a typical 30 Hz camera stream, particularly for fast aerial platforms. Training uses primarily synthetic data with some real observations, gaining precise geometry and poses at the cost of a sim-to-real gap. A preliminary acceleration effort reports a 7–10× gain by skipping “non-useful” image regions, although the selection mechanism and benchmark conditions are not specified.

The reported odometry drift is lower than that of a ZED-X stereo visual-inertial system in the demonstrated setting, but the available evidence provides no full benchmark protocol or uncertainty intervals. Precise synthetic geometry is a major training advantage, yet it also contributes to a pose-conditioning mismatch: when poses are supplied, the model assumes they are accurate. Coarse GPS estimates or uncertain alignment can therefore degrade performance. Noise augmentation or explicit pose-uncertainty modeling remains open.

### Resilience Through Sensor Diversity

A unified RGB encoder reduces duplication, but RGB remains vulnerable to darkness, smoke, glare, and texture loss. Resilience therefore also requires representations that transfer across thermal cameras, inertial sensors, and wide-field-of-view optics.

#### AnyThermal aligns modalities instead of rebuilding tasks

Long-wave infrared cameras operate without visible illumination and can remain informative in darkness and some smoke or dust conditions. Their images, however, have very different statistics from RGB: no color, different contrast, lower spatial resolution, and camera-specific noise.

AnyThermal treats this as a representation-alignment problem. Across a dataset of $N$ synchronized observations, a thermal student backbone learns to reproduce the DINOv2 features generated from the corresponding RGB views:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left\| F_{\text{AnyThermal}}(I^{(i)}_{\text{thermal}}) - F_{\text{DINOv2}}(I^{(i)}_{\text{RGB}}) \right\|_2^2
$$

If alignment succeeds, downstream models built for DINOv2 features can accept AnyThermal features without being retrained task by task. This is cross-modal distillation: preserve the feature space and change the sensor that produces it.

The TartanRGBT dataset supplies thermal and RGB-D observations from a custom open-source sensor payload. Hardware triggers align capture time, while careful spatial calibration is also required before paired pixels can describe the same scene. Style transfer can augment training, but real paired data remains important for different seasons, times of day, thermal contrasts, and manufacturer-specific sensor noise. The reported results are still data-limited; performance had not saturated as more thermal data was added.

The strategy is not automatically universal. Thermal and RGB share a dense 2D spatial structure, which makes pixel alignment possible. LiDAR and radar have substantially different sampling and signal statistics, so a direct feature-matching recipe may not transfer cleanly.

#### Learning an IMU before it becomes the fallback

An IMU consumes little power and does not depend on external radio signals or visible features. Its weakness is drift: small accelerometer and gyroscope biases accumulate rapidly under integration.

The proposed fallback strategy learns the bias model online while a stronger localization source is available. During normal operation, LiDAR SLAM supplies pose corrections that refine the inertial model. If LiDAR later degenerates, the system switches to the improved IMU model for short-term continuation.

The schematic integration equations are

$$
\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \mathbf{v}_t + R_t(\mathbf{a}_t - \mathbf{b}^a_t - \mathbf{n}^a_t) \Delta t
$$

$$
\mathbf{p}_{t+1} = \mathbf{p}_t + \mathbf{v}_t \Delta t + \frac{1}{2} R_t(\mathbf{a}_t - \mathbf{b}^a_t) \Delta t^2
$$

with the learned bias estimate

```math
\hat{\mathbf{b}}^a_t = h_\phi(\mathbf{a}_{t-K:t},\ \boldsymbol{\omega}_{t-K:t},\ \text{LiDAR\_corrections}_{t-K:t})
```

These equations express the role of bias learning but are schematic. A complete inertial model would normally include gravity, gyroscope bias and noise, orientation propagation, and covariance evolution.

A reported 40-minute nighttime campus run crossed stairs, featureless hallways, and open fields with small endpoint error and consistent map alignment, without loop closure. No exact error value or comparison protocol is included, so the result is evidence of feasibility rather than a complete quantitative validation.

The phrase “an IMU cannot be jammed” captures the resilience motivation but should not be interpreted as invulnerability. An IMU does not rely on an external RF signal, yet it can still suffer saturation, vibration, thermal drift, hardware failure, or deliberate physical interference.

#### Moving convolution from the image plane to the sphere

Fisheye and omnidirectional cameras give a drone much wider awareness than a narrow pinhole lens, but their distortion makes visual patterns vary across image position and undermines the translation equivariance of ordinary planar convolution. The proposed remedy maps every pixel to its ray on the unit sphere, resamples those rays on a more uniform spherical grid, and performs convolution with geodesic rather than image-plane distance.

For a signal $f:S^2\to\mathbb{R}^d$ and a distance-only kernel,

$$
(\mathcal{K} * f)(\hat{r}) = \int_{S^2} k\!\left(\cos^{-1}(\hat{r} \cdot \hat{r}')\right) f(\hat{r}')\, d\mu(\hat{r}')
$$

The angular distance $\cos^{-1}(\hat r\cdot\hat r')$ replaces the translation difference used by flat convolution. A kernel that depends only on this distance is designed for rotation-equivariant behavior. A kernel that also depends on angle or orientation can be more expressive but gives up that symmetry.

Uniform sampling is essential. An equirectangular grid overrepresents the poles, so icosahedral or HEALPix-like sampling can distribute support more evenly. The choice is task-dependent: a navigation model may deliberately preserve gravity or “up,” even though that breaks full rotation equivariance.

The continuous integral describes ideal isotropic filtering. Discretization, finite sampling, interpolation, and imperfect calibration can weaken exact equivariance in an implemented network.

### Correspondence as the Common Currency of Robot Vision

Depth estimation, structure from motion, optical flow, stereo, and visual odometry all depend on correspondence: determining which observations in two images refer to the same point in the world. UFM, or Unified Flow and Matching, treats small-baseline optical flow and wide-baseline feature matching as the same dense prediction problem at different viewpoint scales.

For image domains $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$, a correspondence field satisfies

$$
I_1(x) \approx I_2(x + \mathbf{u}(x)) \quad \forall x \in \Omega_1,\ m(x) = 1
$$

The vector $\mathbf u(x)$ maps a pixel in $I_1$ to its corresponding location in $I_2$. The intensity relation is a photometric-constancy approximation, not the general definition of correspondence, and can fail under lighting, viewpoint, material, or modality changes. The visibility mask $m(x)$ says whether such a match actually exists. A point may be occluded, outside the second camera's field of view, or behind the camera; forcing a model to invent a match in those cases injects bad supervision.

UFM therefore trains correspondence only on visible pixels while separately predicting visibility:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{UFM}} = \frac{1}{|\{x : m(x) = 1\}|} \sum_{x:\, m(x)=1} \| \hat{\mathbf{u}}(x) - \mathbf{u}^*(x) \|_1 + \lambda_m \cdot \text{BCE}(\hat{m}(x), m^*(x))
```

The architecture uses DINOv2 features, cross-image attention, a dense flow head, and a visibility head. Synthetic geometry can provide the ground-truth correspondence and visibility labels. The reported work emphasizes visibility supervision as a key ingredient because it creates an explicit “do not care” region wherever correspondence is undefined. The displayed loss is schematic: the binary-cross-entropy term still contains a free $x$, so an implementation must specify how that term is aggregated and how an empty visible set is handled.

Demonstrations include dense registration across large viewpoint changes and pixel-level matching between an ego camera and a fixed external camera.

#### MAC-VO carries uncertainty into pose estimation

MAC-VO uses UFM correspondence and uncertainty estimates inside stereo visual odometry. At a high level, its pose objective is

$$
\hat{T}_{\text{cam}} = \arg\min_{T} \sum_{i=1}^N m_i \cdot w_i(\Sigma_i) \cdot \left\| \pi(\hat{P}_i, T) - \hat{x}'_i \right\|^2
$$

Visibility removes invalid matches, while the uncertainty function $w_i(\Sigma_i)$ reduces the influence of unreliable ones. This communicates the intended principle—propagate match visibility and uncertainty into geometric estimation—without fixing a particular probabilistic weighting. The pseudocode below uses `exp(-sigma²)`; a complete implementation must define the residual, covariance parameterization, and weighting explicitly.

<details><summary>The combined pipeline is:</summary>

### From Unified Perception to Task-Conditioned Planning

Shared features become useful to autonomy only when a planner can turn them into decisions. RayFronts, aerial manipulation, and self-supervised off-road cost learning illustrate three different paths from representation to action.

#### RayFronts combines metric maps with long-range semantic direction

A dense 3D voxel map is precise and queryable, but it ends at the range of the depth sensor. A vision-language model can recognize or reason about a distant target but does not by itself provide a collision-free metric plan. RayFronts combines the two:

$$
\text{RayFronts} = \underbrace{\text{SemanticVoxelMap}(\mathbf{x}, \text{label})}_{\text{metric, short-range}} + \underbrace{\{(\mathbf{x}_f,\ \hat{r}_f^{(\text{target})}) \mid \mathbf{x}_f \in \text{Frontiers}\}}_{\text{directional, long-range}}
$$

The semantic voxel map supports local geometry, collision checking, and navigation. At frontiers—the boundary between known and unknown space—the system stores rays toward semantically relevant objects visible beyond metric range.

For class $c$, the stored direction is

$$
\hat{r}_f^{(c)} = \frac{\bar{\mathbf{d}}^{(c)}_t}{\|\bar{\mathbf{d}}^{(c)}_t\|}, \quad \bar{\mathbf{d}}^{(c)}_t = \frac{1}{|S^{(c)}_t|} \sum_{i \in S^{(c)}_t} \hat{r}_{i,t}
$$

The system averages the camera rays of pixels assigned to the class and normalizes the result. It stores direction, not range. Navigation then uses a three-tier policy:

1. If the target has a 3D position in the voxel map, plan toward it using the metric map.
2. If only a frontier ray is available, move toward that direction and remap.
3. If neither is available, use a large vision-language model's suggestion as an exploration heuristic.

A water tower visible beyond LiDAR range is the motivating example: the robot cannot yet place it in the metric map, but it can remember which frontier and viewing direction lead toward it. The mean-ray expression is an economical illustration rather than a complete multi-object data association method. Disconnected instances can average to a direction that points at neither object, and deployed rays must also be transformed from camera coordinates into the map frame. A real implementation must additionally define frontier-to-target association and maintain that state across observations.

This is deliberately an economic representation. It avoids reconstructing distant objects metrically when a direction is sufficient for the next planning decision. OpenVDB provides sparse voxel storage, while dimensionality-reduced features trade semantic fidelity for memory. Open-text queries also require a language-aligned model such as CLIP or another compatible encoder; DINOv2 features alone do not supply arbitrary text alignment.

#### Aerial manipulation combines a learned prior with optimization

A fully actuated hexacopter with an articulated arm has to manipulate while its base is continuously moving. Its end-effector-centric whole-body controller accepts a desired 3D end-effector position and moves the aerial base while maintaining stability. The reported work explores both actor-critic learning from teleoperation demonstrations and a diffusion policy whose proposals are refined or selected by model predictive control.

The intended tradeoff is clear: a diffusion model represents multimodal candidate actions, while MPC evaluates or refines them under modeled dynamics and constraints. The precise implementation-level scoring and regularization objective is not specified in the available description.

#### Off-road driving learns cost from experience

Manual traversability labels are expensive and robot-dependent. A rock that is harmless to a large vehicle may stop a small one. Instead, the system stores DINOv2 features in a 3D voxel map and learns cost from the robot's own physical outcomes, including jolts, slippage, heading deviation, or recovery behavior:

$$
c(\mathbf{f}_v) = h_\psi(\mathbf{f}_v), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{trav}} = \sum_v \left( h_\psi(\mathbf{f}_v) - y_v \right)^2
$$

The feature vector $\mathbf f_v$ describes terrain appearance, $y_v$ is a self-supervised experience label, and $h_\psi$ predicts traversability cost. At night, AnyThermal features and stereo thermal odometry can replace their RGB counterparts while preserving the overall map-and-cost architecture.

Across the broader application portfolio, these components support nighttime triage, wildfire mapping, off-road driving, and shared-airspace operation. The Triage work brings together thermal sensing, degraded-environment localization, and semantic search, while control-barrier-function-based detect-and-avoid provides a related safety layer for shared airspace. Any formal safety guarantee remains conditional on the controller's model, state estimate, feasibility, and invariance assumptions.

### The Compute and Memory Limits Behind the Architecture

A unified model is only resilient if it can run for the duration of the mission. Two bottlenecks dominate the available discussion: quadratic attention across images and high-dimensional features stored across large voxel maps.

#### Attention limits temporal context

For $N$ images of resolution $H\times W$ tokenized with patch size $p\times p$, the dense-global-attention complexity is

$$
\text{Complexity}_{\text{attn}} = O\!\left(\left(\frac{N \cdot H \cdot W}{p^2}\right)^2\right)
$$

The total token count grows linearly with the number of images, and full self-attention squares it. This is a generic bound for dense attention; the exact cost depends on the implemented factorization. MapAnything nevertheless cannot simply attend over an unlimited mission history. The pragmatic answer is to process a bounded recent window, estimate relative motion, chain it into a global trajectory, and restart the visual context.

#### Semantic features dominate map memory

For $V$ voxels, each holding a $d$-dimensional float32 feature,

$$
\text{Memory}\ (\text{bytes}) = V \cdot d \cdot 4
$$

Raw foundation-model features can exhaust an onboard memory budget quickly. Sparse spatial indexing prevents empty space from consuming storage, but it does not remove the cost of every occupied voxel. The reported responses are dimensionality reduction and compact feature storage, with a corresponding tradeoff in semantic quality.

A bounded sliding window can control local compute, but it gives up global attention and can accumulate chaining error.

### Cross-Cutting Systems Questions

Four questions remain across the stack.

#### Explicit or implicit geometry

RayFronts uses explicit voxels because they are interpretable, directly queryable, and compatible with collision checking and frontier planning. Neural fields or other implicit representations may offer more compact alternatives, but query latency and safety validation are harder. No universal winner is identified; the right choice depends on the task and hardware budget.

#### Uncertain auxiliary inputs

Flexible conditioning is useful only if the network knows how much to trust the input. MapAnything currently handles accurate rays, depth, and poses more naturally than noisy GPS-scale priors. A resilient model needs either training-time corruption, a probabilistic conditioning interface, or explicit covariance so that a weak auxiliary sensor helps rather than silently distorting the reconstruction.

#### Modality imbalance and hardware variation

RGB pretraining uses orders of magnitude more data than thermal or radar. Feature distillation exploits that imbalance when modalities are spatially aligned, but camera-specific thermal response and radar's very different physics remain obstacles. Realistic radar simulation is itself a difficult electromagnetic modeling problem, so “generate more synthetic data” is not a complete answer.

#### Energy, usability, and institutional memory

Sensor selection is also an energy decision. LiDAR supplies range directly but adds weight and power draw. Cameras are lightweight but shift the burden into GPU computation. IMUs require very little power but provide only drifting motion information.

Field robustness also includes details that rarely appear in model diagrams: hardware buttons, interfaces that do not require typing during a mission, reproducible calibration, and software that survives student turnover. The observation that teams sometimes rediscover ideas from an earlier SubT stack is not merely anecdotal. Maintaining institutional knowledge is part of maintaining a resilient system.

## Part III — From Traversable Terrain to Gentle Humanoids: A Roadmap for Human-Centered Robot Autonomy

Outdoor delivery robots and humanoids that physically assist people appear to occupy different parts of robotics. One crosses grass, sidewalks, and traffic; the other tracks human motion, makes contact, and regulates force. Yet both face the same fundamental problem: a robot must understand which actions are physically possible, socially appropriate, and safe for the particular body and person involved.

The two research programs examined here follow that problem from mobile navigation to embodied interaction. The first replaces binary obstacle maps with morphology-dependent traversability, then gives vision-language models a constrained role in trajectory and traffic reasoning. It extends the navigation stack toward companion robots for older adults. The second turns to digital humans, compliant humanoid control, and language-conditioned 3D motion. Together they form a useful roadmap, although they have not been demonstrated as one integrated end-to-end system.

The connecting idea is **human-centered physical intelligence**. Perception must capture geometry, material properties, social context, and intent. Planning must generate alternatives without handing raw control to a language model. Control must remain stable at millisecond timescales while yielding gently to unexpected contact. Together, these layers outline a path from robots that move around people to robots that can move with them and, eventually, assist them through direct physical interaction.

### Outdoor Navigation Is More Than Obstacle Avoidance

Indoor warehouses simplify navigation with structured layouts, predictable floors, and clear free-space boundaries. Outdoor environments replace those assumptions with three continuous judgments: whether this particular robot can cross the terrain, whether its path will disturb or divide nearby people, and whether its motion respects signals, crosswalks, and right of way.

Traversability is not a property of terrain alone. Tall grass may be harmless to a heavy quadruped and immobilize a micro-rover. A curb can stop a wheeled platform while remaining routine for a legged one. Even two robots with similar geometry may value vibration, energy, mechanical wear, and route length differently.

This shifts outdoor navigation away from a binary occupancy grid ($0$ = free, $1$ = obstacle). The planner needs graded, morphology-dependent costs and several feasible local choices before it can reason about the best one.

### Generate Trajectories Before Asking a VLM to Choose

[VL-TGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.02454v6) uses a vision-language model as a high-level selector rather than a direct controller. A conditional variational autoencoder first generates candidate trajectories through learned, context-dependent affine projections. Hausdorff-distance filtering promotes separation among the retained candidates. Those trajectories are drawn on the camera view as numbered alternatives, and the VLM chooses the option that best matches the goal and visible context.

The following latent-space equations are analytical reconstructions from the source notes, not the published VL-TGS formulation. Let the latent representation be $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma) \in \mathbb{R}^D$ and let the projection matrices be $P_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times D}$, with $P_i P_j^\top = 0$ for $i \neq j$. The projected components are:

$$
z_k = P_k z \sim \mathcal{N}(P_k \mu,\ P_k \Sigma P_k^\top)
$$

Each projected sample is decoded into a trajectory:

$$
\tau_k = \text{Decoder}(z_k)
$$

The intent is to make candidate paths fan out into different local regions instead of collapsing onto nearly identical routes. Orthogonal projection matrices do not alone make the resulting random variables uncorrelated; that additionally requires the cross-covariance condition $P_i \Sigma P_j^\top=0$. Each decoded path can be regarded as a waypoint set $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^T$. The analysis defines the Hausdorff distance between two sets as

$$
d_H(A, B) = \max \left( \sup_{a \in A} \inf_{b \in B} d(a,b),\ \sup_{b \in B} \inf_{a \in A} d(a,b) \right)
$$

and maximizes average pairwise separation by minimizing a negative diversity term:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{diversity}} = -\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}} \sum_{i=1}^{K-1} \sum_{j=i+1}^{K} d_H(\tau_i, \tau_j)
$$

For finite waypoint sets, the suprema and infima in the Hausdorff definition reduce to maxima and minima evaluated directly over the trajectory points.

This encourages spatial coverage, but diversity alone does not guarantee validity or safety. Without reconstruction, smoothness, collision, reachability, or boundary constraints, maximizing Hausdorff distance could reward candidates that simply spread arbitrarily far apart. The term is therefore best understood as one regularizer inside a fuller trajectory objective.

<details><summary>PyTorch implementation of Latent Orthogonal Projection + Trajectory Decoding:</summary>

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

class TrajectoryGenerator(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent_dim=64, num_trajectories=5, trajectory_len=20):
        super().__init__()
        self.num_trajectories = num_trajectories
        self.trajectory_len = trajectory_len
        # Orthogonal projection vectors
        self.projections = nn.Parameter(torch.eye(latent_dim)[:num_trajectories], requires_grad=False)
        self.decoder = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Linear(latent_dim, 128),
            nn.ReLU(),
            nn.Linear(128, trajectory_len * 2) # (x, y) coordinates
        )

    def forward(self, mu, logvar):
        # Sample latent z
        std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar)
        eps = torch.randn_like(std)
        z = mu + eps * std

        trajectories = []
        for k in range(self.num_trajectories):
            # Project onto k-th orthogonal axis
            z_k = z * self.projections[k]
            tau_k = self.decoder(z_k).view(-1, self.trajectory_len, 2)
            trajectories.append(tau_k)

        return torch.stack(trajectories, dim=1) # Shape: (batch, K, T, 2)
```

</details>

The code is illustrative rather than a literal implementation of the matrix equation. It applies an elementwise standard-basis mask that retains the original latent dimension, whereas $P_k\in\mathbb R^{d\times D}$ in the displayed formulation maps into a $d$-dimensional subspace.

#### Why constrain the VLM's role

The VLM never outputs motor torques or unconstrained coordinates. It ranks trajectories that another model has already expressed in the robot's workspace. This division gives the VLM access to semantic judgment—“which route leads toward the requested building?”—while keeping geometric generation separate.

Reported comparisons favor VL-TGS over the direct-direction CoNVOI baseline and the goal-conditioned NoMaD system, but the analysis provides no exact scores, sample counts, or statistical intervals. It also reports out-of-distribution failures in candidate generation and clips marked red when human intervention was required. In response, the project assembled a 10-campus dataset with roughly 11 hours of ROS bags, five-category global traversability maps, and LiDAR, RGB, 360-degree imagery, IMU, GPS, and odometry. The larger training set reportedly reduced these failures, but the source does not quantify the improvement or establish general out-of-distribution reliability.

The larger lesson is useful even without a definitive benchmark: language models are easier to integrate safely when they choose among structured actions rather than inventing low-level motion from scratch.

### Mapping What Terrain Is Made Of

Geometry and visual semantics still leave an off-road planner with unanswered questions. Grass, soil, wood, and rock can look similar while differing in friction, hardness, stiffness, and density. A useful map must represent not only where a surface is, but how the robot is likely to interact with it.

<details><summary>The proposed local map combines camera-driven 3D Gaussian Splatting with a LiDAR-derived Euclidean Signed Distance Field:</summary>

```
Camera Observations ──> [3D Gaussian Splatting] ──┐
                                                  ├──> [Integrated Local Planning Map]
LiDAR Point Cloud   ──> [Euclidean SDF (ESDF)] ───┘            │
                                                               ▼
Material Semantics  ──> [Gaussian Posterior]    ──────> [Red = Non-Traversable]
Physical Properties ──> [Dirichlet Posterior]   ──────> [Green/Blue/Gray = Splats]
 (Friction, Hardness, Stiffness, Density)
```

</details>

The analysis represents an anisotropic splat as $\mathcal{G}(x) = (\mu, \Sigma, c, \alpha)$ and describes friction as $\mu_f$, stiffness as $k_s$, and density as $\rho$. It separates a continuous semantic-material posterior, $\mathcal{N}(\mu_{mat}, \Sigma_{mat})$, from categorical physical-property beliefs. Using Dirichlet prior parameters $\boldsymbol\alpha_0$ and evidence counts or weights $\mathbf c$, the posterior is

$$
p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathbf{c}) = \text{Dirichlet}(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \boldsymbol{\alpha}_0 + \mathbf{c}) = \frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{k=1}^K (\alpha_{0,k} + c_k)\right)}{\prod_{k=1}^K \Gamma(\alpha_{0,k} + c_k)} \prod_{k=1}^K \theta_k^{\alpha_{0,k} + c_k - 1}
$$

with expectation

$$
\mathbb{E}[\theta_k \mid \mathbf{c}] = \frac{\alpha_{0,k} + c_k}{\sum_{j=1}^K (\alpha_{0,j} + c_j)}
$$

This makes uncertainty explicit: weak evidence leaves a diffuse categorical belief, while repeated evidence concentrates it. The categories remain an approximation of continuous physical properties, and the evidence may combine visual priors with interaction signals rather than literal integer counts.

For its geometric discussion, the analysis writes a splat kernel as $\mathcal{G}_i(x) = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(x - \mu_i)^\top \Sigma_i^{-1} (x - \mu_i)\right)$. The ESDF term is intended to keep those visually optimized Gaussian primitives aligned with metric geometry:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{map}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{render}}(RGB, Sem) + \lambda_{\text{ESDF}} \sum_{i=1}^N \max\left(0,\ \phi_{\text{ESDF}}(\mu_i) - d_{\text{thresh}}\right)^2
$$

The sign and threshold convention are important. The source defines $\phi < 0$ inside obstacle, $\phi > 0$ in free space. Under that convention, the expression penalizes Gaussian centers farther than $d_{\text{thresh}}$ into positive-distance regions; that can suppress floating splats, but it does not penalize negative obstacle penetration as described. The full map objective must therefore define its valid surface band, sign, and hinge direction consistently.

<details><summary>The accompanying Bayesian update is:</summary>

```python
import numpy as np

def update_physical_property_dirichlet(alpha_prior, sensor_evidence):
    """
    Updates Dirichlet categorical posterior parameters for physical terrain properties.
    alpha_prior: np.ndarray shape (K,) - prior hyperparameter
    sensor_evidence: np.ndarray shape (K,) - observed evidence counts (e.g., from vision/tactile)
    """
    alpha_posterior = alpha_prior + sensor_evidence
    expected_probs = alpha_posterior / np.sum(alpha_posterior)
    variance = (alpha_posterior * (np.sum(alpha_posterior) - alpha_posterior)) / \
               ((np.sum(alpha_posterior)**2) * (np.sum(alpha_posterior) + 1))
    return alpha_posterior, expected_probs, variance

# Example: Hardness category [Soft/Pliable, Medium, Hard/Obstacle]
prior = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]) # Uniform Dirichlet prior
evidence = np.array([5.2, 1.1, 0.1]) # Strong evidence for soft/pliable grass
post, expected_p, var = update_physical_property_dirichlet(prior, evidence)
print("Expected Hardness Category Probabilities:", expected_p)
```

</details>

By distinguishing appearance from mechanics, the reported demonstration planned through pliable vegetation while coloring rigid regions red as non-traversable, rather than outlining every plant as a hard obstacle. This remains a qualitative example, not a universal material-classification guarantee. Historical human trail data can supply a useful global prior, but robot-specific costs still matter: a route preferred by hikers may not minimize joint energy, vibration, rollover risk, or mechanical damage for a particular platform.

### Social Navigation Requires Perception, Prediction, and Action

Traversability answers whether the robot can move through a region. Social navigation asks whether it should. A robot on a sidewalk must avoid splitting groups, preserve personal space, anticipate pedestrian motion, and obey traffic lights and crosswalk conventions.

The SNEI dataset supplies specialized social and traffic scenarios, with roughly 40,000 human-annotated visual questions and answers drawn from 2,000 interactions. The resulting system, [Social-LLaVA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09024), adapts LLaVA with low-rank matrices while its original weights remain frozen:

$$
W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + B \cdot A, \quad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}, A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}, r \ll \min(d, k)
$$

For an input $x$, the scaled adapter is

$$
h = W x = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\gamma}{r} B A x
$$

Here $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ is frozen, $B = 0$ at initialization, $r \ll \min(d, k)$, and common illustrative ranks are $r \in \{4, 8, 16\}$; $\gamma$ scales the adapter update. This makes task adaptation more memory-efficient than full fine-tuning. It does not, by itself, prevent catastrophic forgetting or guarantee safe behavior; those properties depend on training data, evaluation coverage, and how the output is connected to the controller.

The reported proof-of-concept benchmark ranks Social-LLaVA highest across perception, prediction, and action, with Gemini strong on pedestrian prediction and ChatGPT lower. The paper's reported comparison uses average human-judge scores over 50 visual questions and answers. Because the analysis omits the detailed metrics and versions, these should be read as results within that limited SNEI evaluation, not permanent rankings of changing commercial models or proof of safe closed-loop control.

GPS routing and a low-level model-predictive controller integrate the social layer on legged and wheeled platforms. The language model reasons about context at a high level; the motion controller remains responsible for dynamically feasible execution.

### From Avoiding People to Accompanying Them

Companion robots for older adults invert a common navigation objective. A delivery robot typically increases distance from pedestrians. A companion must remain nearby, match the person's pace, and preserve a comfortable side-by-side position while both avoid hazards.

A conceptual local objective balances pace, relative position, and obstacles:

$$
J(v_r, \omega_r) = w_{\text{pace}} \|v_r - v_h\|^2 + w_{\text{pos}} \|p_r - (p_h + R(\theta_h) d_{\text{side}})\|^2 + w_{\text{obs}} \mathcal{C}_{\text{obs}}(p_r)
$$

The first term matches robot and human velocity. The second preserves a side offset expressed in the human's heading frame—for example, the source gives $[0.8\text{m}, \pm 0.5\text{m}]^\top$. The third accounts for environmental obstacles. Despite the argument list $J(v_r,\omega_r)$, the displayed expression does not explicitly use angular velocity or encode acceleration limits, social comfort uncertainty, loss of person tracking, or the coupled collision envelope of the human-robot pair. It is a useful local cost template rather than a complete companion planner.

Gitamini had already been demonstrated following a person; the wheeled-legged Unitree robot was still a planned platform and had not yet been acquired at the time of the talk. Beyond navigation, onboard cameras could observe gait and motion patterns associated with fall risk or mobility degradation. Such health inferences would require clinical validation, consent, privacy protection, and careful handling of false alarms; the available work presents a direction rather than a deployed diagnostic system.

### Digital Humans Become a Physical-Interaction Testbed

Digital human models learned from motion capture and video supply reference motions. Physical humanoids test those models under gravity and contact while collecting new ego-vision, force, and joint data.

The proposed flywheel cycles human data into digital models, deploys those models on a physical humanoid, and feeds the resulting contact and sensor data into the next model iteration.

New humanoid-interaction data can update the digital model, which can then support another round of simulation-based policy training. This is a conceptual loop, not a guarantee that more deployed data automatically improves either model. Data selection, safety constraints, privacy, simulator fidelity, and validation determine whether the flywheel compounds useful knowledge or reproduces errors.

The immediate motivation is unexpected contact. A rigid tracking policy treats obstruction as error and may command greater effort precisely when a person or object is blocking the motion. The desired humanoid should remain balanced while reducing, redirecting, or limiting that force.

### GentleHumanoid: Tracking Motion Without Fighting Contact

[GentleHumanoid](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04679v1) learns force-aware compliance during simulation training. Its deployed policy produces joint-position targets directly; the virtual force construction is a training mechanism rather than a force model recomputed from Inter-X measurements at inference.

<details><summary>The notes describe resistant contact, guiding contact, and a tunable spring-damper limit:</summary>

```
   Reference Motion (AMASS / Inter-X)
                  │
                  ▼
┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│              Force Modeling & Compliance                  │

  - Compliant hand dragging & shaking
  - Variable stiffness (5N soft vs 15N stiff)
  - Vision-based adaptive human hugging
```

</details>

Inter-X and the other human-motion datasets supply motion and posture examples; they should not be read as measured contact-force distributions. The following equation is a source-derived conceptualization rather than GentleHumanoid's published Cartesian upper-body link formulation. In this reconstruction, $q, \dot{q}$ are current joint-position and velocity vectors, $q_{\text{ref}}, \dot{q}_{\text{ref}}$ are reference values, and $k_p, k_d$ are virtual stiffness and damping gains:

$$
\tilde{F}_{\text{drive}} = k_p (q_{\text{ref}} - q) + k_d (\dot{q}_{\text{ref}} - \dot{q})
$$

The drive command is then capped at a tunable limit. A concrete implementation must specify whether this means componentwise saturation or projection onto a norm ball, since those operations are not equivalent for vectors. The use of force units for joint-space drive commands also requires a clear mapping between Cartesian force, joint torque, and actuator limits.

The source analysis then combines motion tracking with force and penetration terms:

$$
\mathcal{R} = w_{\text{track}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{track}} + w_{\text{force}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{force}} + w_{\text{pen}} \mathcal{R}_{\text{pen}}
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{force}} = -\exp\left(\max\left(0,\ \|F_{\text{contact}}\| - F_{\text{limit}}\right)^2\right)
$$

$$
\mathcal{R}_{\text{pen}} = -\|p_{\text{penetration}}\|^2
$$

The force penalty $R_{force}$ equals -1 at or below the limit and becomes exponentially more negative above it, potentially creating numerical overflow if implemented naively. It is a schematic penalty, not GentleHumanoid's published reward. Calling $F_{limit}$ a hard safety bound would also be too strong if it appears only inside a soft reward; real contact depends on control delay, state estimation, actuator dynamics, unmodeled impacts, and the sim-to-real gap.

The reported Unitree G1 demonstrations include arm dragging without a fall, soft and stiff behaviors using nominal $5\text{ N}$ and $15\text{ N}$ settings, pressure-pad measurements on a mannequin, and vision-conditioned hugging. In the limited mannequin setup, the force profile was flatter than the standard tracking baseline. The learned policy outputs joint-position targets at 50 Hz in the reported implementation. These experiments support controllable compliance in the demonstrated scenarios. They do not establish general human safety, and mannequin pressure is only one part of evaluating whole-body physical interaction.

### Turning Language and Images Into 3D Reference Motion

Compliance determines how a humanoid executes a reference, but it does not decide whether to hug, shake hands, step back, or assist. [ChatPose](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.18836v2) introduces a 3D body-pose output into a vision-language model, while [ChatHuman](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.04533) acts as a tool-using agent across specialized 3D human models.

The SMPL body model is represented as

$$
M(\boldsymbol{\beta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}) \in \mathbb{R}^{6890 \times 3}
$$

In the published method, body shape is fixed to the average shape, the pose head predicts a 144-dimensional 6D-rotation representation, and LoRA-based instruction tuning combines language cross-entropy with an L1 pose loss.

The parametric body model constrains outputs to the SMPL family, which is more structured than printing 3D coordinates as text. It does not guarantee anatomical feasibility, contact feasibility, collision-free motion, or a dynamically executable humanoid trajectory. A static pose or mesh is also not a trajectory chunk: temporal generation, human-to-robot retargeting, feasibility checks, and embodiment mapping are still required before physical execution.

ChatPose is described as inferring explicit poses from implicit descriptions such as “a man proposing,” which becomes a kneeling 3D pose. ChatHuman extends the idea by selecting specialist tools for reconstruction, motion, biomechanics, and temporal reasoning, with examples involving cooking and reaching motions. Those demonstrations do not establish that its outputs are safe future reference trajectories for a physical robot. Task accuracy, tool-selection failure rates, retargeting, and control-time validation remain open questions.

### Decoupling Slow Reasoning From Fast Control

Vision-language reasoning may take $1.0 - 2.0\text{ seconds}$, while generic low-level humanoid control may run at $100 - 1000\text{ Hz}$ ($1 - 10\text{ ms}$). The system therefore cannot place a VLM directly inside the torque loop.

At the high level,

$$
\text{High-Level VLM (1 Hz):} \quad \tau_{\text{ref}}^{(k)} = \text{ChatPose}(I_k, \text{Prompt})
$$

while low-level execution runs at the control rate:

$$
\text{Low-Level RL (1000 Hz):} \quad a_t = \pi_\phi\left(s_t,\ \text{Interpolate}(\tau_{\text{ref}}^{(k)},\ t),\ F_{\text{limit}}\right)
$$

Future nursing applications deepen the requirements. Tactile sensing must detect soft contact, thermal sensing must check water temperature, active vision must resolve metric geometry near the body, and digital human models must represent impaired rather than only healthy motion. These are high-stakes medical and assistive uses, so technical demonstrations would need clinical validation, privacy safeguards, human-factors research, and fail-safe control before deployment.

## Part IV — Grandmasters of Driving: How Autonomous Racing Stress-Tests Physical AI

Chess is difficult for people and comparatively easy for computers. Driving is routine for many people and remains extraordinarily difficult for machines. That contrast captures Moravec's paradox: abstract reasoning can fit inside a tightly defined computational problem, while ordinary sensorimotor intelligence must contend with uncertain perception, continuous dynamics, physical contact, and an environment that does not follow a finite rulebook.

Autonomous driving is therefore a demanding test of physical AI. A vehicle must operate across changing roads, weather, objects, and human behavior, while every decision unfolds through tire forces, latency, and irreversible motion. A photorealistic video model can imitate how a road looks, but visual plausibility alone does not establish correct causal dynamics or make a safety argument.

This approaches the problem through comparative scenario retrieval, adversarial falsification, scaled racing platforms, probabilistic trajectory representations, full-size autonomous racing, and fault-tolerant system engineering. Racing provides the experimental through-line. It compresses perception, planning, control, and hardware reliability into a deliberately narrow setting where failures appear quickly and physical limits cannot be ignored.

The central claim needs one qualification at the outset: a racetrack is a powerful stress test for agility and systems integration, not a substitute for public-road validation. Track performance can reveal whether an autonomous system remains competent near the edge of its dynamic envelope. It cannot prove that the same system understands pedestrians, construction, regional traffic rules, or the full diversity of an open world.

### Driving Is an Open-System Problem

Chess and Go are closed systems. Their pieces, legal moves, and state transitions are bounded by explicit rules. Driving is open-ended: a vehicle may encounter unfamiliar objects, unusual weather, construction, sensor degradation, regional conventions, and combinations of agent behavior absent from its training data.

A conceptual shorthand treats the total scenario space as

$$
\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{O} \times \mathcal{E} \times \mathcal{T}
$$

Here, $\mathcal O$ represents objects, $\mathcal E$ environments, and $\mathcal T$ temporal interactions. An operational design domain covers only a bounded subset of this much larger scenario space. The equation is conceptual rather than a literal product of three well-defined manifolds: no agreed measure assigns a meaningful volume to every possible object, road, and interaction.

#### Why photorealism is not physics

Video foundation models can generate convincing road scenes while introducing inconsistent background motion, spontaneous objects, impossible contact, or incorrect traffic behavior. Those failures do not mean generative models have no role in simulation. They show that visual realism and physical validity are different properties.

A safety-relevant world model must preserve stable object identity, contact constraints, multi-agent causality, and dynamics that respond correctly to intervention. Physical AI ultimately has to be tested against those properties, not only judged by how realistic its output appears.

### Make Safety Comparative Before Making It Absolute

Absolute safety is difficult to summarize with one number. Collision rates depend on exposure, geography, road type, weather, intervention policy, and the difficulty of the situations a fleet encounters. A more tractable question is comparative: when two software versions or fleets face sufficiently similar scenarios, which behaves better?

The implementations differ in important ways. In [Scenario2Vector](https://past.date-conference.com/proceedings-archive/2024/DATA/6018_pdf_upload.pdf), a custom video-transformer method detects ego-vehicle actions from front-camera BDD-X video and is compared with C3D and ViViT baselines; it is not simply a ViViT-to-SDL converter. A trajectory-based method called [TAP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20353) extracts hierarchical Scenario Description Language labels and optimizes their importance for search on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset.

The trajectory route avoids comparing raw appearance, but it is not fully perception-independent unless the trajectories come from trusted ground truth. In ordinary fleet logs, their quality still depends on detection, tracking, data association, calibration, and localization.

#### Measuring similarity in trajectory space

For two scenarios $S_A$ and $S_B$ containing $N$ corresponding agents, a conceptual distance is

$$
d(S_A, S_B) = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i \cdot d_{\text{DTW}}(\tau_i^A,\ \tau_i^B) + \lambda \cdot \| \text{Map}_A - \text{Map}_B \|_{\text{geom}}
$$

where Dynamic Time Warping aligns trajectories that unfold at different temporal rates:

$$
d_{\text{DTW}}(\tau^A, \tau^B) = \min_{\pi} \sum_{(k, l) \in \pi} \|\tau^A(k) - \tau^B(l)\|_2
$$

The first term measures agent motion, and the second compares map geometry. This can retrieve interactions that look different in pixels but share a similar structure.

The scenario-distance formula is an analytical reconstruction, not TAP's published objective or a complete scenario-retrieval specification. It assumes that agent identities and counts are already aligned, coordinate frames are normalized, and map difference has a defined geometry. Unconstrained time warping may also erase a timing difference that is itself safety-critical.

LLM transcription can add semantic search—such as finding tunnel interactions or aggressive merges—but it may omit metric details or introduce interpretations absent from the trajectories. Retrieval enables controlled comparison; it does not by itself prove that either system is safe.

### Falsification: Search Deliberately for Failure

Real-world mileage is expensive and statistically inefficient for finding rare failures. Random simulation is safer but still spends most of its time on ordinary cases. Automated falsification changes the objective: search directly for situations that make a target planner violate a safety specification.

The [CRASH framework](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.16996) uses reinforcement learning to control background traffic adversarially: it evaluates a simplified two-lane, two-agent highway setting with one adversarial NPC, not a demonstrated fleet of colluding vehicles. In that setting, the adversary produced reported crash rates of 97% against a rule-based planner and 90% against a DQN planner; iterative hardening reduced the ego planner's average crash rate from 64% to 38%, a drop of 26 percentage points. The paper explicitly characterizes the results as preliminary.

A generic analytical reconstruction of a realism-aware adversarial reward is

```math
R_{\text{NPC}}(s, \mathbf{a}) = \mathbb{I}_{\text{crash}}(e, \text{NPCs}) \cdot C_{\text{realism}}(s) - \lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^M \|u_i\|_2^2 - \lambda_2 \mathbb{I}_{\text{unrealistic\_traffic\_violation}}
```

The crash indicator rewards successful falsification, while the other terms discourage physically impossible or trivially malicious behavior. This is not CRASH's published reward, which uses a collision term and longitudinal and lateral time-to-collision shaping.

Discovered crashes can be saved as regression scenarios and used to retrain the ego planner. Repeated adversary–planner training resembles a minimax tournament, but it does not certify safety. An adversary may exploit simulator artifacts, and both sides can overfit a narrow crash library. Falsification produces valuable counterexamples; their real-world relevance still depends on simulator fidelity and independent validation.

### Racing as a Physical-AI Laboratory

Motorsport has long served as a concentrated test bed for vehicle engineering. Autonomous racing applies the same idea to perception, planning, control, compute, and fault tolerance.

The open-source [F1TENTH](https://f1tenth.org/) platform—rendered as “F1/10” in the diagram—makes that experimentation accessible at one-tenth scale. The overview is conceptual: sensor configurations vary by build, and the historical arrow describes racing as a development and testing environment rather than proving that each listed technology originated there.

A prototype was developed at the University of Pennsylvania in 2015, and the F1TENTH competition community dates its organized races to 2016. It grew into an ecosystem of open build instructions, software, curriculum, and more than 30 international races. A typical car combines onboard computation, configurable sensing, electronic steering, and a high-performance radio-controlled chassis. Researchers and students can test localization, planning, overtaking, and control on hardware without the cost and danger of a full-size race car.

#### The kinematic foundation

A common planning approximation is the kinematic bicycle model:

$$
\dot{x} = v \cos(\theta + \beta)
$$

$$
\dot{y} = v \sin(\theta + \beta)
$$

$$
\dot{\theta} = \frac{v}{L} \cos(\beta) \tan(\delta)
$$

$$
\beta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{l_r}{L} \tan(\delta)\right)
$$

The state consists of position and heading. Here, $v$ is the center-of-mass speed magnitude, $\delta$ the front steering angle, $L$ the wheelbase, $l_r$ the rear-axle-to-center-of-mass distance, and $\beta$ a kinematic approximation of the velocity angle at the center of mass—not a dynamic tire-slip state. The model is compact enough for real-time trajectory tracking and model-predictive control.

Near the tire limit, it becomes incomplete because it omits tire slip, load transfer, actuator dynamics, and transient forces. Scale racing also does not preserve full-size aerodynamic, tire, and impact similarity. F1TENTH is valuable because it isolates real sensing and control problems in repeatable physical experiments—not because it reproduces every feature of a road car.

### Compressing Trajectories With Probabilistic Curves

Testing richer multi-agent strategies motivated DeepRacing, a simulator interface built around the Codemasters F1 game series and ROS 2. The environment supplies camera data, vehicle state, and competitor motion without putting hardware at risk.

Predicting hundreds of waypoint values creates a large output space and can accumulate local errors. A Bézier curve describes an entire path using only a few control points:

$$
B(t) = \sum_{i=0}^P \binom{P}{i} (1-t)^{P-i} t^i \mathbf{P}_i, \quad t \in [0, 1]
$$

The scalar coefficients in this sum are Bernstein basis polynomials.

A cubic example uses four control points, reducing a long waypoint sequence to eight spatial scalars. In either case, the curve is smooth, but smoothness alone does not guarantee feasible curvature, timing, collision avoidance, or compliance with track boundaries.

#### Differential Bayesian Filtering

Instead of predicting one deterministic curve, [Differential Bayesian Filtering](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.07587) places probability distributions over Bézier control points, samples candidate trajectories, and filters them using knowledge of the vehicle's feasibility limits. The primary paper evaluates the method in the DeepRacing Formula One simulation environment, where it reports the fastest lap and speed among its comparisons while remaining inside track bounds.

A simplified analytical reconstruction scores a trajectory particle as

$$
w^{(k)} = \exp\left(-\frac{J_{\text{speed}}(\tau^{(k)}) + \lambda_{\text{bound}} J_{\text{bound}}(\tau^{(k)}) + \lambda_{\text{dyn}} J_{\text{dynamics}}(\tau^{(k)})}{\sigma_{\text{score}}}\right)
$$

followed by the mean update

$$
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{updated}} = \sum_{k=1}^K w^{(k)} \mathbf{P}^{(k)}
$$

These two equations are analytical reconstructions rather than a reproduction of the published filter. This combines piecewise likelihoods for centripetal acceleration, longitudinal acceleration, and track boundaries, then explicitly normalizes sample weights before taking a posterior mean. In the displayed simplification, the second equation assumes normalized weights; otherwise it must divide by $\sum_k w^{(k)}$. An implementation must also specify its sampling covariance, resampling or stopping procedure, and treatment of correlations among control points. In practice, the filter must sample control points, score the resulting curves against feasibility likelihoods, normalize the weights, and update the posterior.

Commercial game telemetry offers a rich, safe sandbox, but its dynamics remain an approximation. Simulation results need hardware validation before they can support claims about real tire behavior or vehicle safety.

### Scaling to a Full-Size Autonomous Race Car

Cavalier Autonomous Racing moved from small prototypes and simulation to a modified Dallara race car in the Indy Autonomous Challenge (IAC). The early IAC platform was the Dallara AV-21, based on the IL-15 Indy Lights chassis; the 2024 competition used the newer AV-24.

In the 2024 AV-24, UVA reports three LiDARs, two radars, six cameras, and four GNSS antennas; computers occupy the former driver space, while steering, throttle, and braking are actuated electronically.

The car replaces human control with drive-by-wire steering, throttle, and braking, supported by LiDAR, cameras, radar, satellite positioning, and liquid-cooled computation. The testing program increases risk gradually:

1. Initiate and stabilize an emergency stop from 150 mph.
2. Overtake virtual “ghost” opponents.
3. Track and predict a real vehicle at speed.
4. Attempt head-to-head physical overtaking.

This progression matters as much as peak speed because it isolates failure modes before combining them in a multi-car event. The braking test issued an emergency-stop command at 150 mph and evaluated controlled deceleration to rest.

#### Emergency braking at speed

A simplified braking-force envelope is

$$
F_{x,b}^{\max} = \mu \left( F_{z,0} + \frac{1}{2} \rho C_L A v^2 + \frac{h}{L} m a_x \right)
$$

The expression combines static vertical load, aerodynamic downforce, and longitudinal load transfer. It illustrates why available braking force changes with speed: aerodynamic load falls with $v^2$, so brake pressure appropriate early in a high-speed stop may exceed the tire limit later.

This is not a complete axle-level brake model. It is best read as an illustrative axle-level bound: if $F_{z,0}$ represented total vehicle load, longitudinal transfer would cancel across the whole vehicle. For an axle, the sign of $a_x$, static and aerodynamic load split, and brake bias must be specified. Combined tire forces, actuator delay, temperature, and anti-lock behavior also change the usable force.

At the inaugural 2021 event, [UVA reported](https://engineering.virginia.edu/news-events/news/uva-earns-international-bragging-rights-driverless-racing) an average near 120 mph and a 124 mph top speed; the [team's own summary](https://autonomousracing.dev/who-we-are) lists a 126 mph peak. Later events included head-to-head overtaking around 145 mph.

Those results are distinct from Cavalier's 2024 self-correcting pass. In that maneuver, [UVA reports](https://www.engineering.virginia.edu/news-events/news/watch-uva-displays-its-new-high-speed-self-correcting-ai-algorithms) that the car accelerated to about 90 mph for an outside pass, revised its prediction as the opponent moved outward, slowed from 91 to 65 mph, and completed an inside pass. The example demonstrates closed-track replanning under dynamic load, not general open-road reasoning.

### Racing at the Tire-Friction Limit

On September 6, 2024, Cavalier won the IAC time trial with a 52.628-second lap. The organizer reports a 171.012 mph average and a 184 mph peak, describing the peak as a [world record for autonomous speed on a racetrack](https://www.indyautonomouschallenge.com/ims-results-2024). An afternoon downpour delayed the event, but UVA says officials dried the course and wet asphalt was not a risk factor during the run.

The front-axle slip-angle approximation is

$$
\alpha = \delta - \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{v_y + l_f \omega}{v_x}\right)
$$

and a Pacejka-style tire relation gives

$$
F_y(\alpha) = D \sin\left(C \arctan\left(B \alpha - E (B \alpha - \arctan(B \alpha))\right)\right)
$$

Here, $\delta$ is steering angle, $v_x$ and $v_y$ are longitudinal and lateral body-frame velocities, $l_f$ is the center-of-mass-to-front-axle distance, and $\omega$ is yaw rate. The tire initially generates more lateral force as slip grows, reaches a peak, and eventually loses effectiveness. Temperature, surface condition, load, and alignment shift that envelope, making state estimation important near the limit.

These equations are simplified. Individual wheels have different slip angles and normal loads, and the expressions omit combined longitudinal–lateral slip, transient tire relaxation, banking, camber, and aerodynamic coupling. The slip-angle expression also needs numerical protection when $v_x$ approaches zero.

The team attributed part of a gap between an internal prediction of roughly 175 mph and the achieved 171.012 mph average to unmodeled wind and aerodynamic effects. That is an engineering hypothesis about model mismatch, not a uniquely identified causal result.

#### Beyond the oval

Oval racing offers repeated geometry and sustained speed. A road course adds elevation, alternating turns, heavy braking, and occlusion. The reported Laguna Seca work uses high-speed LiDAR simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) as a redundant localization path where satellite positioning degrades under bridges or vegetation.

SLAM provides redundancy; it does not guarantee localization integrity. Reliability still depends on map quality, geometric features, synchronization, motion distortion, and sensor health. Four-car racing was initially framed as the next frontier; a [four-car exhibition followed in January 2025](https://engineering.virginia.edu/news-events/news/cavalier-autonomous-makes-history-again-time-thrilling-4-car-robot-race), but a controlled demonstration with multiple overtakes is not the same as a solved general multi-agent capability.

#### Rigid-body auto-labeling

Because both race cars have known geometry and synchronized pose logs, an opponent's relative pose can be computed as

$$
T_{\text{opp}}^{\text{ego}} = T_{\text{ego}}^{-1} \cdot T_{\text{opp}} = \begin{bmatrix} R_{\text{ego}}^\top R_{\text{opp}} & R_{\text{ego}}^\top (p_{\text{opp}} - p_{\text{ego}}) \\ 0_{1 \times 3} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and projects the known vehicle mesh into a sensor representation:

$$
\text{BoundingBox}_{\text{3D}} = \pi_{\text{sensor}}\left(T_{\text{opp}}^{\text{ego}} \cdot \text{Mesh}_{\text{opp}}\right)
$$

The first equation places the opponent in the ego vehicle's body frame, not directly in a camera or LiDAR frame. A calibrated body-to-sensor extrinsic is therefore required before sensor projection. A sensor-frame 3D cuboid must also be distinguished from a 2D image box produced by projection.

This process can create large quantities of labels without manually drawing boxes. The equations are an idealized analytical reconstruction, and the output is better described as geometric pseudo-ground truth than as “perfect” labels. RTK-GPS uncertainty, timestamp alignment, calibration, suspension motion, mesh mismatch, occlusion, and sensor latency all contribute error. The method is especially effective in racing because the vehicles are rigid, instrumented, and geometrically known; ordinary road users do not exchange comparable telemetry.

### What Racing Teaches—and What It Does Not

Autonomous racing contributes several transferable lessons:

- **Representations matter.** A few probabilistic Bézier control points can be easier to learn and constrain than hundreds of independent waypoints.
- **Search should seek failures.** Adversarial falsification targets rare planner weaknesses more directly than undirected mileage.
- **Risk should increase incrementally.** Emergency braking, ghost opponents, real tracking, and head-to-head racing test different layers before they are combined.
- **Physical limits expose integration errors.** Tire forces, latency, actuator behavior, and sensor timing cannot be hidden behind visually convincing output.
- **Redundancy must be operational.** A fallback localization or watchdog path matters only if it is tested under the failure it is meant to handle.
- **Data pipelines can exploit structure.** Known rigid bodies and synchronized telemetry can replace large amounts of manual annotation.

A track has controlled access, known geometry, specialized rules, trained participants, and no ordinary pedestrians or intersections. Racing's strongest transfer lies in planning, control, localization redundancy, and system integration—not in reproducing the full open-road environment.

Historical comparisons with human racing records can provide context, but they involve different vehicles, rules, surfaces, and eras. They are not controlled evidence that an autonomous driver has reached general human driving competence.
