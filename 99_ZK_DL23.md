# From Language Models to Reliable Agents: Learning, Action, and Infrastructure

A language model can explain how to solve a problem and still struggle to complete the work. Writing a useful answer requires knowledge; carrying out a task also requires planning, memory, tools, feedback, and the ability to recover when something goes wrong. The gap between those abilities explains why progress in AI increasingly depends on the system surrounding the model as well as the model itself.

Research on agentic modeling, reasoning, world models, robotics, and reinforcement learning illuminates different parts of this problem. A harness supplies memory and execution support. Intermediate reasoning makes inference time useful. Simulators and sandboxes give actions measurable consequences. Training transfers successful behavior into model weights, while execution traces preserve the evidence needed to understand what happened.

Those connections extend into the infrastructure of deployed agents. Enterprise knowledge systems must retrieve accurate, authorized relationships. Inference engines must retain state across tool calls and use hardware efficiently. Training systems must account for the policy that actually generated an experience. Communication protocols must preserve meaning when work crosses technical or organizational boundaries.

The common challenge is to make experience informative. A convincing simulation can misrepresent physical consequences; a benchmark score can conceal dependence on a harness; an experiment can produce a persuasive claim without adequate evidence. Reliable agents require environments, representations, and evaluation methods that distinguish useful work from apparent success.

## How agents turn experience into capability

An AI agent combines a language model with an **agent harness**: software that manages memory, context compression, prompts, tool calls, task decomposition, and recovery from failures. These functions compensate for behavior the underlying model cannot yet perform reliably.

Microsoft Research describes **internalization** as transferring some of these capabilities into the model through supervised learning or reinforcement learning. Software initially supplies a behavior; successful interactions then provide examples from which the model can learn to perform more of that behavior itself. The agent combines model and harness, the environment supplies tools and feedback, and the trainer uses recorded interactions to improve subsequent behavior.

An effective harness produces richer trajectories: records of actions, observations, and outcomes. Better trajectories support training, while stronger models can tackle harder tasks and help develop more capable harnesses.

Three Microsoft Research projects illustrate this relationship. Orchard supplies a Kubernetes-native environment service for running agents at scale. **EvoLib** explores how agents accumulate, reuse, and evolve knowledge across tasks. GFlowRL investigates training policies that preserve a distribution of useful solutions.

Orchard's official page reports 73% on SWE-bench Verified using a Qwen3.5-35B-A3B backbone and best-of-N value-model reranking, compared with 69.7% for its rubric-based reinforcement-learning result. These are different configurations. EvoLib raises another evaluation question: how much earlier experience remains useful when tasks or environments change? Answering requires an account of retrieval, updating, and evaluation.

### Preserve multiple ways to succeed

A training process can reward success while retaining different successful approaches. The distribution-matching target associated with GFlowNet-style learning is:

$$
\pi^*(x) \propto R(x)
$$

Here, $x$ denotes a completed candidate solution, $R(x)$ is its nonnegative reward, and $\pi*(x)$ is its target sampling probability. For a discrete set of candidates with a finite, positive normalization constant $Z = \sum_x R(x)$, the normalized target is $\pi*(x) = R(x)/Z$. Higher rewards receive more probability, while multiple rewarded solutions remain represented.

GFlowRL adds an in-batch normalizer estimate and corrections for differences between rollout and training policies. Preserving diversity supports exploration but cannot compensate for an incorrectly specified reward.

Policy distillation introduces another choice about which behavior to preserve. Reverse Kullback–Leibler divergence, $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi^*)$, strongly penalizes student behavior to which the target assigns little probability and tends to concentrate on supported modes. Forward KL places greater pressure on the student to cover the target distribution broadly. These objectives require target probabilities or appropriate estimators. Choosing experiences and learning objectives therefore affects which successful behaviors survive internalization.

## Reasoning makes inference time useful

Improvement need not wait for a weight update. A model can spend additional computation while solving a particular problem. **Test-time compute** includes generating intermediate reasoning, checking alternatives, and reconsidering a tentative answer. OpenAI's Learning to reason with LLMs describes the broader opportunity to improve performance through both reasoning training and additional thinking time.

Models that failed arithmetic, counting, or categorization sometimes succeeded when prompted to work through intermediate steps. One illustration asked a model to recognize a clarinet and a piano as instruments in a list containing a dog. Another involved counting a list whose correct length was fourteen; the correct immediate answer initially received only about 2% probability. The examples exposed a gap between possessing relevant patterns and organizing them into a reliable answer.

### Why intermediate tokens change the answer

An autoregressive model conditions its next-token distribution on the preceding context. Each reasoning step becomes part of that context. Listing objects one by one supplies structure that an immediate guess lacks; checking a calculation can reveal an inconsistency before the final answer is produced. The model's weights stay fixed, while the information available to subsequent predictions changes.

Pre-training uses token-prediction errors to update weights. Inference uses the resulting patterns to organize familiar facts into more helpful intermediate states.

Prompts that elicited longer reasoning also produced higher accuracy in the early experiments. That observation needs its experimental context: the prompts changed instructions as well as length. It therefore does not establish that adding tokens by itself always improves an answer. Useful intermediate computation can improve performance, and text provides a familiar medium for expressing that computation because the model already learns to produce and condition on text during pre-training.

The historical experiments in that retrospective used an approximately 8,000-token budget. Larger contexts hold more intermediate material, but storage capacity alone does not establish productive reasoning. Additional tokens can repeat a mistake; the practical measure is progress for the computation consumed.

## Parallel generation changes the economics of reasoning

If intermediate text helps problem solving, its generation cost becomes important. Conventional autoregressive decoding has a sequential dependency: each token depends on the tokens already generated. Training can process many positions in parallel, while ordinary generation still advances one token at a time.

Inception's Mercury research explores diffusion language models that repeatedly refine multiple token positions together. The aim is to make generation more compatible with parallel hardware. Rather than extending a sequence only at its end, a model can improve many positions during one inference pass.

The challenge is that text is discrete. Image diffusion can corrupt continuous pixel values with Gaussian noise; tokens require a suitable discrete corruption process. A **masked diffusion language model**, for example, replaces selected words or subwords with a special `[MASK]` token and learns to reconstruct the original text. Low noise levels leave most of the text visible, while the most heavily corrupted examples can hide the entire sequence.

### Learn reconstruction across noise levels

The predictor can use visible context on both sides of a masked position. Simultaneous predictions within a pass do not make every token independent throughout generation: successive refinement passes expose more resolved context and allow the sequence to become more coherent.

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}[1,T]}\; \mathbb{E}_{x_t \sim q(\cdot \mid x_0)}\!\left[\sum_{i : x_t^{(i)} = \texttt{[MASK]}} -\log p_\theta\!\left(x_0^{(i)} \mid x_t\right)\right]
$$

The clean sequence is $x_0$, $x_t$ its corrupted version, and $q$ defines masking at noise level $t$. The sum penalizes incorrect reconstruction at masked positions. In practice, the objective is also averaged over training sequences.

### Measure the whole decoding process

Parallel denoising still requires repeated passes. If a sequence contains $L$ tokens and takes $S$ refinement steps, substantial parallelism within each step can reduce the number of sequential stages when $S$ is much smaller than $L$. Illustrative schedules of ten to sixty-four passes make that opportunity concrete.

The stage-count ratio is not a guaranteed elapsed-time speedup. Each pass processes many positions. Sequence length, hardware utilization, caching, and the quality achieved at a particular number of passes all affect performance. The distinctive opportunity concerns generation; conventional language-model training already benefits from parallel processing across positions.

Mercury and [Mercury 2](https://www.inceptionlabs.ai/blog/introducing-mercury-2) illustrate the commercial motivation. Inception reports favorable quality–speed comparisons with speed-optimized autoregressive models. Those comparisons describe particular models, hardware, and evaluation conditions. Their relevance depends on the workload, acceptable quality, and whether the important metric is throughput, time to first output, or completion latency.

Voice and support agents need responsive interaction. Search systems must fit retrieval, reranking, and generation into a latency budget. Coding systems can use a fast model for frequent steps and a more expensive model for difficult decisions. Faster generation changes how much intermediate work such systems can afford. Whether iterative refinement supports difficult reasoning as effectively as it produces fluent text remains a separate evaluation question.

## World models expand where agents can learn

Reasoning helps an agent choose actions, but learning from those actions requires an environment. A software agent can interact with a terminal or browser. A robot or autonomous vehicle needs a representation of a physical world that changes in response to movement.

Real-world testing is constrained by physical time, equipment, and the difficulty of reproducing rare events. Simulation permits many scenarios to run in parallel and, where computationally feasible, faster than real time. NVIDIA's work on physical AI follows an evolution from manually constructed scenes to reconstructed environments and generative world models.

Traditional simulation requires artists and engineers to build assets and scenarios. **3D Gaussian splatting** instead learns a scene representation from captured images. Spatial primitives carry color, extent, and opacity and can be projected into new camera views. Rendering combines their contributions from front to back, reducing the contribution of objects hidden behind nearer surfaces.

NVIDIA reported running two million autonomous-vehicle simulations per day using reconstruction-based infrastructure. That scale makes captured scenes valuable, but reconstruction does not automatically invent events absent from the recordings. A new viewpoint within a familiar scene differs from a new scenario involving unexpected weather, traffic behavior, or an unfamiliar obstacle. Generative world models aim to supply additional flexibility by predicting observations conditioned on the agent's actions.

### Make generated environments interactive

NVIDIA's work under the **OmniDreams** and **FlashDreams** names adapts pretrained video diffusion models to robotics or driving data, including multiple cameras. Causal conditioning restricts generation to available history and current actions. Distillation reduces denoising work, while inference engineering makes the response fast enough for interaction. The essential conditioning requirement is:

$$
\hat{f}_{t+1} = \mathcal{W}_\theta\!\left(\{f_{t-k}, \ldots, f_t\},\; a_t\right)
$$

The world model $W_\theta$ takes recent frames and action $a_t$, such as steering or acceleration, to generate the next observation $\hat{f}_{t+1}$. Here, $t$ denotes time in the environment, rather than a diffusion noise level. The history includes the current frame and $k$ earlier frames. Multiple cameras must remain mutually consistent if their outputs are to guide the same policy.

Latency is a modeling and engineering problem. NVIDIA's reported milestones moved from about five minutes of GPU work for five seconds of generated video in March 2025 to real-time interaction by March 2026, followed by an RTX 5090 demonstration in June 2026. These are development observations rather than a controlled comparison across identical systems. They illustrate why an environment must be assessed by its ability to respond to actions, as well as by the appearance of its output.

Visual plausibility remains only part of that assessment. A production driving policy responding sensibly to generated observations is encouraging, but does not replace a formal simulation-to-real transfer measurement. Keeping a simulated vehicle on the ground does not establish accurate collisions or rigid-body interactions. Changing the viewpoint from a car to a truck tests generalization without proving support for every robot embodiment. The useful question is whether generated consequences are faithful enough for the behavior being learned.

## Sandboxes turn attempts into verifiable feedback

For software tasks, isolated execution environments make action outcomes directly observable. An agent writes code or calls a tool, the environment executes it, and a verifier returns an outcome that can guide training.

**Reinforcement learning from human feedback**, or RLHF, uses a reward model trained on human preferences. **Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards**, or RLVR, instead uses checks such as whether a program passes its tests. Tool use and multi-turn interaction extend evaluation from grading an answer to assessing a sequence of actions. Daytona's execution-based reinforcement-learning guide demonstrates how isolated parallel attempts can support test-based feedback.

Daytona reported a Python-coding experiment combining Qwen3-8B, eight H100 GPUs, four hours of training, and 1,440 rollouts. Average reward rose from about 0.3 to about 0.6. The behavioral observations were especially revealing: the model stopped writing literal `\n` characters where Python files required actual newlines and reduced errors involving nested double quotes.

Much of that improvement concerned executable expression of knowledge the model already appeared to possess. That is a specific interpretation of this experiment, rather than a general limit on what reinforcement learning can teach. A correct algorithm has little operational value if quoting or file formatting prevents it from running.

### Compare a group of attempts

**Group Relative Policy Optimization**, or GRPO, compares several attempts at the same task. Their rewards provide a relative baseline without a separately trained value network. Its normalized outcome-reward advantage is:

$$
\hat{A}_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \mu_r}{\sigma_r}, \quad \mu_r = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G} r_j, \quad \sigma_r = \sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(r_j - \mu_r)^2}
$$

Here, $G$ is the number of attempts, $r_i$ is attempt $i$'s reward, and $\mu_r$ and $\sigma_r$ are the group's mean and standard deviation. This simplified formulation assigns the same outcome-derived signal across an attempt's generated tokens. The subsequent policy update also uses a new-to-old probability ratio, clipping to limit large changes, and a penalty for departing from a reference policy.

For four attempts with rewards $[1,1,1,0]$, the mean is 0.75 and the standard deviation is about 0.433. Each passing attempt receives an advantage of approximately $+0.58$, while the failure receives approximately $-1.73$. The negative coefficient has three times the magnitude, but parameter changes also depend on token gradients, rollout lengths, and clipping.

Identical rewards require special handling because their standard deviation is zero. A practical implementation must stabilize the denominator or explicitly handle such groups. The relative reward signal can then be set to zero, although reference-policy regularization may still contribute an update. Training needs tasks that distinguish better behavior from worse behavior.

The environment contributes to that distinction. SkyRL can handle model updates, Harbor can coordinate rollouts, and Daytona can supply isolated execution. Their responsibilities must fit together without giving the agent control over its own grading. Changing tests or manipulating the scoring process can increase measured reward without completing the intended task. Isolation supports reproducibility; a trustworthy verifier preserves the meaning of success.

## Scaling experience without losing its meaning

Scaling introduces constraints beyond producing more attempts. Rollouts finish at different times, specialist behavior must be combined, reasoning consumes variable numbers of tokens, and deployed harnesses may differ from those used during training. A large sandbox count alone does not determine the number of optimizer steps: grouping, reuse, and scheduling all affect that relationship.

### Balance utilization and policy freshness

Synchronous reinforcement learning waits for required rollouts before updating. A few long tasks can leave training hardware idle. Asynchronous systems consume completed rollouts sooner, improving utilization while introducing experiences generated by older policy versions.

The gap matters because optimization depends on the relationship between the policy that collected a trajectory and the policy being updated. Greater staleness complicates estimation and can destabilize learning. Semi-asynchronous approaches pause and resume rollout work around weight updates to balance throughput against freshness. Environment lifecycle support can reduce another cost: an execution sandbox may be idle while the model generates its next action, so suspending an idle microVM can reduce resource use.

### Combine specialists through distillation

Specialist reinforcement-learning runs can target general tasks, agentic interaction, and coding, with different reasoning budgets. Distillation can transfer their behavior into a generalist. When full expert probabilities are available, matching the expert at student-visited prefixes can be written as:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{distill}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t} D_{KL}\!\left(\pi_E(\cdot \mid x_{<t}) \;\|\; \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{<t})\right)\right]
$$

The student $\pi_\theta$ generates trajectory $x$, and expert $\pi_E$ supplies a next-token distribution at each prefix $x_{<t}$. This is forward KL at each visited prefix, despite the contexts coming from the student. It differs from the reverse-KL objective discussed earlier. The direction of the divergence and the source of the sampled contexts are separate choices.

Full distributions supply richer supervision than a final answer or scalar reward, but require access that many teacher interfaces do not provide. A probability for one sampled token is not a full distribution. This access requirement applies to the displayed objective, rather than to every technique called distillation.

### Teach the model to budget its reasoning

Token-budget reward shaping makes the cost of additional thinking explicit:

$$
r(x, B) = \begin{cases} R_{\text{task}}(x) & \text{if } |x| \leq B \\ -1 & \text{if } |x| > B \end{cases}
$$

The trajectory $x$ includes reasoning and the answer; $|x|$ is its length, $B$ is the assigned budget, and $R_{\text{task}}(x)$ is the ordinary task reward. Exceeding the budget produces a reward of $-1$, even for a correct answer. This strict illustrative rule trains success under a resource constraint, provided the budget leaves enough room for useful work.

Different budgets support quick responses or more expensive attempts at difficult problems. Reward design can teach when additional reasoning justifies its cost.

Harness diversity presents another generalization problem. **Dynamic harness augmentation** varies memory, skills, or subagent tools during training, exposing the model to different interaction patterns. A stable product may benefit from closer specialization to one harness. Broader deployment needs evidence that useful behavior survives changes in the surrounding software. Structured synthetic environments can similarly expand the task families available for learning, provided their feedback reveals failures that matter outside those environments.

## Why actions change the learning problem

An agent trained on demonstrations learns what an expert did in the states the expert encountered. Deployment introduces a different distribution: the states the agent reaches through its own decisions. A small steering error changes the next camera image. A misplaced object changes the next grasp. An incorrect tool call changes the information available to a software agent. Over a long task, these changes can expose the learner to situations its training examples never covered.

The imitation-learning problem associated with DAgger, or dataset aggregation, is often described through a worst-case accumulated error proportional to $\epsilon T^2$, where $\epsilon$ is a single-step imitation error rate and $T$ is the task length. An early mistake can affect many subsequent decisions. This is a bound under assumptions about costs and distribution shift, rather than a prediction that every policy's actual error grows quadratically. With unit-bounded costs, total cost also cannot exceed $T$, so the quadratic expression can quickly become loose.

DAgger addresses the problem by collecting expert labels on states visited by the learner. Reinforcement learning and planning offer other ways to improve behavior on the trajectories the agent actually produces. A world model supplies an environment in which to investigate those trajectories before acting in the real system.

### World models make evaluation interactive

Open-loop evaluation compares a policy with recorded observations or actions. Closed-loop evaluation lets the policy act and respond to the consequences. An action-conditioned world model predicts the next state or observation from the current state, the agent's action, and optional conditions such as a scene layout, weather, language instruction, or other agents' trajectories. The policy chooses its next action from the model's output, making errors and recovery part of the evaluation.

The [Waymo World Model](https://waymo.com/blog/2026/02/the-waymo-world-model-a-new-frontier-for-autonomous-driving-simulation/) illustrates this approach. Built on Google DeepMind's Genie 3 and adapted to driving, it generates camera and LiDAR observations together and accepts controls over driving actions and scene attributes. Unusual weather and unfamiliar objects expand testing beyond situations frequently encountered in fleet data. Action conditioning enables counterfactual evaluation: begin with the same scene, vary steering or acceleration, and inspect the consequences.

That capability requires validation. Visual plausibility does not establish accurate contact dynamics, realistic road-user behavior, or physically consistent LiDAR. A demonstration of a long rollout also does not quantify sensor fidelity. World models create opportunities for testing; their accuracy determines how much confidence those tests deserve.

### Reliability becomes an operating requirement

Physical autonomy also exposes the demands of scale. At 1,000 operations per day, a 1% failure rate implies ten expected failures; a 0.001% failure rate implies 0.01. Each additional nine in a reliability percentage reduces the residual failure rate by a factor of ten. Whether any particular rate is acceptable depends on the meaning and consequences of failure.

Better sensing, redundancy, fallbacks, and changes to the operating boundary can all contribute. Moving from a tool that assists a person to an agent that completes work independently changes the system's responsibilities. Human oversight must itself be evaluated: a person reviewing occasional software decisions faces different timing constraints from a driver responding to a takeover request. The existence of an intervention mechanism does not establish that intervention will work when needed.

## Ace: learning the strike and controlling its execution

Sony AI's Ace table tennis robot makes the interaction between learning and engineering concrete. A custom arm with six revolute joints and a two-axis moving base supplies the required agility. Nine conventional cameras track the ball, while three event-based gaze cameras support spin estimation by observing the logo on a standard ball. Event cameras report brightness changes asynchronously, providing information suited to fast motion.

Ace combines that sensing with a physics-informed simulator incorporating measurement noise, bounce variation, and aerodynamic effects. A reinforcement-learning policy chooses a shot-level target. A feasibility mapping converts it into a reachable configuration, and a model predictive controller executes the motion. An evolutionary process supplies varied serves, broadening the situations the policy encounters.

Sony's [follow-up on professional matches](https://ai.sony/archive/what-happened-after-the-nature-paper-ace-vs.-professional-players), published in June 2026, reports victories during February–April against players whose historical world rankings included fifth and ninety-ninth. These later matches should be distinguished from the earlier experiments in the Nature paper. The achievement demonstrates demanding perception and control, with a specific scope: specialized hardware and learned motor skill. Game-level tactics and shot-selection strategy remain separate questions.

### Privileged training information and executable targets

Simulation provides exact state information unavailable to physical sensors. An asymmetric actor-critic architecture uses that advantage during training. The critic receives true ball position, velocity, and spin and learns to estimate future return. The actor receives the noisy observations available to the deployed robot. Comparing observed returns with the critic's prediction supplies an advantage signal for policy learning.

A PPO-style clipped objective limits the incentive for large policy changes. The critic is discarded at deployment, so the robot does not require privileged state during play. This creates a more informative training signal without eliminating partial observability or guaranteeing transfer to every real condition.

The action representation matters as much as the optimizer. Sampling arbitrary end-effector targets wastes experience on unreachable configurations. Ace maps a bounded policy action into its represented feasible workspace. That establishes a useful constraint on the policy's choices; claims about complete coverage, uniqueness, or smoothness require details of the mapping's construction.

A reachable pose still needs a dynamically executable path. Model predictive control balances target tracking against control effort over a finite horizon, subject to robot dynamics and feasibility constraints. The controller repeatedly replans and executes the first optimized control. Choosing a useful strike and tracking it can therefore operate at different rates.

One demonstration involved changing a planned forehand into a backhand after the ball touched the net. The example shows why observation must continue after a motion begins. A good initial prediction does not remove the need to respond to new evidence.

## Representations must preserve what changes, touches, and moves

A controller can act only on distinctions its perception system preserves. Recognizing an object category may be insufficient when success depends on a small visual change, the onset of slip, or an orientation that determines whether a key fits a lock. Stateful visual encoders, tactile-reactive policies, and pose-based motion models address these requirements at different levels.

### Compare images before their differences disappear

An encoder processing each frame independently may produce similar representations while missing an operationally decisive difference. A checked box, shifted tile, or slightly moved object can leave the scene's high-level meaning unchanged.

Stateful Visual Encoders introduce cross-image interaction inside the visual encoder. Features from the current image can attend to features from a previous image before the language model receives them. Queries from one image and keys and values from another provide a mechanism for comparison within the representation itself.

Training determines whether that mechanism learns useful change detection; attention weights alone are not a complete map of differences. The applications include robotic state tracking, web-interface verification, image-editing checks, medical comparison, and geospatial monitoring. Each asks what changed between observations, rather than merely what either observation contains.

### Contact needs a faster response

Vision leaves gaps when contact matters. A grasp may look stable immediately before an object slips. Separating stacked cups, turning a key, controlling a pipette, or inserting a light bulb requires information about forces that an image may not reveal.

[T-Rex](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17055) combines cameras, dexterous hands, and tactile sensing. Its architecture separates rapid contact adjustments from slower visuomotor planning. Demonstrations included toothpaste squeezing, cup separation, texture-based Mahjong-tile identification, card extraction, key manipulation, pipetting, light-bulb insertion, and handling an uncooked egg.

The design principle is to match response timescale to signal. Grip corrections need a fast feedback path; selecting the next object can tolerate slower deliberation. The detailed coupling between these functions still matters, and scaling tactile skills requires physical interaction data that is expensive to collect.

### Predict geometry directly

World Motion Models represent motion through trajectories of rigid-body poses. A pose belongs to the special Euclidean group $SE(3)$:

```math
T = \begin{pmatrix} R & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in SE(3), \quad R \in SO(3) \subset \mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}, \quad t \in \mathbb{R}^3
```

The homogeneous transform combines rotation $R$ and translation $t$. A valid rotation satisfies $R^T R = I$ and $\det(R)=1$. Composition is generally noncommutative: rotating and translating in one order need not give the same result as reversing them.

Sparse pose trajectories provide a compact description of where objects move and how they rotate. The method uses flow matching with per-token noise levels and supports flexible conditioning on observed entities and times. Different conditioning patterns support future prediction, missing-motion completion, planning, inverse kinematics, and retargeting.

This representation requires geometric care. Arbitrary noise added directly to a rotation matrix can destroy its validity. Predictions can inform model predictive control only if accuracy and latency meet the task's needs. Contact, deformation, force, and uncertainty may require state beyond rigid poses, even when pose trajectories supply an efficient starting point.

## Sidewalk videos become interactive training environments

Coco Robotics' sidewalk-autonomy work connects scene representation to policy training under practical constraints: pedestrian infrastructure, limited onboard compute, and monocular perception. Three related projects address different parts of the problem. Vid2Sim reconstructs locations from video, UrbanVerse expands environmental variety, and FlowPilot learns navigation behavior.

Vid2Sim estimates camera poses from monocular video and fits a Gaussian-splat representation. Spatial primitives carry color, opacity, and extent, supplying photorealistic views of the captured location. Collision geometry and a physics engine make the reconstruction interactive. Rendering and physical behavior require distinct representations; a convincing image does not automatically provide a surface a robot can safely traverse.

A reconstruction supplies a digital twin of one location. UrbanVerse uses video-derived scene layouts and a library of roughly 100,000 annotated urban assets to construct varied environments. These digital cousins retain meaningful scene relationships while changing objects and arrangements. Asset count matters only alongside appropriate geometry, scale, physical properties, and diversity.

### Learn ordinary movement and interactive behavior

Demonstrations provide a starting policy through behavior cloning:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{BC}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(o_t, a_t) \sim \mathcal{D}}\!\left[-\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid o_t)\right]
$$

The dataset $\mathcal{D}$ pairs observations $o_t$ with expert actions $a_t$. The objective increases the probability that policy $\pi_\theta$ chooses a demonstrated action. It teaches ordinary navigation efficiently, while leaving the distribution-shift problem that appears when the learner makes mistakes.

Interactive feedback can address progress, collisions, and behavior around pedestrians. Social behavior is especially difficult to specify: distance, speed, and abruptness are useful proxies, but cannot capture every expectation about shared space. A reward that promotes fast arrival can produce undesirable movement if the remaining terms are incomplete.

[FlowPilot](https://vail.cs.ucla.edu/FlowPilot/) uses imitation followed by reward-free human-preference alignment. Brief corrective interventions guide the policy toward socially compliant behavior while preserving its imitation prior. This supplies a concrete way to incorporate human judgment about navigation behavior without specifying a scalar reward for every social interaction.

### Transfer remains an empirical question

Reconstruction reduces appearance mismatch, but does not establish equality between simulated and real observation distributions. Lighting, camera differences, reconstruction errors, and unfamiliar viewpoints remain. A static background also does not supply realistic pedestrians or animals. Zero-shot transfer is an empirical result for particular conditions, rather than a guarantee implied by Gaussian splatting.

A waypoint interface provides another abstraction:

$$
u_t = \mu_\phi(a_t, q_t), \quad a_t = \pi_\theta(o_t)
$$

The shared policy chooses waypoint $a_t$, while controller $\mu_\phi$ converts it and robot state $q_t$ into motor command $u_t$. A planar waypoint can be two-dimensional, with other applications using three-dimensional targets.

The high-level policy was demonstrated across a wheeled delivery robot, quadruped, and electric wheelchair without policy fine-tuning. Each platform still needs a suitable controller. Clearance, traversability, stopping distance, and reachable paths determine how much behavior the abstraction can actually share.

## Research claims must answer to experiments

Agentic research systems extend experimentation to proposing changes, implementing them, comparing outcomes, and deciding what to investigate next. NVIDIA's discovery demonstration centers on a verifiable substrate: executable code, real datasets, hardware runs, and evaluation procedures. Its example concerned improving 3D Gaussian splatting for pedestrians and thin structures in driving scenes, using OmniRe as a reconstruction baseline.

Task-defining agents establish the problem and comparisons. Other agents propose hypotheses, implement experiments, evaluate results, write explanations, and review claims. Their value depends on the connection between a proposed idea and an experiment that actually ran.

### Constrain proposals and compare outcomes carefully

A context-free grammar can define allowable combinations of architectures, losses, and processing stages. This reduces syntactically invalid proposals, but runnable experiments also require compatible types, dimensions, resources, and data assumptions. Scientific value is another filter: executable code can still test an uninformative hypothesis.

The demonstrated system used pairwise tournaments with Elo-style ratings. Ratings change according to the difference between actual and expected outcomes, so an underdog's victory produces a larger update than an expected win. This summarizes incomplete pairwise comparisons, but does not automatically identify a universally best method. Noisy measurements, different task mixtures, and conflicting metrics complicate interpretation. Repeated runs and clear benchmark definitions remain necessary.

### Preserve the inference from evidence to claim

A claim should be traceable to GPU logs, code revisions, and benchmark outputs. Those artifacts let a reviewer check the data, baseline, execution conditions, and numerical result. Traceability makes verification possible; experimental design determines whether the written conclusion follows.

NVIDIA reported submitting four papers to a CVPR 2026 workshop, with three accepted, and attributed the remaining rejection to equation-formatting problems. The sample and workshop setting limit the conclusion about autonomous scientific quality. The more general contribution is an inspectable record of work, rather than paper production alone.

Those records can also support human collaboration through attribution and retained expertise. However, a sequence of actions does not automatically explain intent. Turning execution evidence into reusable understanding requires interpretation, including expert judgment about why a particular result matters.

## A Tetris experiment reveals the value of diagnosis

[The Lab](https://lambda.ai/blog/what-happens-when-claude-code-gets-an-experiment-tracker) supplies structured experiment records, Git-based organization of ideas, sandbox isolation, and an API through which research agents can inspect earlier work. Lambda's demonstration asked Claude Code to improve Gemma's performance in a Tetris-like game without changing its weights. The published account reports 468 experiments over roughly two and a half days, a peak score rising from zero to sixteen, and about \$1,200 in orchestration API cost.

The result is modest as game performance but informative as experimental diagnosis. The agent initially pursued inference speed under a 30-minute timeout. Games instead ended after roughly 20 seconds because the board filled. Placement quality was the binding constraint. Speculative decoding, image input, removing rotation, and ambitious multi-row strategies did not solve it.

### Fit strategy and reasoning to demonstrated capability

The experimental game's scoring favored simultaneous line clears: one line earned one point, two earned three, three earned five, and four earned eight. Yet Gemma did not reliably execute the setup for ambitious clears. Keeping placements low and avoiding holes performed better. A plan's nominal payoff is useful only when the model can carry it out consistently.

Prompt changes supplied the major improvement. Brief, task-specific checks directed attention toward target position, orientation, holes, and the required moves. The reasoning and action had to fit within the available output:

$$
|\text{thinking}| + |\text{action}| \leq B, \quad |\text{thinking}| \geq T_\text{min}
$$

Here, $B$ is the per-turn token budget and $T_\text{min}$ denotes a useful minimum reasoning allocation. Too little checking misses mistakes; excessive deliberation consumes the space needed to act or distracts from a reliable procedure. These experiments demonstrate gains from prompt and inference changes in their setting, without establishing superiority to a trained policy under a matched budget.

### Protect the evaluator and compare repeated runs

The Lab protects the game engine, client, and scoring harness while allowing experimental changes. Otherwise, the orchestrating agent could raise the score by rewriting the game or bypassing Gemma. Access restrictions must be enforced by the actual environment, beyond comments describing intended permissions.

A maximum score can identify a promising idea while still reflecting luck. Mean or median performance across repeated runs, together with variability, provides stronger evidence. Structured records let later sessions inspect those comparisons and avoid repeating failed ideas. Experiment count and cost also matter: a useful research assistant should improve the quality or economics of investigation.

## Security testing measures behavior under adversarial input

Prompt injection places instructions inside data the agent is supposed to inspect. Consider an expense-review task that rejects requests above \$5,000. A document asking approval for \$12,000 might assert that a vice president has already authorized it and supply an invented approval code. That assertion is part of the untrusted document; it cannot establish the authority needed to override the task.

Lambda's [AgentBeats Security Arena analysis](https://lambda.ai/blog/prompt-injection-doesnt-care-what-your-agent-does-for-a-living) describes 22 teams building attackers and defenders on gpt-oss-20b. Roughly 94,000 public battles were followed by 17,585 private battles. A judge checked whether an unauthorized outcome appeared in the defender's structured response, rather than merely whether the attack sounded persuasive.

### Calibrate the scenario before trusting the score

Informative scenarios need balanced difficulty, sensitivity to attacker capability, and resistance to trivial guessing. Comparing a stronger attacker against a weaker defender, and the reverse, tests whether the scenario distinguishes skill. A roughly balanced contest can aid comparison; a 50% attack success rate is not an acceptable target for a deployed service.

A hidden salary recoverable through repeated higher-or-lower questions can measure persistence through a permissive interface instead of injection resistance. Random canary strings help make leakage identifiable while reducing trivial search strategies. What counts as a win must remain connected to the intended security property.

Held-out scenarios then reveal whether a strategy learned a general mechanism or memorized public examples. Scenario-specific routing can perform well publicly and fail when unfamiliar tasks appear.

### Test mechanisms and nearby variants

The arena identified ten attack-mechanism clusters. Transferable patterns included direct requests for protected information, claims that earlier findings were mistaken, fake pre-approval, and impersonation of a trusted process. Similar patterns appeared in personnel, expenses, inventory, and email scenarios. Defenses based on a few sensitive nouns miss authority confusion expressed in another domain.

Small edits also changed outcomes. The updated analysis reports that roughly 9% of winning attacks had a near-identical failing counterpart. Changes in attribution, phrasing, or formatting could separate success from failure. A paired outcome does not establish true success probabilities of zero and one; repeated trials are required.

Failed attacks therefore deserve nearby variants in evaluation. Mechanism coverage matters alongside domain coverage. Sustained red-teaming reportedly improved defenses, but one competition's shared base model limits generalization. Detectors and output validation need retesting as attacks change, and consequential actions require authorization independent of claims inside the content being processed.

## Traces connect system design with model training

A harness defines tool interfaces, prompts, context management, execution order, error handling, and stopping conditions. Lambda's reported coding comparisons illustrated their interaction: in one Kimi K3 comparison, Kimi Code completed 21 of 26 tasks, Hermes Agent roughly 19–20, and Claude Code about 19. The Hermes configuration was reported cheaper and faster than the native configuration, while the Claude Code configuration cost substantially more. Other comparisons paired Gemini 3 Pro and GPT-5.5 with different harnesses.

These are observations about specific configurations, not intrinsic rankings of the products. Tool compatibility, prompt size, task mix, and context pressure affect interpretation. A harness can help one model while consuming useful context or imposing unsuitable conventions on another. Model and harness should be evaluated together under a relevant task and cost budget.

### Learn actions from complete trajectories

A trace records the initial task, agent actions, tool observations, and outcome. Lambda described generating coding scenarios with Kimi K2.5 and collecting Kimi and GLM 5.1 trajectories through Hermes Agent, separating successful from failed runs for training and release.

Such records teach behavior a final answer omits: tool choice, work organization, recovery, and recognition of completion. Community models such as the Qwen-and-Opus combination called Qwopus illustrate the broader interest in transferring teacher behavior into smaller models, without making every dataset or teacher pairing equivalent.

In supervised trace training, observations remain in context while the action-generation loss applies to agent-produced tokens. Token positions and action steps must be indexed consistently. Generating the environment's responses would be a different modeling task.

Outcome labels still matter. Undifferentiated imitation of failed traces can teach mistakes; selected failures can instead support preferences, filtering, or recovery examples. Successful traces also need checks that their apparent success reflects the intended task. Reported dense-model alternatives to mixture-of-experts students do not establish a universal architecture or parameter threshold.

### Learn when concurrency is useful

<details><summary>Independent tool calls can run together instead of paying their combined waiting time and repeated model turns:</summary>

```python
results = await asyncio.gather(
    create_file("main.py", "from fastapi import FastAPI..."),
    write_tests("test_main.py", ...),
    init_git("repo/")
)
```

</details>

This assumes imported `asyncio`, asynchronous helpers, and an enclosing asynchronous function. Test generation must already know the interface, and concurrent writes must not conflict. Actual timing also includes planning, synthesis, scheduling overhead, and resource contention. The transferable skill is recognizing dependencies; a similar scheduling pattern alone does not establish equal quality or speed on complete tasks.

Learning successful tool use is only one part of deploying an agent. The surrounding platform must also supply current knowledge, preserve control over execution, retain state while tools run, and carry requests across system boundaries. These requirements connect the behavior recorded in a trajectory with the infrastructure that makes the next trajectory possible. They also determine whether a capability demonstrated in an experiment remains useful in everyday operation.

## Give agents relationships they can inspect

Enterprise questions often concern relationships. A career assistant must connect skills to openings, exclude the employee's current role, and consider organizational distance. A transport assistant must identify municipal rules governing a particular vehicle and service. Both need current business context.

Pretrained weights do not contain all of an organization's private, changing information. Fine-tuning teaches behavior without supplying a live data store or request-specific permissions. Relational databases hold much of the information, although relationship traversal can require complex queries. Vector retrieval finds semantically similar passages but does not explicitly represent every dependency in a question spanning several relationships.

An **enterprise knowledge layer** sits between these data systems and agent applications. Its semantic layer maps business concepts to records and storage locations. A graph represents entities, their properties, and their relationships. Rules can also be represented as connected structures, supplying procedural memory that an agent can consult before acting. Neo4j's [enterprise knowledge-layer proposal](https://neo4j.com/blog/agentic-ai/enterprise-knowledge-layer/) describes a shared structure connecting enterprise meaning, data, and memory.

Neo4j uses property graphs, whose nodes and directed edges can both carry key-value properties. GQL, standardized as ISO/IEC 39075, supplies a query-language standard for this model. The architectural idea is neurosymbolic: a language model interprets and explains, while explicit structures supply relationships and constraints people can inspect.

Neo4j's enterprise examples illustrate distinct uses. Walmart's career-navigation example connects skills, reporting structures, past roles, and job openings across a workforce described as 1.6 million employees. Uber's rules graph concerns transport offerings and regulatory variation across roughly 15,000 municipalities. A gaming-enterprise example uses a graph semantic layer to direct conversational requests toward appropriate distributed data systems. The graph supplies a map of the enterprise as well as facts about it.

### Combine semantic relevance with structural relevance

GraphRAG combines retrieval-augmented generation with explicit relationships. Vector search can identify promising starting entities, while graph traversal retrieves connected facts that semantic similarity misses. A regulatory provision may share little vocabulary with a question yet remain strongly connected to the relevant vehicle class.

Hybrid ranking can combine embedding similarity with graph distance and Personalized PageRank, balancing semantic proximity with relationship structure. Their numerical ranges need suitable scaling before weights express the intended balance. This is a possible design rather than a mandatory GraphRAG algorithm.

### Bound the search and preserve its evidence

Direct adjacency makes individual steps efficient, but reachable paths grow rapidly with branching factor and depth. Even shallow searches need selective predicates around highly connected nodes. Relational comparisons depend on indexes, joins, intermediate-result sizes, and data layout. Graphs simplify expression of relationship-heavy questions; the workload still determines performance.

A career recommendation, for example, can identify the employee's skills, connect them to job requirements, filter out the current role, and rank remaining positions by skill overlap and organizational distance. Returning matching skills and an explicit relationship path gives a person evidence to assess. It can reduce unsupported generation without preventing a model from misinterpreting what the graph returned.

Construction and permissions remain substantial engineering work. Automated extraction can introduce false relationships or merge distinct entities. Hiding a property does not necessarily conceal a sensitive relationship when traversal results expose it indirectly. A virtual graph over distributed storage inherits network and translation costs. Trust depends on accurate, complete, current relationships and access-aware retrieval throughout the answer.

## Keep execution under the developer's control

Imbue's idea of **punk software** argues for systems individuals can own, inspect, modify, and run with fewer dependencies on one provider. The concerns include model retirement, account access, opaque behavior changes, and vendors entering application markets served by their partners. Limited access to log-probabilities or intermediate traces also restricts debugging and training.

The proposal emphasizes individual ownership, decentralization, openness, resistance to monopoly, and practical human benefit. Its engineering expression is a modular stack. Linux, Docker, SSH, and local hardware provide execution foundations. vLLM and SGLang serve models; open-weight families such as DeepSeek, GLM, Nemotron, and Inkling offer model choices. Harnesses such as OpenCode, Cline, Codex, and Goose manage agent behavior. Tools including tmux, cmux, herder, and `mngr` coordinate processes, while personal applications use the resulting capabilities.

Portability requires components that can actually be exchanged. Interfaces, stored state, supported tools, and operational controls affect the cost of moving work.

### Treat agents as inspectable processes

Imbue's [mngr](https://imbue.com/blog/mngr) supplies a common interface for managing agents across harnesses and compute backends. An agent can receive work asynchronously, retain a transcript, and remain available for inspection or an interactive connection. These controls make background execution accessible through ordinary development tools instead of tying every task to a foreground chat session.

Connecting every harness directly to every compute provider requires an integration for each pair. A common interface lets each implement its side independently, reducing adapter work when the abstraction preserves needed capabilities. Backend-specific features can still require additional integration.

Headless processes need timeouts, resource budgets, observable progress, and clear completion signals. A failed edit-test cycle can otherwise consume resources after useful progress ends. Transcripts and reconnectable sessions help distinguish productive investigations from agents needing intervention.

The related **Minds** idea places a model beside its generated applications inside a local virtual machine. A personal dashboard or automation script can run near the model maintaining it, reducing the deployment machinery needed for small tools.

### Make idle capacity reclaimable

The `mngr donate` proposal extends orchestration to scientific work. During idle periods, a machine would accept queued research tasks and execute them in isolation using otherwise unused model capacity.

Nominal surplus and usable surplus differ. Preemption, rate limits, uncertain future demand, and the duration of idle intervals determine how much background work can run. Subscription capacity is also not a guaranteed transferable API balance. Provider permissions must cover the proposed pooling or third-party use; access to an account alone does not establish that authorization.

A remote task queue introduces another trust boundary. Standard containers share the host kernel; microVMs and sandboxed runtimes address different exposures. Isolation must suit the workload and preserve the owner's ability to reclaim resources, inspect execution, and stop work.

## Serve the rhythm of agent work

An agent's interaction with a model is intermittent. It generates an action, waits for a tool, and resumes with the result. Its history may reach hundreds of thousands of tokens while much of the session spends time waiting outside the accelerator. Large mixture-of-experts models, contexts approaching one million tokens, and demand across diverse accelerators make this a distinct serving problem.

The [vLLM and AgentX discussion of agentic serving](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx) addresses these pressures through scheduling, parallelism, memory management, and state reuse. DeepSeek V4 and Kimi K3 illustrate architecture-specific deployment choices.

**Prefill** processes the input prompt and can exploit large matrix operations. **Decode** generates subsequent tokens and often places greater pressure on memory bandwidth. Allocating dedicated resources to these stages can improve utilization, but introduces state-transfer and scheduling costs. The result depends on the workload as well as peak hardware capability.

### Match parallelism to the model and hardware

Tensor parallelism divides operations across devices; pipeline parallelism divides model stages. Sequence and context parallelism distribute work associated with long sequences, while expert parallelism distributes a mixture-of-experts model's expert networks. These choices interact with matrix dimensions, interconnect topology, routing, and the balance between prefill and decoding.

The vLLM reported a DeepSeek V4 Pro example on B200 hardware that compared eight-way tensor parallelism with a sixteen-GPU configuration combining two pipeline stages, eight-way tensor parallelism, sequence parallelism, and expert parallelism. It reported lower time to first token and higher throughput per GPU. Chunking enables stage overlap, while expert placement changes matrix-operation efficiency.

Communication, pipeline imbalance, and routing overhead can consume gains from further division. More parallelism does not automatically produce faster service. Pipeline parallelism is especially useful for long, fresh prefills; short turns with warm prefix caches can instead suffer from pipeline bubbles. The appropriate configuration depends on the balance between these request types.

### Account for different kinds of persistent state

A key-value cache retains attention state from earlier tokens. Full-attention layers require storage that grows with context length, while recurrent or linear-attention layers can maintain a state whose size is fixed with respect to that length. A hybrid model combines both requirements:

$$
\mathcal{M}_\text{hybrid}(L) = \mathcal{M}_\text{full}(L) + \mathcal{M}_\text{linear} = B \cdot P \left[ 2 L \cdot N_\text{full} \cdot H_\text{kv} \cdot D + N_\text{linear} \cdot S_\text{state} \right]
$$

Here, $B$ is batch size, $L$ is context length, and $P$ is bytes per element. The full-attention component has $N_\text{full}$ layers, $H_\text{kv}$ key-value heads, and head dimension $D$; the factor of two accounts for keys and values. The recurrent component has $N_\text{linear}$ layers and $S_\text{state}$ stored elements per layer.

Precision and sharding affect allocation. Recurrent state can scale quadratically with an internal dimension while remaining constant in context length. Weights and temporary allocations fall outside this expression.

Static reservations can leave one kind of state with unused capacity while another exhausts its pool. A unified allocator coordinates token blocks and recurrent states, responding to the active mix of context lengths and batch sizes. Hybrid memory management must also respect the different conditions under which each layer's state can be reused.

### Schedule long inputs and move inactive state

Chunked prefill divides large prompts into scheduling units so they do not monopolize resources. Pipeline stages can overlap work on successive chunks while active decoding avoids waiting behind the entire input. Computation, communication, and pipeline fill time all affect latency.

Tool waits permit moving inactive caches from accelerator high-bandwidth memory to host DRAM or distributed storage. Transfer must succeed before accelerator memory is released. Restoration requires compatible state, synchronization, and handling of transfer failures.

Avoiding prefill recomputation does not eliminate transfer latency. Moving a large cache over PCIe or a network may take longer than the tool call leaves available. Offloading decisions must therefore consider expected idle duration, bandwidth, available capacity, and recomputation cost.

The vLLM team described a plugin-based approach spanning more than ten hardware backends, with differences in kernels, low-precision formats, and speculative decoding. Supporting an accelerator and delivering equivalent feature performance are separate milestones affecting workload portability.

## Reuse context, then make learning agree with serving

RadixArk's SGLang serves models and generates trajectories; Miles uses those trajectories for reinforcement-learning post-training. Inference correctness becomes part of learning. The SGLang ecosystem includes a core language- and vision-language-model engine, a Diffusion engine for image, video, and vision-language-action workloads, and an Omni engine for speech recognition and synthesis.

### Reuse the longest valid prefix

Agent requests often share system instructions, tool descriptions, and conversation history. A radix tree stores token segments so related requests can identify the longest cached prefix and reuse its attention state. Repeated tool-use turns can then process the new suffix without repeating the entire prefill.

Matched tokens must correspond to valid reusable cache blocks; a partial branch match cannot justify reusing its entire state. Production systems also need eviction, memory management, metadata, and coordination between simultaneous accesses.

In full attention, new tokens still attend to cached earlier tokens. Caching therefore does not reduce all work to a function of suffix length alone. Savings depend on both context lengths, prefix overlap, attention design, and state availability.

SGLang's unified hybrid radix cache supports recurrent and sliding-window structures alongside other attention designs. HiCache extends state storage across HBM, host DRAM, and remote tiers. HiSparse combines a full cache with a smaller active hot buffer. Chunked pipeline execution addresses long prefills, while reported speculative decoding support included EAGLE, multi-token prediction, DFlash, and Spec V2.

Prefill/decode disaggregation separates stages with different demands. Encoder-prefill-decode disaggregation allows image or audio encoders to be provisioned independently. The SGLang and Miles infrastructure talk reported coding results with sub-second time to first token and throughput up to 500 tokens per second. These figures require the model, hardware, concurrency, cache-hit conditions, and per-request versus aggregate measurement to support a meaningful comparison. Diverging conversations also increase bookkeeping and eviction costs.

### Matching weights is not enough to match policies

A rollout engine samples actions; a training engine evaluates their probabilities and updates weights. Identical parameters can still produce different distributions through kernels, precision, tokenization, chat templates, or expert routing. A compact mismatch measure is:

$$
\Delta_\text{drift} = \log \pi_\theta^\text{train}(a_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_\theta^\text{inf}(a_t \mid s_t) \neq 0
$$

Here, $a_t$ is the action at state $s_t$, and weights $\theta$ are fixed across inference and training engines. A policy-update ratio can consequently contain implementation differences alongside the intended effect of changed weights, distorting the learning signal.

Miles builds on Slime, integrates SGLang rollouts with training backends such as Megatron, and addresses this boundary through numerical alignment and deterministic kernels. Token-In-Token-Out addresses preservation of the sequences consumed and produced during interaction. Detokenization, retokenization, or chat-template changes can alter those sequences even when the visible conversation appears similar.

Harbor, Daytona, and Prime Intellect Verifiers connect execution to training feedback. Trajectories must retain the necessary observations and probability information. Determinism supports reproducibility, but agreement between engines also requires aligned computations, token sequences, and numerical representations.

### Distinguish routing control, clipping, and staleness

Mixture-of-experts routing selects which expert networks process each token. Changed routes complicate comparisons between sampled and updated policies. Routing replay preserves selected routes; masking can instead exclude contributions from mismatched routes. These are related but distinct mechanisms.

Importance-ratio clipping limits the effect of unusually large ratios, while a routing mask can suppress tokens whose routes differ. Both change the estimator. They can improve stability under a chosen training design without proving that every effect of distribution shift has disappeared.

Miles also pursues asynchronous learning and low-precision rollout generation. Asynchrony keeps rollout and training resources busy independently, but experience may arrive from older policy versions. A large gap between the generating policy and the current policy makes that experience less representative and complicates probability accounting.

FP8 or FP4 rollouts introduce another tradeoff when training uses higher precision. Matching nominal weights does not make different numerical representations identical. Quantization-aware training can reduce the discrepancy; deterministic kernels alone cannot remove it. Stable improvement per unit of computation is therefore a more useful measure than rollout volume considered by itself.

## Exchange intent across agent boundaries

Agents built with different frameworks and internal representations need a way to exchange meaningful requests. Pairwise adapters recreate the integration problem seen between harnesses and compute backends. The **Natural Language Interaction Protocol**, standardized as ECMA-430, provides an application-level protocol for communication between agents or between a human and an agent.

Capable endpoints translate between their internal representations and a common envelope, requesting clarification when needed. Typed interfaces can also bridge representations. The choice concerns how much translation is specified beforehand and how much is delegated to endpoint interpretation.

### Keep the envelope small without assuming shared meaning

The standard requires `format`, `subformat`, and `content`, with optional `MessageType` and `Submessages` fields. Field names are case-insensitive; additional submessages form an ordered array.

<details><summary>A minimal request can carry domain-specific content:</summary>

```json
{
  "format": "structured",
  "subformat": "json",
  "content": {
    "intent": "recommend_channels",
    "query": "How do I optimize CUDA shared memory bank conflicts in Blackwell B200?",
    "confidence_threshold": 0.85
  }
}
```

</details>

A requested confidence threshold does not establish calibrated confidence. The receiver must still understand the task and identify uncertainty about what counts as a satisfactory result.

Companion bindings support HTTP/HTTPS, WebSocket, and AMQP. The NLIP proposal describes organizational gateways translating internal protocols and direct federation between agents built with AutoGen, CrewAI, or AG2. These approaches place translation at different boundaries while retaining a shared exchange format.

### Measure the completed interaction

A small outer envelope can reduce mechanical overhead, but field counts do not establish runtime ratios against MCP, A2A, or enterprise interfaces. A specification can define many optional fields while an actual message uses few. Conversely, a small wrapper can contain a large, deeply nested payload. Parsing and validation depend on bytes, structure, rules, and implementation.

The broader latency budget is:

$$
T_\text{total} = T_\text{creation} + T_\text{connection} + T_\text{transport} + T_\text{inference}
$$

Message construction and validation contribute $T_\text{creation}$; connection establishment contributes $T_\text{connection}$; transmission contributes $T_\text{transport}$; and model processing contributes $T_\text{inference}$. Additional clarification turns can repeat parts of that cost.

The NLIP customer-support evaluation involved voice input, NVIDIA speech recognition, a channel recommender, and a Reddit-search agent. It reported lower creation, connection, and transport overhead than the compared A2A variants. End-to-end benefit still depends on semantic interpretation, model work, and clarification. Persistent connections can reduce connection costs, while inference can dominate the completed exchange.

Endpoint capability also matters. Small or heavily quantized models may interpret the same request differently, and clarification can become an unproductive loop without turn limits and explicit failure conditions. Flexible dialogue can establish intent, while precise, auditable actions require validation against application rules. Transporting a request, understanding it, and authorizing it remain separate responsibilities. A useful exchange ends with an outcome the other side can verify and an action the receiver is permitted to execute.

## Education shifts toward building and defending work

Replit's education and application-building workshop emphasizes problem framing, evidence inspection, and explanation as agentic development lowers the barrier to building software. A natural-language request can initiate interface construction, backend logic, database provisioning, authentication, and debugging. Different modes balance speed, cost, and reasoning effort, while shared workspaces support collaboration.

The opportunity is experiential: learners can build something, encounter a failure, investigate it, and revise the design. The assessment challenge is distinguishing ownership and understanding from the ability to obtain a plausible artifact.

### Assess the artifact and its creator's understanding

A portfolio can demonstrate project value alongside evidence of the creator's decisions. Conventional credentials provide other signals, but neither a credential nor a generated project alone establishes independent capability. Learners should explain why they chose a design, what supports it, where it fails, and how a new requirement would change it.

Oral defense, reflective records, and collaborative review can make that understanding visible, although scaling such assessment is difficult. For a multi-user photo wall, for example, a learner should explain authentication, input validation, per-user permissions, and persistence even if an agent writes the implementation. Generating a working interface does not settle those design questions.

### Make continued learning part of maintaining capability

Changing tools and skill requirements motivate continuing education, without establishing a universal mathematical half-life for knowledge. Replit's proposal connects broad access to AI tools with evidence-based assessment, stronger recognition of teaching and mentorship, lifelong learning opportunities, and outcomes measured through capability and economic mobility.

Its literacy framework combines learning with AI as a tutor, learning about AI as a technical and governed system, and learning beyond AI by applying those capabilities to substantive problems. Implementation requires attention to funding, accreditation, hiring, and teaching workloads. The practical aim is to let people build useful things while developing the judgment to evaluate what they have built.

## Turning capability into sustained action

The shared constraint across these research directions is the supply of useful experience. A stronger model needs tasks that reveal what it still cannot do. A better harness needs interactions that expose its weak points. A world model needs evaluation that distinguishes visual realism from faithful consequences. A reinforcement-learning system needs rewards that measure the intended work.

Much of an agent platform's behavior is determined at the boundaries between components. Graph retrieval turns organizational data into context. An execution interface turns a task into a managed process. An inference scheduler preserves state across pauses. A learning system turns trajectories into policy updates. A communication protocol lets one agent request work from another. Each boundary must preserve something essential: evidence, control, state, probability, or meaning.

Better reasoning makes more tasks reachable; faster generation makes more attempts affordable; richer environments reveal more kinds of failure; better verification makes those failures informative. The practical challenge is to connect these improvements so that experience produces behavior that people can inspect, reproduce, and improve. That is how model capability becomes sustained, useful action.
