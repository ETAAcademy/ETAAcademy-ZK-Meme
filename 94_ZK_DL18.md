# Building Verifiable AI Agents: From Next-Token Prediction to Evaluation and Rack-Scale Systems

AI agents sit at the intersection of model training, software systems, and measurable outcomes. A foundation model can predict and generate language, but an agent must turn that ability into a sequence of actions that works in a changing environment. It must use tools precisely, recover from failure, respect constraints, and leave behind evidence that the requested task was actually completed.

That requirement connects questions that are often discussed separately. Pretraining determines what a model knows; post-training shapes how it behaves; application and infrastructure design determine what it can do economically and reliably; evaluation defines what counts as progress; and verifiers turn real outcomes into learning signals. A weakness at any one layer can invalidate gains elsewhere:

1. how large language models acquire broad capabilities and become useful assistants;
2. how agent benchmarks establish validity, reliability, and real task success;
3. how applications, retrieval, infrastructure, and hardware turn model capability into usable systems; and
4. how environment data, verifiers, evaluation, and reinforcement learning form a practical training loop for verifiable agents.

The central thesis is simple: a reliable agent is not only a capable model. It is a model embedded in a system whose tools, environments, feedback, and operational limits all support the same objective.

## Conclusion

Reliable AI agents emerge when the entire stack is designed around verifiable outcomes. Pretraining supplies knowledge and generalization. Supervised fine-tuning teaches useful interaction patterns. Reinforcement learning improves behavior through feedback. Applications, retrieval systems, and infrastructure give the model current information, practical tools, and an economically sustainable operating environment. Evaluation determines whether progress survives new tasks, interfaces, and failure modes.

The unifying design principle is alignment between layers. Tasks need explicit contracts; tools need precise schemas; sandboxes need faithful state transitions; verifiers need to check correctness, policy compliance, side effects, and cost; benchmarks need continuous auditing and renewal; and training needs enough diversity to preserve exploration. Optimizing one component in isolation can raise a local metric while weakening the complete system.

For builders, the most useful question is therefore not simply, “How capable is the model?” It is, “Can this model, inside this environment, use these tools to achieve the intended state safely, repeatedly, and at an acceptable cost—and can we prove it?” That is the standard that turns an impressive language model into a dependable agent.

## Part I — How Large Language Models Are Trained for AI Agents

Modern agents begin with language models, then add instruction following, reasoning, tool use, and reliable operation. These capabilities come from three broad stages: pretraining builds knowledge and language ability; reasoning-oriented reinforcement learning improves performance on verifiable problems; and classic post-training aligns behavior with instructions and preferences. Prompting or domain fine-tuning can then specialize the model.

### The LLM Training Pipeline at a Glance

Architecture and objectives matter, but data, evaluation, and infrastructure often decide whether a promising recipe works at scale. The stages differ greatly in purpose and cost:

| Stage                            | Primary goal                                                                  |            Illustrative data scale | Illustrative duration | Main bottlenecks                            |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------: | --------------------: | ------------------------------------------- |
| Pretraining                      | Learn language, knowledge, and general patterns through next-token prediction |       More than 10 trillion tokens |                Months | Data and compute                            |
| Reasoning reinforcement learning | Improve answers on tasks with objective or verifiable outcomes                |        Around one million problems |                 Weeks | Environments, verifiers, and reward hacking |
| Classic post-training            | Follow instructions and align with human preferences                          |  Roughly 5,000 to 500,000 problems |                  Days | Data quality and evaluation                 |
| Domain fine-tuning               | Specialize a general model for a particular use case                          | Roughly 10,000 to 100,000 examples |                  Days | Data quality and evaluation                 |

These figures are orders of magnitude, not recipes. Reasoning RL and classic alignment are both forms of post-training, and their order varies: SFT often supplies the starting policy for RL, while further supervised or preference training may follow.

### Pretraining: Learning by Predicting the Next Token

Pretraining supplies the model's broad foundation. Its objective is often summarized as _predicting the next word on the internet_, although contemporary models actually predict tokens, which may represent words, subwords, punctuation, or other units.

This apparently simple task is powerful because accurate prediction across many domains requires the model to capture patterns within those domains. A model that can predict continuations of scientific writing, software, history, dialogue, and mathematics must learn useful regularities in each. Pretraining therefore aims to expose the model to as much high-quality, diverse data as possible.

#### What Happens During Next-Token Prediction

Consider the incomplete sentence “She likely prefers ...” The training and inference process can be understood in several steps:

1. **Tokenization:** The input is split into tokens, and each token is mapped to a numeric identifier.
2. **Embedding:** Each token identifier is converted into a learned vector representation.
3. **Forward pass:** The sequence of vectors passes through the neural network, usually a transformer.
4. **Vocabulary projection:** The model converts its internal representation into one score for every token in its vocabulary.
5. **Probability calculation:** A softmax turns those scores into a probability distribution for the next token.
6. **Optimization or sampling:** During training, cross-entropy loss increases the probability of the observed next token. During inference, the system selects or samples a token from the predicted distribution and converts it back into text.

#### From N-Grams to Neural Language Models

An n-gram model estimates a continuation from counts: if “green” follows “the grass is” in half of the observed cases, its estimated probability is 0.5. Such tables become enormous as context grows and assign no useful probability to unseen sequences.

Neural language models replace exact-count tables with a parameterized function. Embeddings represent related tokens in a learned vector space; a transformer combines them into a contextual state; a linear layer and softmax produce the next-token distribution. Backpropagation adjusts the entire differentiable system, allowing it to generalize statistical structure to novel text and code.

### Building a Pretraining Dataset

At frontier scale, acquiring text is only the beginning. Most raw web data is noisy, repetitive, poorly formatted, or unsuitable for training. The useful asset is not the crawl itself but the processed distribution produced by a long data pipeline.

#### From Web Crawl to Training Corpus

A typical pipeline includes the following steps:

1. **Acquire raw data.** Open-source efforts often begin with large web crawls such as Common Crawl, which contains hundreds of billions of pages and more than a petabyte of raw data.
2. **Extract meaningful text.** HTML must be separated from navigation, advertisements, scripts, boilerplate, and other page structure. JavaScript-rendered content and mathematical notation make this deceptively difficult.
3. **Remove undesirable material.** Filters may target personally identifiable information, unsafe content, or other categories that should not enter the training distribution.
4. **Deduplicate.** Repeated documents, paragraphs, lines, headers, and footers waste compute by teaching the model the same content again. Deduplication may therefore operate at several levels.
5. **Apply heuristic quality filters.** Extremely short or long documents, excessive repetition, unusual token patterns, and other outliers can indicate low-quality material.
6. **Apply model-based filters.** A classifier can learn to distinguish a trusted distribution from arbitrary web pages. For example, pages cited by Wikipedia can serve as positive examples of relatively high-quality documents, allowing the classifier to identify similar material at much larger scale.
7. **Classify and rebalance domains.** The final mixture can upweight underrepresented but valuable categories such as code, scientific text, books, or particular languages.

Extraction and normalization should precede deduplication so that superficial HTML or whitespace differences do not preserve copies. A smaller, well-filtered corpus can outperform a larger raw crawl because compute is no longer spent on repeated or low-value material. The final mixture should also reflect the target: coding models upweight code, while multilingual models may upweight languages underrepresented online. Teams can test these choices at smaller scale before a frontier run.

#### Midtraining: Refining the Distribution

After broad pretraining, many pipelines continue with a smaller phase sometimes called _midtraining_ or _continued pretraining_. This stage commonly uses less than 10 percent of the original pretraining volume and shifts the distribution toward specific desirable properties.

Midtraining can upweight code, science, languages, or reserved high-quality sources; introduce conversational or reasoning patterns; and extend the context window after cheaper short-sequence pretraining. DeepSeek-V3, for example, extended context from roughly 4,000 to 128,000 tokens in this stage.

#### The Scale of Modern Pretraining

Prominent open models grew from roughly two trillion tokens for Llama 2 to about 15 trillion for Llama 3 and DeepSeek-V3, and approximately 20–40 trillion for Llama 4. Exact mixtures are rarely public because they are strategic and may raise provenance concerns, but datasets such as C4, The Pile, Dolma, and FineWeb show the main construction techniques.

### Scaling Laws and Compute Allocation

One of the most consequential empirical findings in language modeling is that pretraining performance often follows predictable scaling laws. When test loss and compute are plotted on logarithmic axes, the best result at each compute budget frequently falls close to a line. This relationship allows researchers to run many small experiments, fit a scaling trend, and estimate how a recipe will behave at a much larger scale.

A common power-law form is

$$
L(C)=L_\infty+aC^{-\alpha},
$$

where $C$ is training compute, $L_\infty$ is the irreducible loss under the assumed data distribution, and $a,\alpha>0$ are fitted constants. After subtracting $L_\infty$, the relationship becomes linear on logarithmic axes.

For a dense transformer with $N$ parameters trained on $D$ tokens, a useful back-of-the-envelope estimate is

$$
C_{\text{train}}\approx 6ND\quad\text{FLOPs}.
$$

The constant is only approximate—the exact total depends on architecture and implementation—but the expression makes the two scaling levers explicit: parameter count and token count.

Scaling laws let teams compare recipes on smaller runs and reserve most compute for one final campaign. They also distinguish current performance from scaling rate: a method that starts behind but improves faster may be the better frontier choice.

#### The Chinchilla Tradeoff

Compute can be spent in two basic ways: use a larger model, or train a model on more tokens. The Chinchilla scaling work studied how to allocate a fixed training budget between these choices and produced the widely cited rule of roughly 20 training tokens per parameter for compute-optimal training.

Written as an order-of-magnitude rule for a model with $N$ parameters and $D$ training tokens,

$$
D\approx 20N.
$$

The rule optimizes training compute, not lifetime cost. A smaller model trained longer may cost more once but serve far more cheaply, so production decisions must include expected inference volume.

#### What a Frontier Run Requires

Llama 3 405B illustrates the magnitude of a state-of-the-art pretraining run. The model was trained on approximately 15.6 trillion tokens and contains 405 billion parameters—about 40 tokens per parameter. A rough estimate puts a run of this size at `3.8e25` floating-point operations, 16,000 H100 GPUs, around 70 days of training, and a cost on the order of USD 50–80 million.

These estimates vary with hardware contracts, utilization, failures, power, staffing, and cluster design. At this scale, a small efficiency improvement can be worth millions of dollars—a concrete version of the “bitter lesson” that scalable methods and systems tend to dominate hand-designed shortcuts.

### Post-Training: Turning a Predictor into an Assistant

A pretrained model predicts plausible continuations, which is not the same objective as helping a user. Post-training uses much less data to teach the conversational contract: follow instructions, use required formats and tools, refuse inappropriate requests, and optimize correctness or preference. Its two main methods are supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning.

### Supervised Fine-Tuning

Supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, continues next-token training on examples of desired behavior. Each example pairs an input with an answer that the model should imitate. The learning objective remains language modeling, but the distribution is now deliberately constructed around useful responses.

Given an instruction $x$, a desired response $y_{1:T}$, and a mask $m_t$ that is 1 only for response tokens, the SFT objective is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)
=-\sum_{t=1}^{T}m_t\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t}).
$$

Masking the prompt focuses the loss on learning the desired response rather than reproducing the user's text.

#### Sources of SFT Data

Desired answers come from human demonstrations, synthetic responses from a stronger teacher, or rejection sampling in which a verifier keeps the best of several candidates. Synthetic data can be cheaper and more consistent, but teacher distillation becomes difficult at the frontier when no stronger model is available.

#### What SFT Can Teach

SFT readily teaches style, formatting, tool-use patterns, and initial reasoning. Familiar behavior may need only thousands of examples; complex multi-tool interaction may need hundreds of thousands. Agent datasets can simulate interactions among a model, a user, and tools, then filter trajectories before training.

#### The Limits of Behavior Cloning

SFT remains behavior cloning: the student is bounded by its demonstrations, demonstrations use less information than comparative judgment, and polished but ungrounded examples can teach hallucination. RL instead optimizes outcomes or preferences.

### Reinforcement Learning for Reasoning and Alignment

In reinforcement learning, the central design question is: _What reward should the model maximize?_ The answer determines what behavior the training process will produce.

Rewards commonly come from rule-based verifiers, learned preference models, or LLM judges. Exact checks, tests, compilers, and formal validators suit objective tasks; writing and conversation usually require human or model-based judgments.

#### Reasoning Reinforcement Learning

Reasoning-oriented RL trains a model to solve hard problems whose results can be verified. This introduces _test-time scaling_: after training, the model can spend more computation generating and checking a longer reasoning trajectory before answering. The principle resembles human work—a thoughtful answer produced after sustained effort is often better than an immediate reaction.

A group-relative algorithm samples several responses to a problem, scores them with a verifier, normalizes rewards within the group, and increases the likelihood of stronger behavior. The same verifier can select SFT examples before RL; code must execute in an isolated sandbox.

Training often includes a penalty based on KL divergence from a reference model. This discourages the policy from moving too far from a stable base distribution while it pursues reward. Such constraints matter because a powerful optimizer will exploit weaknesses in a reward function. If a coding environment rewards passing a test, for example, a model may discover a way to modify or bypass the test rather than solve the intended problem. This is reward hacking: the system satisfies the written objective without satisfying the designer's actual goal.

At the conceptual level, the regularized RL objective is

$$
\max_\theta\;
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}[R(x,y)]
-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)
\right).
$$

The coefficient $\beta$ trades aggressive optimization against staying near the reference behavior. More training cannot compensate for an insecure environment or a verifier that rewards the wrong result.

#### Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

RLHF uses the same principle when no objective oracle exists: collect rankings of SFT-model responses, train a reward model to predict them, then optimize that reward with PPO or learn directly from comparisons with methods such as DPO. It made assistants broadly useful, but “better” remains ambiguous: annotators and LLM judges may prefer length, confidence, or polish over substance and may share the candidate model's blind spots. Clear rubrics and independent audits are essential.

### Why Agentic Reinforcement Learning Is a Systems Problem

Sampling is often agentic RL's largest bottleneck: rollouts are long, call slow tools, vary in length, and may receive reward only at the end. Systems therefore run trajectories concurrently, pause stragglers, and separate training, checkpoint distribution, and inference services. The practical goal is to keep accelerators occupied despite delayed environment feedback.

### Evaluation Is Part of the Training System

Evaluation guides training, checkpoint and prompt selection, safety decisions, and deployment. Closed-ended tasks allow exact answers or executable tests but remain sensitive to prompting and contamination. Open-ended work needs pairwise human or model judgment, which must be audited for bias. A useful suite combines objective tests, preference judgments, safety checks, and application-specific tasks. Part II develops these requirements for agents in detail.

### Systems and Infrastructure: Making Scale Usable

More compute helps only when the system can use it. GPUs are expensive, and communication can erase the benefit of adding them.

#### What Makes GPUs Different

CPUs favor flexible, low-latency work; GPUs apply similar instructions across many threads and specialize in matrix multiplication. Modern accelerators can compute faster than memory and networks deliver data, so bandwidth and communication often dominate runtime.

This is measured in part through Model FLOP Utilization, or MFU: the ratio between observed model throughput and the hardware's theoretical maximum. An MFU near 50 percent is generally considered excellent for a large training workload. Low utilization means costly processors spend too much time waiting for data or communication.

$$
MFU
=\frac{\text{observed model FLOPs per second}}
{\text{theoretical peak FLOPs per second}}.
$$

#### Low-Precision Computation

Mixed precision performs large matrix multiplications in formats such as `bf16` while retaining higher precision where stability requires it. This reduces memory and data movement, but large runs still need distributed state, checkpointing, recovery, and throughput measurement.

#### Kernel Fusion, Tiling, and Recomputation

Kernel fusion loads data once for several operations; tiling reuses cached submatrices; recomputation recalculates intermediates when doing so is cheaper than storing and loading them. FlashAttention combines these ideas to accelerate attention without changing its mathematical result.

### Fitting Large Models Across Many GPUs

A model with `P` parameters trained naively in `fp32` with Adam can require approximately `16P` bytes of device memory: `4P` for weights, `4P` for gradients, and `8P` for the optimizer's first- and second-moment states. A seven-billion-parameter model therefore needs about 112 GB before accounting for activations and other overhead. Large models must distribute both computation and state across multiple devices.

$$
M_{\text{state}}
\approx \underbrace{4P}_{\text{weights}}
+\underbrace{4P}_{\text{gradients}}
+\underbrace{8P}_{\text{Adam moments}}
=16P\ \text{bytes}.
$$

For $P=7\times10^9$, this is about 112 GB before activations and temporary buffers. Several forms of parallelism address the problem.

#### Data Parallelism

Data parallelism gives each GPU a model copy and different batch slice, then averages gradients; it increases throughput but not per-device model capacity. ZeRO-style methods instead shard optimizer states, gradients, and parameters so the model can use aggregate cluster memory.

#### Model Parallelism

Model parallelism divides computation: pipeline parallelism assigns layers to stages, while tensor parallelism splits individual matrix operations. Both fit larger models at the cost of communication and synchronization.

#### Sparsity and Mixture of Experts

A mixture-of-experts router sends each token to a subset of expert networks, increasing parameter capacity without activating the entire model. Efficient routing and communication remain difficult, but sparsity changes the relationship between total parameters and per-token compute.

### Specializing a Trained Model

Application developers can adapt a model through prompting, which is fast and requires no weight updates, or domain fine-tuning, which uses representative examples to change the model. Both need application-specific tests: without them, a change is not evidence of improvement.

## Part II — Evaluating LLM Agents: From Valid Benchmarks to Green-Agent Architecture

Part I explains how model capability is created. Part II asks whether measurements of that capability deserve to be trusted—a prerequisite for choosing training signals, comparing systems, and deciding when an agent is ready to operate.

Evaluation is the measurement system that makes progress in artificial intelligence visible. It guides optimization during training, selects checkpoints, supports safety decisions before deployment, and allows researchers to compare systems on common ground. For a conventional language model, evaluation may be as simple as mapping an input to a text response and checking that response against a label. An agent is harder to assess because it acts over time: it plans, calls tools, observes a changing environment, revises its strategy, and may succeed or fail through a long sequence of decisions.

This difference changes the central question. A useful agent benchmark cannot merely ask whether the final answer _looks right_. It must establish that the task genuinely measures the intended capability, that the environment ran correctly, and that the evaluator can distinguish real success from plausible-looking output. In an operational evaluation system, these responsibilities belong to a **green agent**: an evaluator that prepares tasks and environments, supervises participant agents, verifies outcomes, and reports reproducible metrics.

This part develops that idea from first principles. It introduces the major types of agent evaluation, formalizes validity and reliability, examines several benchmark designs, and ends with a concrete blueprint for building a green agent.

### Evaluation Is Part of the Entire AI Lifecycle

Evaluation appears throughout development: the training loss supplies an optimization signal; held-out tests compare recipes and checkpoints; production evaluations measure reliability, cost, and safety; and shared benchmarks support scientific comparison. Because metrics steer research, their scoring rules must reward behavior that actually matters.

### Why Agent Evaluation Is Harder Than Model Evaluation

Unlike a static prompt-response model, an agent behaves like a policy in an environment. At step $t$, it receives an observation $o_t$, chooses an action $a_t$, and causes the environment to transition from state $s_t$ to state $s_{t+1}$:

$$
a_t\sim\pi_\theta(a\mid h_t),
\qquad
s_{t+1}=T(s_t,a_t),
$$

where $h_t=(o_0,a_0,\ldots,o_t)$ is the interaction history and $T$ is the environment transition function. The complete execution is a trajectory

$$
\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\ldots,s_T).
$$

This creates several new evaluation problems:

- A fluent final message may claim success even though the required API call never occurred.
- The final state may be correct even though the agent violated a policy while reaching it.
- A correct strategy may fail because the sandbox, network, or tool layer malfunctioned.
- The same agent may succeed on one stochastic run and fail on the next.
- A metric may be vulnerable to shortcuts that satisfy the checker without completing the intended task.

Agent evaluation must therefore inspect more than text. Depending on the task, it may need to verify actions, intermediate states, terminal state, policy compliance, resource consumption, and repeatability.

### A Practical Taxonomy of Evaluation Tasks

Three distinctions help determine what kind of evaluator a task needs.

| Dimension          | Category                      | Main implication                                    |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Output space       | Closed-ended vs. open-ended   | Whether a finite label set is available             |
| Verifiability      | Verifiable vs. non-verifiable | Whether a programmatic oracle can determine success |
| Test-set lifecycle | Static vs. dynamic            | Whether test cases remain fixed or are refreshed    |

These dimensions are related, but they are not interchangeable. An open-ended coding task can still be objectively verifiable with tests, while a short response can remain subjective if quality depends on tone or usefulness.

#### Closed-Ended Evaluation

Closed-ended tasks have a finite set of possible outputs. Multiple-choice questions, binary decisions, sentiment labels, entailment, and named-entity recognition are common examples. Their constrained output space makes automated scoring straightforward.

For binary classification, accuracy summarizes total correctness, while precision and recall separate false positives from false negatives; $F_1$ balances the latter two. Accuracy can hide poor behavior on imbalanced data, but these label-based metrics work well when the desired label is unambiguous. They become much less informative when an agent must create an artifact, modify an environment, or complete a multi-step workflow.

#### Open-Ended Evaluation

Open-ended tasks permit an enormous range of outputs. Storytelling, rewriting, code generation, mathematical proof, planning, and tool-driven problem solving all fall into this category. The key follow-up question is whether the result is verifiable.

A **verifiable task** has a clear oracle: a program that can determine whether the result satisfies the goal. Examples include:

- running generated code against unit tests;
- checking a formal proof with a proof assistant;
- comparing a final database state with a required state;
- executing a proof of concept against patched and unpatched software;
- testing whether specified files, records, or reservations exist after an agent acts.

A **non-verifiable task** lacks a complete objective oracle. A story may be coherent, original, well paced, and stylistically appropriate without having one uniquely correct form. Such outputs often require multidimensional rubrics and either human or model-based judgment.

#### Static and Dynamic Benchmarks

A **static benchmark** keeps fixed cases, enabling longitudinal comparison but inviting contamination, tuning, and eventual saturation. A **dynamic benchmark** refreshes cases to reduce memorization and follow real distributions, but every result must identify its snapshot, generation procedure, and date.

### Human Evaluation and LLM-as-a-Judge

When no programmatic oracle exists, human evaluation remains the most direct reference. Reviewers can assess correctness, coherence, factuality, relevance, style, and usefulness. However, human evaluation is slow, expensive, and variable. Different reviewers may disagree, and the same reviewer may change their judgment over time.

For two categorical annotators, Cohen's kappa corrects raw agreement for agreement expected by chance:

$$
\kappa=\frac{P_o-P_e}{1-P_e},
$$

where $P_o$ is observed agreement and $P_e$ is the agreement expected from the annotators' category frequencies. Reporting agreement matters because an average score can look precise even when the underlying judgments are unstable.

An LLM judge offers a cheaper and faster approximation to human review. A strong judge can recognize paraphrases and apply a detailed rubric more flexibly than exact matching. It also introduces its own failure modes:

- stochastic variation across repeated runs;
- sensitivity to prompt wording or response order;
- biases toward particular writing styles, lengths, or domains;
- poor interpretability;
- prompt injection embedded in the candidate response.

One mitigation is to repeat judging across several seeds, judge models, or prompt configurations and aggregate the scores or vote distributions. Repetition reduces sampling noise, while evaluator diversity reduces dependence on one judge's bias. Neither step proves correctness, so a carefully reviewed human sample should still test alignment with the intended rubric.

Candidate content should always be treated as untrusted data. Delimit it from evaluator instructions, require a structured output schema, and prevent text inside the candidate response from altering the grading policy. These defenses reduce prompt-injection risk, but the highest-value tasks should also include independent checks or human audits.

### The Two Validities Every Benchmark Needs

A good score is meaningful only if two links in the measurement chain are sound:

$$
\text{target capability}
\xrightarrow{\text{task validity}}
\text{real task success}
\xrightarrow{\text{outcome validity}}
\text{reported score}.
$$

#### Task Validity

**Task validity** asks whether success actually demonstrates the capability the benchmark claims to measure. A planning benchmark has weak task validity if cases can be solved by memorizing a fixed lookup table. A web-navigation benchmark has weak task validity if a hard-coded URL bypasses the intended search and reasoning process.

High task validity means that passing is strong evidence of the claimed capability and that an agent lacking that capability cannot pass through a shortcut.

#### Outcome Validity

**Outcome validity** asks whether the evaluator's verdict faithfully reflects actual task success. Let $V=1$ mean that the evaluator reports a pass. The evaluator can then be audited like a classifier:

$$
\mathrm{Evaluator\ Precision}=P(E=1\mid V=1),
$$

$$
\mathrm{Evaluator\ Recall}=P(V=1\mid E=1).
$$

Low precision means false passes: the evaluator accepts failed work. Low recall means false failures: the evaluator rejects valid solutions. Both are damaging. False passes exaggerate capability, while false failures hide real progress and can distort model selection.

The distinction is crucial. Improving the task does not automatically improve the checker, and improving the checker does not guarantee that the task measures the right capability.

### Match the Evaluator to the Output

Outcome validity depends on the artifact being evaluated. Text, code, and environment-changing actions require different verification strategies.

#### Text: Exactness Versus Meaning

Exact string matching is appropriate when formatting is part of the task or when the output is a canonical identifier. It is brittle for semantic answers: capitalization, whitespace, harmless explanation, or a valid paraphrase may create false negatives.

Substring matching is more tolerant but easier to game. If the correct answer is one of `A`, `B`, `C`, or `D`, an agent can include all four and satisfy a naive substring checker. Negation creates another trap: the presence of the word `yes` does not imply an affirmative answer.

A practical text evaluator should therefore use the narrowest reliable method:

1. normalize superficial formatting only when it is irrelevant;
2. use exact matching for identifiers and canonical outputs;
3. use structured parsing for fields and schemas;
4. use semantic judging only when multiple valid phrasings are unavoidable;
5. audit automated judgments against human review.

#### Code: Execute It in a Sandbox

Code should be judged by execution, not appearance. Unit tests should cover normal inputs, boundaries, invalid inputs, and side effects; fuzzing broadens coverage; and end-to-end tests should account for timing, networks, and concurrency. Untrusted submissions need isolated processes or containers with strict time, memory, filesystem, and network limits. Tests do not prove universal correctness, so targeted edge cases and randomized coverage should complement each other.

#### Interactive Tasks: Verify State and Policy

For an agent operating on a database, browser, filesystem, or simulator, the strongest oracle usually examines the terminal environment state. A ticket-booking task, for example, may require that the reservation exists, matches the requested route, stays within budget, and does not duplicate an existing booking. A final message saying “Your ticket is booked” should have no influence on this oracle.

Terminal state is sometimes insufficient. If a policy requires identity verification before an account change, the evaluator must also inspect the trajectory. A fuller verifier is therefore

$$
V(\tau,s_T)
=V_{\mathrm{goal}}(s_T)
\cdot V_{\mathrm{policy}}(\tau)
\cdot V_{\mathrm{environment}},
$$

where $V_{\mathrm{environment}}$ confirms that the test infrastructure itself operated correctly. This separation prevents an infrastructure failure from being mistaken for an agent failure.

A state-and-policy checker should return both a verdict and diagnostic details—for example, whether a reservation exists, matches the route and budget, and was created only after user confirmation. Diagnostics make evaluator failures easier to investigate and prevent a benchmark from collapsing all behavior into an unexplained zero or one.

### What Makes a Benchmark Durable

A useful benchmark should remain informative after the first leaderboard appears. Four properties are especially important.

- **Realism:** Include imperfect data, multi-step work, tool errors, policies, and objective trade-offs.
- **Difficulty coverage:** Use easy cases for basic competence and hard cases for headroom; avoid floor and ceiling effects.
- **Contamination resistance:** Use dynamic refresh, private authoring, and post-cutoff subsets, and split results by contamination risk.
- **Gaming resistance:** Red-team how an agent could maximize the metric without completing the intended task.

### Four Benchmark Case Studies

Several established benchmark designs illustrate how these principles translate into concrete systems.

| Benchmark      | What the agent does                                              | Environment and data                                                                | Main evaluator                                                                       |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| CyberGym       | Reproduces real software vulnerabilities from descriptions       | Containerized pre-patch and post-patch builds reconstructed from vulnerability data | Runtime sanitizer, crash signature, and build-specific behavior                      |
| $\tau$-bench   | Resolves multi-turn retail or airline service tasks through APIs | Simulated user, domain policies, tools, and database                                | Final database state compared with the annotated goal                                |
| $\tau^2$-bench | Coordinates with a tool-using user under partial observability   | Separate agent and user data and tool sets                                          | Environment, communication, natural-language, and action assertions                  |
| GDPval         | Produces realistic professional work products                    | Expert-authored assignments, context, and reference files                           | Blinded experts compare AI and human deliverables; time and cost are also considered |
| CRMArena       | Completes enterprise customer-relationship workflows             | Salesforce sandbox with UI, APIs, SOQL, and SOSL                                    | $F_1$, exact ground-truth identifiers, reliability, turns, tokens, and cost          |

#### CyberGym: Verify the Causal Vulnerability

CyberGym runs generated proofs of concept against reconstructed pre- and post-patch builds. The target crash must occur only on the vulnerable build, which rejects unrelated crashes; post-cutoff vulnerabilities also provide a contamination check.

#### $\tau$-bench and $\tau^2$-bench: Evaluate Tool-Driven Service

$\tau$-bench compares the final database state from tool-driven service work with an annotated goal. $\tau^2$-bench gives both agent and simulated user tools and partial state, then adds assertions about communication and required or forbidden action sequences. Together they show why both state and trajectory may matter.

#### GDPval: Evaluate Professional Deliverables

GDPval uses expert-authored professional assignments and blinded comparisons between model and expert deliverables. Bespoke authoring improves realism and contamination resistance, but expert grading is expensive.

#### CRMArena: Combine Correctness and Efficiency

CRMArena combines $F_1$ or exact identifier matches with reliability, turns, tokens, and cost in a realistic CRM sandbox. If a run consumes $N_{\mathrm{in}}$ input tokens, $N_{\mathrm{out}}$ output tokens, and paid tool calls, its estimated cost is

$$
C
=N_{\mathrm{in}}p_{\mathrm{in}}
+N_{\mathrm{out}}p_{\mathrm{out}}
+\sum_{j=1}^{q}C_{\mathrm{tool},j},
$$

where $p_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $p_{\mathrm{out}}$ are per-token prices. A slightly more accurate agent may still be worse in production if it is orders of magnitude more expensive.

### Reliability Requires Repeated Runs

Agent trajectories are often stochastic. A single successful run does not establish dependable behavior, and a single failure may not reveal the agent's full capability. Repeated trials expose this variance.

Let $X_{i,r}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether run $r$ succeeds on task $i$. Two multi-run metrics answer different questions:

1. **At-least-one success in $k$ attempts:**

   ```math
   \mathrm{Pass@}k
   =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
   \mathbf{1}\!\left[\sum_{r=1}^{k}X_{i,r}\ge 1\right].
   ```

   This measures whether repeated exploration can find a successful trajectory.

2. **Success in all $k$ attempts:**

   ```math
   \mathrm{Pass}^{k}
   =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
   \prod_{r=1}^{k}X_{i,r}.
   ```

   This measures strict consistency: every repeated run must succeed.

The notation is easy to confuse, so an evaluation report should define the metric rather than relying on its name alone. For production agents, strict repeated-run success often reveals reliability problems hidden by average pass rate. Regardless of the metric, always record the sampling settings, model version, tool versions, environment image, retry policy, and random seed when applicable.

### The Green Agent as an Evaluation Orchestrator

A green agent plays the role of examiner and test operator. The participant, sometimes called the white agent, attempts the task. The green agent owns the evaluation procedure but should not solve the participant's task for it.

The lifecycle is:

1. The evaluation platform confirms that the green and participant agents are online and resets their state.
2. The platform gives the green agent a task and the participant agent's endpoint.
3. The green agent creates or resets the environment and exposes the permitted tools.
4. The green agent sends task instructions to the participant.
5. The participant acts, receives observations, and continues until it terminates or reaches a limit.
6. The green agent checks infrastructure health, verifies the final state and trajectory, computes metrics, and submits results.

Its core components are therefore:

- a task dataset and loader;
- an environment factory or reset mechanism;
- tool definitions and access controls;
- a participant-agent interface;
- step, time, and resource limits;
- a verifier or grading rubric;
- structured logging and metric aggregation;
- a protocol-compatible reporting interface.

Standard protocols such as MCP, A2A, or ordinary HTTP APIs can connect these pieces. The essential requirement is a stable contract: tasks, observations, actions, termination signals, and results must have explicit schemas.

#### A Minimal Green-Agent Evaluation Loop

<details><summary>The following example captures the control flow without depending on a particular agent platform:</summary>

```python
from dataclasses import dataclass
from typing import Any, Protocol


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Task:
    task_id: str
    instruction: str
    initial_state: dict[str, Any]
    goal: dict[str, Any]


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class EvaluationResult:
    task_id: str
    passed: bool
    steps: int
    input_tokens: int
    output_tokens: int
    details: list[str]


class Participant(Protocol):
    def act(self, instruction: str, observation: dict[str, Any]) -> dict[str, Any]: ...


class Environment(Protocol):
    def reset(self, state: dict[str, Any]) -> None: ...
    def observe(self) -> dict[str, Any]: ...
    def execute(self, action: dict[str, Any]) -> None: ...
    def is_terminal(self) -> bool: ...
    def is_healthy(self) -> bool: ...
    def state(self) -> dict[str, Any]: ...


class GreenAgent:
    def __init__(self, environment: Environment, max_steps: int = 20):
        self.environment = environment
        self.max_steps = max_steps

    def evaluate(self, task: Task, participant: Participant) -> EvaluationResult:
        self.environment.reset(task.initial_state)
        trajectory: list[Action] = []
        input_tokens = 0
        output_tokens = 0

        for _ in range(self.max_steps):
            observation = self.environment.observe()
            raw_action = participant.act(task.instruction, observation)

            # Token counts would normally come from the participant response metadata.
            input_tokens += int(raw_action.pop("input_tokens", 0))
            output_tokens += int(raw_action.pop("output_tokens", 0))

            action = Action(
                name=str(raw_action["name"]),
                arguments=dict(raw_action.get("arguments", {})),
            )
            trajectory.append(action)
            self.environment.execute(raw_action)

            if self.environment.is_terminal():
                break

        healthy = self.environment.is_healthy()
        passed, details = verify_booking(
            self.environment.state(), trajectory, task.goal
        )
        if not healthy:
            passed = False
            details.append("Evaluation environment was unhealthy")

        return EvaluationResult(
            task_id=task.task_id,
            passed=passed,
            steps=len(trajectory),
            input_tokens=input_tokens,
            output_tokens=output_tokens,
            details=details,
        )
```

This skeleton intentionally separates orchestration from verification. That separation makes it possible to test the evaluator independently, swap environments, and reuse the same control loop for multiple benchmarks.

Production implementations should add process isolation, authentication, schema validation, timeouts, retry rules, immutable logs, cleanup, and explicit infrastructure-error statuses. Most importantly, they should never classify an evaluator outage as an ordinary participant failure.

### Building a Green Agent: Two Project Paths

There are two practical ways to create a green agent.

#### Type 1: Integrate and Improve an Existing Benchmark

An existing benchmark already supplies tasks, expected outcomes, and evaluation logic. The project is to adapt it to the platform faithfully and then improve its reliability.

The work has three stages:

1. **Integration:** Convert task formats, implement dataset loaders, reproduce the required environment, expose tools, and wrap the evaluator in the platform protocol.
2. **Quality analysis:** Manually sample cases, check task clarity and correctness, reproduce original results, audit the evaluator for false positives and false negatives, and document bias or missing coverage.
3. **Correction and expansion:** Repair clear errors, remove invalid tasks, strengthen weak tests, or add cases that improve diversity and coverage.

Faithfulness comes first. An implementation should reproduce the source benchmark before claiming that a correction or extension improves it.

#### Type 2: Design a New Benchmark

A new benchmark is appropriate when an important real-world capability is not adequately measured. Candidate domains include calendar organization, meeting scheduling, to-do management, and other multi-step tasks for which an agent can provide practical value.

A new benchmark needs:

- a precise goal and capability claim;
- realistic tasks with enough difficulty range;
- an environment and tool model;
- a data-generation and validation process;
- robust automatic checks or a feasible human rubric;
- repeated-run reliability measurements;
- contamination, bias, and limitation analysis.

Multi-agent tasks are especially useful when collaboration is inherent to the problem. Different participant agents might research separate components, propose alternative solutions, critique results, or synthesize a final answer. The benchmark should still verify the shared outcome and should attribute costs and failures clearly.

### The SWE-bench Verified Lesson: Audit the Evaluator

The transition from SWE-bench to SWE-bench Verified shows why benchmark curation matters. In the audited sample of 1,700 tasks, professional developers identified underspecified issue descriptions in 38.3 percent of cases and unfair tests in 61.1 percent. Because these categories overlap, 68.3 percent of samples were affected by at least one problem. The resulting verified benchmark retained 500 high-quality tasks.

Because flaw categories overlap, their percentages cannot simply be added: one task may contain several defects.

In one matched evaluation, an unchanged agent scaffold achieved a 16.0 percent resolved rate on the earlier benchmark variant and 33.2 percent on SWE-bench Verified. This does not mean the underlying agent suddenly became twice as capable. It means that ambiguous specifications and unfair tests had produced many false negatives. Better evaluation changed the accuracy of the measurement.

The broader lesson is uncomfortable but valuable: a difficult benchmark is not necessarily a good benchmark. Difficulty caused by broken infrastructure, missing information, or incorrect tests measures tolerance for benchmark defects rather than the intended agent capability.

### A Step-by-Step Benchmark Checklist

Before implementing a new green agent or adapting an existing one, answer the following questions.

- **Define the claim:** What capability is measured, why does success require it, and what shortcuts could fake it?
- **Specify the task:** State the participant's input, required outcome, constraints, policies, and whether multiple solutions are valid.
- **Design the environment:** Define tools, observations, state-changing actions, clean resets, and separate tool failures from participant failures.
- **Design the evaluator early:** Prefer exact fields, tests, or state predicates; inspect trajectories when necessary; calibrate subjective rubrics; and measure false passes and failures.
- **Curate cases:** Cover normal, boundary, adversarial, and varied-difficulty behavior while checking accuracy, diversity, contamination, and saturation.
- **Measure the system:** Report repeated-run reliability, turns, tokens, tool calls, cost, latency, diagnostics, versions, and seeds—not only average success.
- **Audit the benchmark:** Inspect samples, test known-good and deliberately flawed solutions, attack the metric, and document limitations and corrections.

The NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks track guidelines provide another useful reference point for thinking about documentation, data quality, reproducibility, and societal impact.

## Part III — From Next-Token Prediction to Rack-Scale Systems: How AI Is Rewriting Applications, Infrastructure, and Hardware

Training and evaluation are only part of the picture. Statistical prediction, sparse computation, RL, caching, scheduling, and fast interconnects now operate together at a scale that forces cloud infrastructure to resemble a supercomputer. Building an AI product is therefore simultaneously an application, unit-economics, infrastructure, and hardware-software co-design problem.

### The Old Idea Behind Modern Language Models

Mechanical Chinese typewriters used bigram statistics to place frequently adjacent characters near one another, reducing average movement. The objective was physical rather than linguistic, but it used context to predict what came next. Neural language models scale that old idea far beyond the count tables discussed in Part I.

### Why Model Progress Has Not Been One-Dimensional

Recent progress is not only a story of more parameters and data. Sparse architectures, extra inference computation, and outcome-based optimization change how capacity is used.

#### Sparse Computation with Mixture of Experts

A mixture-of-experts model routes each token to a small subset of expert subnetworks. A simplified layer is

```math
y(x)=\sum_{i \in TopK(g(x))}g_i(x)E_i(x),
```

where $E_i$ is expert $i$ and $g_i(x)$ its routing weight. Capacity can grow faster than per-token compute, though routing balance, communication, and placement remain difficult.

#### Spending More Computation at Inference Time

Test-time scaling spends inference compute on intermediate steps, alternative candidates, checks, or revisions. The product chooses a budget by balancing quality against latency and cost:

$$
c^*=\arg\max_c\left[Q(c)-\lambda C(c)\right],
$$

where $c$ is the compute budget and $\lambda$ values efficiency. A proof assistant may tolerate seconds of extra work; autocomplete may not.

#### Optimizing Outcomes Rather Than Only Imitation

Reinforcement learning complements these methods by scoring complete, verifiable trajectories rather than only locally plausible continuations. Pretraining supplies knowledge, sparsity improves capacity efficiency, inference compute lets the model work harder, and RL connects behavior to outcomes.

### Model Quality Matters, but Applications Capture the Value

The model ecosystem includes both proprietary services and openly available weights. Proprietary models often introduce frontier capabilities first; open models frequently close the gap and give developers more control over deployment, customization, and cost. The precise distance between the two changes too quickly to be a durable rule.

For an application builder, the more useful question is not which camp wins. It is where value remains after a better foundation model becomes available to everyone.

A thin interface around a model is fragile. If its only advantage is a prompt or a generic text box, the underlying provider may absorb that feature into the next model release. More durable products tend to own at least one difficult layer around the model:

- a workflow used repeatedly by professionals;
- proprietary or permissioned data;
- deep integrations with existing tools;
- domain-specific evaluation and feedback;
- a specialized model or media pipeline;
- trust, compliance, and operational reliability.

This helps explain why productivity-oriented applications can monetize more reliably than casual novelty. A professional pays when a tool saves time, increases output quality, or reduces operational risk. The same user may decline to pay for an entertaining capability used only occasionally.

#### The Unit Economics of an AI Application

An AI subscription hides variable inference cost behind a fixed monthly price. A simple per-user contribution margin is

$$
M_u
=R_u
-T_{\text{in}}p_{\text{in}}
-T_{\text{out}}p_{\text{out}}
-C_{\text{compute}}
-C_{\text{ops}},
$$

where $R_u$ is revenue per user, $T_{\text{in}}$ and $T_{\text{out}}$ are input and output token volumes, $p_{\text{in}}$ and $p_{\text{out}}$ are their prices, and the remaining terms cover additional compute and operational cost.

Healthy unit economics require $M_u>0$ over the relevant customer segment. Averages can be deceptive: a fixed-price plan may be profitable for light users and deeply unprofitable for heavy users. Model routing, caching, rate limits, and usage-based tiers are therefore product decisions as much as infrastructure optimizations.

Falling inference prices can rescue an initially weak margin, but that is not a substitute for customer value. If a product does not solve an important problem, cheaper tokens merely make an unwanted product cheaper to operate.

#### Consumer Speed and Enterprise Depth

Consumer AI products can grow quickly because users can try them immediately. They also face rapid imitation and low switching costs. Enterprise products move more slowly, but successful ones become embedded in data, permissions, and business processes.

Enterprise search illustrates the difference. The visible experience may resemble a familiar search box or chat interface, but the hard work lies underneath:

- connecting document stores, ticket systems, source repositories, and collaboration tools;
- maintaining each system's access-control rules;
- indexing frequently changing information;
- returning results that are both relevant and authorized;
- showing evidence so users can verify generated answers.

That integration work is unglamorous, but it creates a defensible product. The model supplies general language ability; the application supplies the organizational context and the reliable path from question to action.

Established software companies can benefit from the same complementarity. Domain expertise, customer relationships, and years of interaction data do not disappear when stronger models arrive. They become inputs to better content generation, personalization, support, and workflow automation. In many markets, the likely outcome is not “models replace all applications,” but “applications that use models well outperform those that do not.”

### RAG Is Becoming Infrastructure, Not Disappearing

Technology discussions often abandon a term as soon as a newer one becomes fashionable. Retrieval-augmented generation is sometimes treated as obsolete now that agents receive more attention. In practice, retrieval is becoming less visible because it is turning into standard plumbing.

Long context alone does not eliminate the need for retrieval. Loading every available document into a prompt is expensive, slow, and frequently less accurate than selecting the relevant evidence first. Enterprise systems also need permission filtering and source freshness, neither of which is solved merely by a larger context window.

A practical retrieval pipeline is usually coarse-to-fine:

1. Apply authorization filters before any content reaches the model.
2. Retrieve a broad candidate set cheaply with keyword or vector search.
3. Rerank a smaller set with a more capable model.
4. Generate an answer from the best evidence.
5. Return citations or source identifiers for verification.

If $q$ is a query and $d$ is a document, a coarse hybrid score might be

$$
s_{\text{coarse}}(q,d)
=\alpha\,s_{\text{keyword}}(q,d)
+(1-\alpha)\,\cos(e_q,e_d),
$$

where $e_q$ and $e_d$ are embeddings. An expensive reranker evaluates only the strongest candidates. Authorization must precede ranking and generation: a semantically relevant document is still excluded if the user cannot read it.

Retrieval also clarifies the boundary between general and specific knowledge. A model may carry broad concepts in its parameters, but changing organizational facts should come from current, inspectable sources. When accuracy matters, the system should prefer evidence over an uncertain memory of training data.

### AI Infrastructure Is a Third Computing Pillar

Cloud infrastructure first grew around web services. A web application usually performs substantial I/O, modest computation per request, and easy horizontal replication. If traffic grows, the operator adds more stateless service instances.

The data-cloud era introduced large distributed queries and batch processing. Frameworks such as MapReduce and Spark hid scheduling, shuffling, and recovery behind a data-oriented abstraction. A failed partition could often be recomputed without restarting the entire workload.

AI workloads have a different shape. Training and high-throughput inference are dominated by numerical computation, accelerator memory, and communication among tightly coupled devices. The software stack may be narrower than a general cloud stack, but the physical constraints are stronger.

| Workload       | Dominant resource                      | Parallel structure              | Typical failure response                    |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Web service    | Network and storage I/O                | Many independent requests       | Replace one instance                        |
| Data analytics | I/O plus distributed compute           | Partitioned stages and shuffles | Retry one task or partition                 |
| AI training    | Accelerator compute and interconnect   | Tightly synchronized workers    | Restore a coordinated job from a checkpoint |
| AI inference   | Memory bandwidth, compute, and latency | Batching plus model parallelism | Reroute traffic and rebuild capacity        |

#### Arithmetic Intensity Explains Part of the Difference

The balance between data movement and calculation can be summarized by arithmetic intensity:

$$
I=\frac{\text{floating-point operations}}
{\text{bytes transferred from memory}}.
$$

Low-intensity operations spend much of their time moving data. Large matrix multiplications reuse loaded values and can achieve high arithmetic intensity, making accelerators effective. However, LLM inference with small batches may become memory-bandwidth-bound because the system repeatedly streams a large set of weights to produce relatively few tokens.

This is why “AI compute” is not a single uniform workload. Training, prompt processing, and token-by-token decoding can stress different parts of the machine. Efficient platforms must schedule them with their actual bottlenecks in mind.

#### Locality Is Part of the API, Whether Exposed or Not

A conventional virtual machine is designed to look interchangeable with any equivalent virtual machine. Distributed AI training breaks that illusion. Workers need the right number of accelerators, fast links between them, compatible topology, and nearby storage. Four devices behind the same high-bandwidth switch are not operationally equivalent to four devices scattered across a data center.

In a ring all-reduce, each worker moves nearly twice the per-worker gradient payload. Communication time therefore grows with payload size and falls with effective interconnect bandwidth; placing workers on slower or oversubscribed links directly extends every synchronized training step.

Data locality creates a second constraint. Installing a framework on a new provider may take minutes; transferring a petabyte-scale dataset and rebuilding its storage pipeline does not. Multi-cloud portability therefore depends on both compute orchestration and deliberate data placement.

#### Failures Multiply with Cluster Size

Suppose each worker fails independently at rate $\lambda$ failures per hour. For $N$ workers, a simple exponential model gives the probability that the whole job experiences no worker failure over time $T$:

$$
P(\text{no failure during }T)=e^{-N\lambda T}.
$$

The model is simplified—real failures are often correlated—but it captures the scaling problem. Even reliable individual machines produce frequent job-level interruptions when thousands must remain healthy together.

The cost of a failure grows with cluster size, accelerator price, and the time elapsed since the last usable checkpoint. Checkpointing, health monitoring, restartability, and idempotent job design are therefore economic controls, not merely reliability features.

### The Productive Tension Between Researchers and Operators

AI researchers often want a job-oriented abstraction: allocate several complete machines, establish distributed communication, run a training program, and return the result. Operators want declarative services, resource sharing, automated rescheduling, policy enforcement, and high utilization.

This tension is often labeled Slurm versus Kubernetes, but the labels matter less than the competing requirements. Researchers favor whole jobs, stable topology, reproducible experiments, simple launch interfaces, and coordinated restarts. Operators favor services, flexible placement, fine-grained control, observability, and component-level repair. Neither side can simply eliminate the other. A good AI-native layer exposes a simple job interface while retaining topology-aware scheduling, monitoring, policy, and automated recovery underneath.

Several operating practices follow from this compromise:

1. **Use more than one compute source.** Accelerator shortages and capacity fragmentation make a single-provider strategy risky.
2. **Measure utilization continuously.** An accelerator that is productive only half the time roughly doubles the cost of each useful hour.

3. **Make jobs restartable.** Checkpoints and idempotent setup reduce the blast radius of hardware or network failures.
4. **Separate training and serving assumptions.** Batch training values throughput and coordinated topology; online inference values latency, elasticity, and graceful degradation.
5. **Keep data movement explicit.** A scheduler that can move code but not data has not achieved true portability.

AI-focused cloud providers emerged around these needs, while hyperscale clouds are adding more topology-aware and workload-oriented services. The likely endpoint is not one universal abstraction. It is a layered system that lets developers express AI work simply while allowing the platform to manage physical constraints precisely.

This also changes what cloud vendors sell. The original cloud product was a resource: a virtual machine, a disk, or a database instance. AI platforms increasingly sell a completed workload or measurable unit—such as a training job, hosted model endpoint, or generated token. Hyperscale operators retain an advantage because large resource pools can absorb different demand patterns, but realizing that advantage requires training jobs to become more interruptible, restartable, and efficient.

### Old Reliability Lessons Still Apply

Although AI changes resource requirements, productized AI services inherit familiar distributed-systems failure modes.

Consider a model-serving deployment that is accidentally stopped. Requests accumulate while the service is unavailable. When the first replica returns, the entire backlog floods it, exhausting memory or connection limits and killing it. The next replica suffers the same fate. This is a classic thundering-herd problem.

The recovery condition is straightforward. If each healthy replica sustains $\mu$ requests per second and $n(t)$ replicas are ready, admitted traffic should satisfy

$$
\lambda_{\text{admitted}}(t)
\le n(t)\mu,
$$

rather than immediately releasing the full queued arrival rate. Backpressure, bounded queues, readiness checks, and gradual traffic ramp-up prevent a recovering service from being destroyed by its own demand.

Production gateways typically enforce rate limits and coordinate them across instances. The control principle is simple: bring capacity online first, admit work at a sustainable rate, and increase traffic only as additional replicas become healthy.

This example captures a broader lesson: once an AI demo becomes a service, decades of operational knowledge return. Load shedding, circuit breakers, idempotency, observability, canary releases, and capacity planning remain essential. A powerful model does not repeal distributed systems.

### Hardware Is Coming Full Circle

Computing hardware has moved through alternating periods of integration and disaggregation.

Early supercomputers were designed as large, tightly integrated systems. CPUs and memory participated in a shared machine, which made programming comparatively direct but made the hardware expensive and inflexible. The cloud era pushed in the opposite direction. Standardized, modular servers could be installed, replaced, and scaled independently. Microservices fit that “many small machines” model well.

AI has started bending the architecture back toward integration:

1. A GPU first appeared as an accelerator card inside a conventional server.
2. Multi-GPU servers connected several accelerators with a fast local fabric.
3. Rack-scale systems connected many accelerators and large memory pools through specialized switches.
4. Frontier training clusters began treating an entire data center as a coordinated computational unit.

The reason is model scale. If a model contains $P$ parameters stored with $b$ bits per parameter, the weights alone require

$$
M_{\text{weights}}=\frac{Pb}{8}\ \text{bytes}.
$$

Inference needs still more memory for temporary activations, workspaces, and the attention key-value cache.

As parameter counts, context lengths, and concurrent requests grow, a single accelerator's memory becomes insufficient. The system must partition weights and state across devices, which makes interconnect bandwidth and topology first-class design constraints.

Rack-scale fabrics reduce the penalty of crossing server boundaries. They do not make all memory access free or perfectly uniform, but they let software treat a rack more like one coordinated system than a collection of unrelated hosts. This can simplify model parallelism, prompt-cache sharing, and the separation of prompt processing from token decoding.

The historical echo is striking. High-performance computing has long explored global-address-space abstractions that make distributed memory easier to program. Modern AI hardware is pursuing a related goal in the interconnect itself. Ideas discarded as too rigid for web services can become appropriate again when the workload is a tightly coupled matrix computation.

Software must catch up with this hardware. Frameworks need topology-aware placement, efficient collective communication, failure recovery, memory orchestration, and scheduling policies that understand different inference phases. The next gains will come as much from using the machine correctly as from adding raw silicon.

### The Next Frontier Extends Beyond Text

Text interfaces are only one part of the opportunity. Many valuable systems must ingest physical-world data and cause physical-world actions.

Airport operations provide a useful example. Reducing the time between landing and gate arrival requires more than asking a chatbot for advice. A useful system may combine maps, schedules, aircraft telemetry, computer vision, sensor feeds, taxiway constraints, and human air-traffic decisions. It must estimate the current state, identify bottlenecks, simulate alternatives, and communicate or execute safe actions.

That is closer to a digital twin and a control system than to a conventional prompt-response application. Its success depends on data integration, world modeling, optimization, safety, and organizational adoption. The language model may provide a natural interface or reasoning component, but it is not the entire product.

Robotics, phones, and network-edge deployments introduce the opposite hardware pressure from frontier training. Centralized research seeks the best possible result and willingly consumes large clusters. Edge systems must fit strict limits on power, memory, latency, privacy, and network availability.

Their design problem can be expressed as constrained optimization:

$$
\max_{m,h}\ Q(m,h)
\quad\text{subject to}\quad
\begin{cases}
L(m,h)\le L_{\max},\\
E(m,h)\le E_{\max},\\
M(m,h)\le M_{\max},
\end{cases}
$$

where $m$ is the model choice, $h$ is the hardware configuration, $Q$ is task quality, and $L$, $E$, and $M$ represent latency, energy, and memory. Quantization, distillation, smaller architectures, and specialized accelerators matter because “use a larger cluster” is not available on a robot or phone.

The industry therefore faces two simultaneous directions: scale up centralized systems to explore maximum capability, and make models smaller and more efficient so those capabilities can be exploited in the real world.

### What This Means for Builders

The strongest opportunities are often between layers rather than inside one layer.

Training another general-purpose foundation model demands exceptional capital, data, and research talent. A smaller team can create differentiated value by combining capable models with domain expertise, proprietary workflows, trustworthy data access, and measurable outcomes. Enterprise verticals are especially promising because large general model providers cannot deeply solve every industry's operational details.

That strategy still requires infrastructure discipline. Teams should avoid spending their best model or application engineers on undifferentiated cluster operations when a reliable platform can absorb that work. At the same time, they should understand enough of the underlying system to measure cost, choose providers, manage failure, and avoid building a product whose margins vanish under heavy usage.

Technical quality alone is also insufficient. Awareness attracts users; their behavior supplies feedback and data that can improve the product and drive further adoption.

Distribution is not separate from engineering when user behavior supplies the information needed to refine evaluations, workflows, and models. Teams need both the ability to build and the ability to explain why the product matters.

The same bridge is necessary inside the company. Researchers think in experiments, model quality, and iteration speed. Infrastructure engineers think in reliability, utilization, isolation, and recovery. Both groups are usually optimizing rational objectives, but at different layers. Leaders who can translate between those priorities can turn an unreliable research prototype into a useful system without suffocating experimentation.

Finally, accelerator procurement itself has become a strategic concern. Capacity may be scarce, contracts inflexible, and prices fragmented across providers. A more liquid multi-cloud market would allow builders to spend less time negotiating hardware supply and more time developing models and applications. Until then, supply planning belongs beside architecture and unit economics in an AI startup's technical strategy.

## Part IV — Training Verifiable AI Agents: Data, Evaluation, and Reinforcement Learning

The final problem is to close the loop among environments, tools, verifiers, evaluation, and RL. Unlike a conversational assistant, an agent must change an environment without breaking software, corrupting data, or triggering the wrong transaction. Weak verifiers corrupt rewards, narrow environments exaggerate benchmark gains, and poor exploration blocks better trajectories. Data, evaluation, and optimization must therefore be designed together.

### From Helpful Chatbots to Verifiable Agents

As Part I described, RLHF learns subjective preferences. Given prompt $x$ and responses $y_w$ and $y_l$, a reward model can learn the preferred answer through a Bradley–Terry objective:

$$
P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x)
= \sigma\!\left(R_\phi(x,y_w)-R_\phi(x,y_l)\right).
$$

This suits helpfulness, clarity, and tone, but an agent also needs objective evidence: a patch passes tests, a browser workflow changes the required state, or a mathematical answer is correct.

An agent instead produces a trajectory: a sequence of environment states and actions such as tool calls or generated tokens. A verifier judges the resulting trajectory, giving the basic agent-training objective

$$
J(\theta)
=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\,\tau\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}
\left[R_V(x,\tau)\right].
$$

The distinction is fundamental: preference approximates what people like, while a verifier supplies evidence that the task was completed.

### The Three Parts of an Agentic Training Example

A useful agentic example is not merely a prompt and an answer. It contains three coupled components: an environment, a tool interface, and a verifier.

#### Environment

The environment contains the initial and evolving state: repository files and tests for coding, or records, permissions, and schemas for enterprise work. The same action may be correct in one state and harmful in another, and a good final message is worthless if the requested state change never occurred.

#### Tools

Tools are sensors and effectors: APIs supply current information, editors change files, and test runners reveal whether work succeeds. Generating valid calls and interpreting their observations are part of the learned policy.

#### Verifiers

A verifier judges the trajectory through exact answers, formal proofs, unit tests, state assertions, or database invariants. Because successful trajectories can differ in cost, safety, and side effects, verification is often better represented as a vector:

$$
\mathbf{v}(\tau)=
\begin{bmatrix}
v_{\mathrm{correct}}(\tau)\\
v_{\mathrm{format}}(\tau)\\
v_{\mathrm{safety}}(\tau)\\
v_{\mathrm{efficiency}}(\tau)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

A scalar training reward can be derived from that vector:

$$
R_V(\tau)
=w_c v_{\mathrm{correct}}
+w_f v_{\mathrm{format}}
+w_s v_{\mathrm{safety}}
-w_a N_{\mathrm{actions}}
-w_t N_{\mathrm{tokens}}.
$$

The weights encode application priorities. Correctness should dominate small efficiency gains, and unacceptable safety violations should be hard gates rather than tradeable terms.

### Verifier Design Is Part of the Intelligence Problem

A verifier can fail in two directions. A false positive accepts an invalid solution and teaches the policy to repeat it. A false negative rejects a valid solution and removes a useful learning signal. Both errors matter, and their costs depend on the application.

Let $V$ be the implemented verifier and $V^*$ the ideal judgment. Their disagreement includes both false acceptance and false rejection. These errors are not passive measurement noise: because the verifier supplies the reward, they bias the policy update:

```math
\nabla_\theta J_V
=\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)V^*(y)
\right]
+\mathbb{E}\!\left[
\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)
\bigl(V(y)-V^*(y)\bigr)
\right].
```

The second term points training toward the verifier's errors, so corner cases matter. If the user asks for one-half in simplest fractional form, `1/2` and `2/4` are numerically equal but only the first follows the instruction. A robust verifier therefore separates parsing, semantic correctness, and format adherence. The same pattern lets code verifiers report compilation, tests, security, and resources separately, or database verifiers check both intended and unintended changes.

### Coverage Across Environments, Tools, and Verifiers

Agent data should vary domains, initial states, edge cases, tool schemas and errors, instruction ambiguity, verifier implementations, output formats, budgets, and safety policies. Exhaustive combinations are impossible; the goal is to make the underlying task structure more stable than any one interface.

### Evaluation Must Test Generalization, Not Recognition

A benchmark must cover tools, domains, ambiguous instructions, and operational failures. Suites such as SWE-bench, $\tau$-bench, BFCL, ComplexFunctionBench, GSM8K, and AIME illuminate different slices; no single score measures general agent intelligence. The key test is whether performance survives controlled system changes.

#### Swap the Harness

Evaluate the same tasks through different tool groupings, syntax, observation formats, recoverable failures, and information ordering. Two useful summaries are worst-case performance and harness sensitivity:

$$
p_{\mathrm{worst}}=\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}p_h,
\qquad
\Delta_{\mathrm{harness}}
=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}}p_h-\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}}p_h.
$$

A large gap warns that the agent learned a scaffold rather than the capability.

#### Monitor Benchmark Health

A durable benchmark needs hardness, separability, and diversity. All decay as systems improve, public cases leak, and repeated iterations overfit even private sets indirectly. Suites therefore need independently designed tasks, rotating environments, fresh harnesses, and continuous verifier audits.

### Training in Two Stages: Imitation, Then Exploration

Once data and evaluation are in place, the model must learn from them. A practical post-training pipeline often contains two stages: supervised fine-tuning followed by reinforcement learning.

#### Supervised Fine-Tuning as a Warm Start

SFT uses correct, diverse demonstrations to suppress malformed tool calls and meaningless trajectories. It should act as a warm start rather than force one reasoning style or tool sequence so strongly that later exploration disappears.

#### Reinforcement Learning as Feedback-Driven Exploration

RL then lets the current model attempt tasks, receive verifier feedback, and reinforce successful trajectories.

For each prompt, suppose the current policy samples a group of $G$ trajectories with rewards $R_1,\ldots,R_G$. A simple group-relative advantage is

$$
\hat A_i
=\frac{R_i-\bar R}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}(R_j-\bar R)^2}+\varepsilon},
\qquad
\bar R=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_j.
$$

Successful attempts receive positive relative advantage; weaker attempts receive negative relative advantage. The update teaches the model from its own current behavior, which is the key intuition behind on-policy learning.

This also explains why task selection matters. If every rollout succeeds, all rewards are equal and there is little comparative signal. If every rollout fails, there is no successful behavior to reinforce. For a binary verifier with success probability $p$, reward variance is

$$
Var(R)=p(1-p),
$$

which is largest at $p=\tfrac{1}{2}$ and vanishes at $p=0$ or $p=1$. Training should therefore mix difficulties and adapt the curriculum so that enough attempts are challenging but solvable. A curriculum containing only easy tasks teaches little; one containing only impossible tasks wastes compute.

### Training Longer Without Losing Exploration

Longer RL training can improve reward while silently reducing behavioral diversity. The model becomes increasingly confident in a small set of token sequences, and repeated samples begin to look alike. This is entropy collapse.

High entropy does not guarantee intelligent exploration, and low entropy is sometimes appropriate when an action is obvious. The danger is a systematic collapse in which the policy can no longer generate alternative strategies. Group-based RL then loses information because every rollout receives the same result.

Several interventions follow from this diagnosis.

#### Prefer Fresh On-Policy Rollouts

On-policy data comes from the model being updated. It exposes the policy's current mistakes and current areas of uncertainty. Old or externally generated trajectories can still be valuable, but they may no longer describe the distribution the current model visits.

#### Balance Policy Updates

PPO-style objectives control how far a policy moves in one update. For token $a_t$, define the likelihood ratio

$$
r_t(\theta)
=\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}
{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)}.
$$

A decoupled asymmetric clipping objective uses separate lower and upper thresholds:

```math
L_{clip}(\theta)
=-E_t\left[
\min\!\left(
r_t(\theta)\hat A_t,
clip\!\left(
r_t(\theta),1-\epsilon_{low},1+\epsilon_{high}
\right)\hat A_t
\right)
\right],
```

with $\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}>\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}}>0$. The larger upper range gives the optimizer more room to increase the probability of successful actions that were previously unlikely, while clipping still limits destructive updates.

#### Regularize Entropy Directly

An entropy bonus can preserve exploration:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}
=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RL}}
-\alpha_{\mathrm{ent}}\mathbb{E}[\bar H(y)]
+\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\right).
$$

The coefficients require care. Too little entropy pressure allows collapse; too much makes the policy uncertain even when it has learned a reliable action. Reward, entropy, KL divergence, trajectory diversity, and held-out accuracy should be monitored together.

### Sample Better at Inference Time

Training is not the only place to spend computation. At inference time, an agent can generate several candidate trajectories and then select or aggregate them.

The simplest approach is majority voting over parsed final answers. A stronger variant filters or weights votes using length-normalized generation confidence. The same samples can feed a separate selection pass that compares complete solutions. Both approaches trade additional inference cost for higher expected accuracy.

Confidence must still be validated. Models can be confidently wrong, correlated samples can repeat the same mistake, and a flawed answer parser can distort the vote. Sampling methods complement strong verifiers; they do not replace them.

### An End-to-End Blueprint

A practical program for building verifiable agents follows a disciplined loop.

1. **Define the task contract.** Specify the intended final state, allowed actions, forbidden side effects, output schema, and resource budget.
2. **Build realistic sandboxes.** Reproduce the state transitions and failure modes that matter without exposing production systems during training.
3. **Vary tool interfaces.** Represent the same underlying capabilities through different schemas and harnesses so that the policy learns semantics rather than wrappers.
4. **Implement vector verifiers.** Separate semantic correctness, format adherence, safety, and efficiency. Add adversarial cases for both false acceptance and false rejection.
5. **Curate diverse demonstrations.** Use SFT to establish meaningful tool use while preserving multiple valid solution styles.
6. **Collect fresh rollouts.** Sample several trajectories from the current policy, verify them, and compute relative learning signals.
7. **Balance task difficulty.** Retire saturated tasks, avoid batches with no achievable successes, and include a curriculum of intermediate challenges.
8. **Monitor more than reward.** Track held-out success, worst-harness performance, verifier disagreement, entropy, KL drift, action count, token cost, and side effects.
9. **Refresh evaluation continuously.** Add new tasks, private or rotating holdouts, altered harnesses, and independent verifier audits.
10. **Close the loop cautiously.** Promote a new policy only when improvements survive fresh environments and do not exploit weaknesses in the evaluator.

This process resembles human learning: observe examples, attempt tasks, receive feedback, and improve. The difficult part is that machine learning requires each element of that loop to be made explicit. The environment must expose state, the tools must have precise interfaces, the verifier must encode the real objective, and the optimizer must convert outcomes into stable updates.
