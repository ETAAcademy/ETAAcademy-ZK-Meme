# From Deployment to Proof: Building AI Systems That Earn Trust

Artificial intelligence is often discussed as though capability were the scarce resource: find a stronger model, connect it to more tools, and useful autonomy will follow. In practice, capability is only the starting point. An agent can produce convincing code and still misunderstand the requirement.

It can retrieve every report in a company and still not know which one an experienced operator trusts. It can solve a benchmark while wasting too much time and money to survive in production. And it can generate a mathematically elegant argument whose most important step is false.

These are not isolated model failures. They are failures of the system around the model. Trustworthy AI requires an operating environment that turns human intent into explicit specifications, chooses the right amount of computation, exposes outputs to meaningful evaluation, enforces constraints at the point of action, and preserves evidence about what happened.

The same principle applies across apparently different domains. An enterprise agent needs organizational context and governed access before it can act responsibly; a scientific agent needs search, verification, and formal semantics before its discoveries can be trusted. In both cases, the central question is not simply what the model can generate. It is what the surrounding system can repeatedly verify. That reframes adoption. AI is not just another application to distribute across a workforce, and an agent is not merely a chatbot with additional permissions. The organization must redesign work, inference, governance, and measurement together.

Otherwise, local improvements create new bottlenecks: faster engineers wait on unchanged handoffs, cheaper models silently lower quality, broad integrations amplify mistakes, and human approval becomes a weak substitute for truth. A mature system expands autonomy only when its evidence and controls expand with it.

## Redesign the Organization Before Delegating Work

The first barrier to dependable agentic AI is usually organizational rather than technical. Natural-language interfaces make this barrier unusually difficult to see. When an employee misuses a conventional database or programming language, the system often returns an obvious error.

A language model can instead return a fluent, plausible response. The employee feels productive, even when the prompt omitted essential context, the output was not checked, or the proposed action conflicts with an unstated requirement. Surface fluency can conceal a large gap in actual operating skill.

Ironclad encountered that gap when two engineers produced sharply different estimates for the same feature. Seniority did not explain the difference. One treated AI as a thinking partner, supplied context, iterated on the problem, and validated the result.

The other treated it like a search box and accepted its first plausible answer. The contrast suggested that access to the same model does not create a shared capability. Productive use depends on habits of specification, decomposition, verification, and judgment.

The company responded by training engineering, product, and design together. Its program combined instruction in language-model fundamentals with daily demonstrations in which employees explained what they had built, where it failed, and what they learned. The aim was not to create a small group of prompt specialists.

It was to make building with AI—and evaluating AI-generated work—a normal cross-functional practice. Shared channels and informal working sessions extended the training beyond the classroom.

The company later reported a broad increase in participation and engineering execution, although the underlying measurements, model mix, and comparison design were not disclosed in enough detail to support a causal claim. A reported replication at one large customer is encouraging case-study evidence, not broad statistical validation.

Even successful training did not immediately make the whole company faster. AI-fluent engineers completed their portion of the work sooner, then waited for product decisions, design answers, or requirements that still moved through a sequential handoff. As an analogy, Amdahl's law captures this familiar systems effect: accelerating one part of a process produces limited total improvement when a meaningful fraction remains unchanged:

$$
\text{Speedup}_{\text{overall}} = \frac{1}{(1-P)+\frac{P}{S}}
$$

Here, $P$ is the share of the end-to-end process that can benefit from the intervention, while $S$ is the speedup achieved within that share. Even if the accelerated portion becomes arbitrarily fast, the unaccelerated fraction, $1-P$, still limits the result.

Applied to product development, faster code generation cannot eliminate time spent waiting for a team to agree on the problem, resolve a policy question, or discover that two stakeholders meant different things by the same requirement. AI adoption therefore exposes the next bottleneck rather than abolishing bottlenecks.

The appropriate response is to redesign the work before delegating it. Product, design, engineering, legal, operations, and other affected functions need to converge earlier on the problem definition. They must make assumptions visible, identify constraints, define acceptance criteria, and decide what evidence will count as success.

The resulting specification should be stored in a form that both people and agents can reuse. Markdown documents, issue templates, decision records, and structured knowledge bases can all serve this role; the format matters less than making the context durable and reviewable.

This discipline changes the meaning of a prompt. Instead of being an improvised instruction sent to a model, it becomes one view of a larger contract: the goal, inputs, permissions, non-negotiable rules, expected artifact, evaluation criteria, and escalation conditions. Agents are good at accelerating execution inside a defined problem.

They are much less reliable at resolving hidden disagreement among humans. When a generated result disappoints, the cause is often not a lack of intelligence but a missing decision that the organization never made explicit.

Shared infrastructure can make this discipline easier to follow. Ironclad reported building common systems for context management, retrieval, organizational memory, code review, and reusable quality checks, using established agent and application frameworks as part of its internal platform.

The important design choice is not the brand of framework. It is the decision to encode difficult practices once so that every employee does not need to reconstruct them.

For software work, that may mean giving an agent an isolated environment that resembles production, running tests before creating a pull request, attaching the results, and routing the change to an accountable reviewer. For other work, it may mean grounding a draft in approved sources, checking required fields, or comparing the output with a rubric before it enters a queue.

These controls do not remove human responsibility. They move setup, evidence collection, and routine validation into the platform, allowing reviewers to spend their attention on exceptions and judgment.

The infrastructure should also preserve failures as reusable organizational evidence. A rejected patch, corrected recommendation, or missed requirement is more valuable when it becomes a regression case than when it disappears into a chat history. Teams can attach the original context, the agent's output, the review decision, and the corrected result to a maintained evaluation set.

Over time, that collection reveals whether a new model or prompt actually improves the work or merely changes its style. It also makes lessons portable: one team's discovery about an ambiguous field, unreliable source, or dangerous tool sequence can become a guardrail for everyone using the shared platform.

Adoption metrics need the same end-to-end perspective. Counting prompts, generated lines of code, or active users can show engagement, but none proves that the organization ships better work. Useful measures include cycle time from problem definition to reviewed output, rework after review, escaped defects, reviewer burden, and the share of tasks that reach a decision without avoidable handoffs.

These measures can expose a false productivity gain in which generation rises while verification and correction consume the saved time. They also keep training tied to outcomes: the objective is not frequent AI use, but reliable improvement in the complete process.

The same approach reduces the risk of a two-speed organization. When only a few enthusiasts learn to use AI deeply, their advantage compounds as tools improve while the rest of the workforce remains dependent on old processes or on the experts themselves. Mandatory training alone cannot solve that problem; people also need approved tools, examples tied to their work, access to feedback, and a shared vocabulary for failure. Conversely, a platform alone cannot create sound judgment. Fluency and infrastructure have to develop together.

This is why enterprise AI should be managed as an operating-model change. The unit of improvement is not an individual model response or even an individual employee. It is the complete path from intent to decision to execution to review.Training strengthens the people on that path. Specifications reduce ambiguity. Shared tools standardize good practice.Persistent context allows lessons from one task to improve the next. Only when those elements move together does local acceleration become organizational capacity.

## Make Inference Economical

Once agents can perform useful work, economics becomes a design constraint. A system may produce excellent results in a demonstration yet remain impractical when thousands of employees or customers use it. Each extra candidate, verifier call, retrieval step, or retry consumes tokens, time, and infrastructure.

The relevant objective is therefore not maximum intelligence per request at any price. It is the required quality and reliability at an acceptable cost and latency.

That objective cannot be achieved by choosing a model once and treating the decision as permanent. The operating configuration includes the model, retrieval method, prompt, tools, scaffold, memory policy, sampling strategy, stopping rules, and escalation logic. Prices change. New models appear. Production traffic shifts toward different task types.

The best configuration this quarter may be dominated by another configuration next quarter. Model selection is consequently a continuous systems problem, not a procurement event.

One way to spend computation is best-of-N sampling: generate several candidates and use a verifier or judge to select the strongest. This can allow a less expensive model to approach the performance of a more expensive one on tasks where candidates can be graded reliably. Reported experiments on search and coding benchmarks illustrate the possibility, but the result depends on candidate diversity and on the quality of the selector.

Repeatedly generating the same mistake adds cost without adding coverage, while a weak judge can select a polished failure. Benchmark improvements also do not guarantee the same benefit on legal, financial, or operational traffic. [“Improving Best-of-N with Budget-Aware Execution for SWE Agents”](https://www.ai21.com/blog/improving-best-of-n-with-budget-aware-execution-for-swe-agents/).

A second strategy is model routing. Different models have different strengths, latency profiles, context limits, tool-use behavior, and failure modes. A portfolio creates value when the system assigns tasks to the model most suited to them or combines genuinely complementary outputs.

Simply averaging model scores cannot outperform the best member by arithmetic alone. Any gain beyond the strongest individual model must come from task-dependent selection, diverse errors, or a nonlinear aggregation method. Claims that a portfolio has shifted the cost-quality frontier should therefore be evaluated together with the routing policy, traffic distribution, and selection procedure—not just the list of models.

Cascades make the routing decision sequential. A cheaper model attempts the task first; the system escalates only when the result fails a confidence or verification threshold. If $c_k$ is the incremental cost of stage $k$, the corrected expected cost is

$$
\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{cascade}}] = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Pr(\text{reach stage }k)\,c_k.
$$

The reach probability matters because a request that escalates to stage three has already incurred the costs of stages one and two. This formulation also reveals the central engineering problem: the stopping rule. A permissive threshold accepts too many weak answers and lowers quality.

A conservative threshold escalates almost everything and erases the economic benefit. Confidence must be calibrated on representative tasks, and the organization should measure false stops—cases in which the cascade ended early even though a stronger stage was needed.

Parallel execution offers a different tradeoff. Running several attempts concurrently can reduce wall-clock latency when the system stops after obtaining an acceptable answer, but it may consume more total computation. Specialist models can also improve efficiency on narrow, repetitive interactions, particularly when they incorporate task-specific constraints or verification.

They should not, however, be assumed superior merely because they are smaller. Their advantage must be demonstrated on the actual task distribution, including rare cases that fall outside the specialization boundary.

These choices form a Pareto frontier rather than a single universal winner. A configuration is attractive when no alternative is at least as good on every relevant dimension—quality, cost, and latency—and strictly better on at least one. The operating point depends on consequences.

Drafting an internal summary may tolerate a cheaper first pass; changing a customer record or producing a regulated filing may justify stronger verification and slower escalation. The frontier should also include human review cost, operational failures, and the expense of correcting downstream errors. Token price alone is an incomplete measure of economy.

Continuous optimization requires representative evaluation traffic. Teams can test combinations of models, retrieval, and execution strategies, identify non-dominated configurations, and select one that fits the use case. They should rerun that search when the traffic distribution, model behavior, or business constraint changes.

Public benchmarks such as BrowseComp-Plus or SWE-rebench can provide useful controlled surfaces, but they cannot substitute for domain-specific tasks and production traces. A configuration that looks efficient on a benchmark may fail when real inputs are longer, permissions are narrower, success criteria are ambiguous, or errors carry asymmetric costs.

The registry, evaluators, and shift detector are placeholders, but the control logic is the important part: compare configurations on representative traffic, select a non-dominated operating point, and repeat the search when the workload changes.

The comparison must include fully loaded cost. Token charges are visible, but retrieval infrastructure, tool execution, sandbox time, judge calls, retries, human escalation, and incident remediation can dominate the total. Latency should be decomposed as well: parallel sampling may improve the median while creating expensive tail behavior, and a cascade may reduce average cost while delaying the hardest cases.

Reporting only an average can hide the very requests for which the system is least dependable. Quality, cost, and latency should therefore be measured across task classes and percentiles, with uncertainty attached to small samples.

Organizations can turn those measurements into explicit service policies. A low-consequence drafting task might receive one inexpensive attempt and a lightweight factuality check. A customer-facing recommendation might receive retrieval, two diverse candidates, and a calibrated judge.

A regulated action might require deterministic validation and human authorization regardless of the model's confidence. These are resource-allocation decisions grounded in risk, not declarations that one model is universally safe. They also provide a graceful degradation path: when a preferred model or tool is unavailable, the system can fall back to a slower reviewed mode instead of silently weakening its controls.

Economical inference and reliability are therefore linked. Waste often signals a deeper design problem: unnecessary tool calls, redundant context, poorly calibrated retries, or a verifier that cannot distinguish progress from repetition. Reducing those costs can improve quality by preserving context and shortening the path to the relevant evidence.

The goal is not to make every request cheap. It is to spend computation where it changes the probability of a correct, useful, and safe outcome.

## Govern Autonomy

Efficiency makes deployment feasible, but deployment alone does not create trustworthy autonomy. A connector can give an agent access to a reporting system, customer database, ticketing platform, or code repository. Access alone does not supply the operational knowledge that experienced employees use: which reports they trust, which fields are stale, what sequence of checks reveals an anomaly, or how practice differs across countries and business units.

Access supplies reach. Organizational intelligence supplies judgment. Consider a reporting platform containing hundreds of reports. A generic connector may expose all of them through search or an API. An experienced analyst knows that only a small subset matters for a given decision, that one dashboard lags by a day, and that a particular combination of reports is needed at quarter end.

Encoding that knowledge can make an agent more useful than raw integration alone. Historical usage patterns, documented procedures, correction logs, and interviews with experts can all contribute to such a navigation model.

Capturing practice is not the same as copying it blindly. Logs contain historical accidents, workarounds, obsolete habits, and access patterns that may be inappropriate to reproduce. An organizational intelligence layer needs curation, provenance, ownership, and expiry.

Local experts should be able to correct it, and the system should distinguish a formal policy from a common habit. Otherwise, the agent will automate yesterday's inconsistency with greater speed.

This distinction clarifies what enterprises should buy and what they should build. Some foundation-model services, generic agent harnesses, and standard connectors are becoming more interchangeable, although switching costs, compliance requirements, data residency, and behavioral differences remain material. A company may sensibly obtain much of this infrastructure from vendors and replace components as the market changes.

Its durable asset is the encoded understanding of how it operates: specifications, terminology, exceptions, decision criteria, permissions, feedback, and the relationships among systems. That intelligence cannot be purchased as a generic package because it is precisely what makes one organization different from another.

Governance should follow a federated pattern. A central platform team can own identity, authorization, approved integrations, data boundaries, audit requirements, and common evaluation services. Business units can own the local context needed to perform their work: regional rules, customer-specific nuance, domain terminology, and task procedures.

Centralizing everything would create a knowledge bottleneck because no platform team can understand every operational detail. Decentralizing everything would create inconsistent controls, duplicated infrastructure, and shadow systems. The aim is a common spine with accountable intelligence at the edge.

Clear, usable governance is also an adoption tool. When approved infrastructure is difficult to access or policies are vague, competitive pressure encourages employees to install unapproved tools, paste sensitive material into consumer services, or build local automations without support.

Policy documents alone rarely prevent this. People need a practical path to experiment, clear rules about permitted data and actions, and a process for graduating useful prototypes into governed production services.

The required controls grow as an agent moves from conversation to action. A chat assistant is normally triggered by a person and produces text for that person to inspect. A more autonomous system may retain state across days, react to an event or schedule, and modify a real system without a new prompt. At that point, identity, permissions, versioning, and auditability become part of the product's behavior. The system must record whose authority it used, what information it accessed, which model and tools participated, what changed, and why the action was allowed.

Authority should be granted in stages. A new agent can begin in observation mode, where it reads approved data and compares its recommendation with what experts actually do. It can then move to draft mode, producing artifacts without sending or committing them, and later to sandboxed execution against reversible test systems.

Write access to production should follow only after the organization has measured performance on representative cases, defined rollback, and assigned an owner. Even then, permissions should remain narrow: the agent needs the least authority required for the task, for the shortest practical period, with transaction-level limits where appropriate. This staged path makes autonomy an evidence-based promotion rather than a feature toggle.

Context governance is equally important. Persistent memory can make an agent more coherent, but it can also retain outdated decisions, sensitive data, or information gathered under permissions that no longer apply. Every durable memory should have provenance, an access policy, and a retention or review rule.

When an employee changes roles, a customer withdraws consent, or a policy is superseded, the agent's effective context must change with the source system. Otherwise, a technically correct access check at action time may still rely on knowledge the agent should no longer possess.

Natural-language instructions are not sufficient enforcement. Inside a prompt, “be helpful” and “never call an unapproved API” are both strings. Repetition or capitalization does not turn the second instruction into a hard boundary.

Consequential constraints need an enforcement surface outside ordinary generation: permission checks, schema validation, allowlists, deterministic business rules, transaction limits, sandboxing, and refusal or escalation when a requirement cannot be satisfied. A second model can assist with review, but model-checks-model is still probabilistic and adds cost. It should not be mistaken for a guarantee.

Uncertainty creates a related challenge. A model's self-reported confidence is often poorly calibrated unless it has been validated on the target distribution. Retrieval can ground an answer, but the difficult part is deciding when retrieval, another tool, or human expertise is required.

Confidence estimates should therefore be calibrated against observed errors rather than accepted as certainty. Provenance matters as much as confidence: a reviewer needs to know whether a claim came from an approved record, a public source, a model's parametric memory, or an inference that combined several inputs.

Human oversight is most valuable at the boundary between thought and consequential action. Requiring approval after every retrieval or intermediate inference turns the reviewer into a step-by-step supervisor and destroys much of the benefit of delegation. Letting the agent research, compare, score, and draft can be appropriate when those steps are reversible and observable.

The system should then pause before sending a message, updating a record, moving money, rejecting a candidate, or making another action that is difficult to undo. The human receives a decision-ready package containing the proposed action, relevant evidence, uncertainty, and alternatives.

This placement does not make human approval equivalent to truth. Reviewers can be rushed, defer to confident language, or lack the information needed to catch a subtle error. Approval is an accountability and risk-control mechanism, not a proof of correctness.

High-stakes decisions still need appropriate evidence, deterministic checks where possible, and monitoring after execution. Some actions may require two-person approval or remain outside the agent's authority entirely.

Enterprise automation platforms illustrate both the promise and the limits of this design. Ema describes systems that can be triggered through APIs or schedules, maintain persistent context, route work across models, interact with business applications, and pause before external action. It has reported deployments in recruiting, employee support, health administration, and appointment scheduling.

A workshop demonstration assembled recruiting roles quickly, and a customer case study reported a substantial reduction in time to hire. These examples show how a non-technical domain expert might configure automation around a familiar process. They do not establish the quality ceiling for unfamiliar or heavily regulated work, and vendor-reported improvements should not be generalized without the baseline, sample, error distribution, and independent evaluation.

Governance continues after launch. Agent versions must be reproducible so that a regulated organization can explain when behavior changed. Audit logs should reveal new task types, tool failures, overrides, and drift.

Multi-tenant platforms need strict boundaries so that learning from one customer's failure never exposes that customer's data to another. Systems with many agents must also manage dependencies, conflicting actions, rate limits, and contention for shared resources. If a routing layer can switch models automatically, regulated users may need to see or restrict which model made a particular decision.

Outcome-based economics can align platform cost with business value more clearly than raw token billing, but it also creates incentives that require scrutiny. “Completed case” is not a meaningful unit if the system closes cases prematurely; “candidate contacted” is not valuable if the screening criterion is biased; “ticket resolved” can reward suppressing legitimate follow-up. The measured outcome must include quality, reversals, complaints, and downstream harm rather than merely throughput.

The governing principle is straightforward: autonomy should increase only when the system can constrain action, preserve evidence, and recover from failure at the same scale. Yet policies, permissions, and review checkpoints cannot by themselves show that an agent is effective.

They define where and how the system may operate; they do not prove that it completes the intended task, chooses the right tools, uses context efficiently, or improves a business outcome. The next requirement is therefore an evaluation architecture that treats production reality—not a single benchmark score—as the surface on which trust is earned.

## Replace Static Scores with Production Evidence

A benchmark can show that a model answered a curated question, but an agent is more than the model at its center. It receives incomplete instructions, retrieves context, chooses tools, changes state, recovers from errors, and sometimes acts on systems that matter. A single score at the end of that process hides the questions an enterprise actually needs answered: Did the agent finish the user's task?

Did it take a safe and efficient path? Would it succeed again under a slightly different input? Did the result improve a business outcome, or merely look impressive in a test environment?

That distinction is why agent evaluation has to move beyond static leaderboards. Curated benchmarks still provide useful common ground, especially when they contain realistic environments and well-audited tasks. They are poor substitutes, however, for evidence drawn from the work an organization intends to delegate.

Known test sets can be optimized until their scores separate from general usefulness. They can also become saturated, retain ambiguous graders, or represent a task distribution unlike the one encountered in production. The right response is not to discard benchmarks, but to place them inside a broader evidence system.

### Turn Production Use into a Controlled Experiment

Real usage supplies what a benchmark cannot: organic goals, messy language, changing context, multi-turn correction, and consequences that users care about. Arena-style evaluation uses those interactions as an experimental surface.

Instead of allowing users to select a system—which would confound model quality with user preference, expertise, and task type—the platform can conceal and randomly assign the model or orchestrator. The comparison then estimates how assignment changes an observed outcome rather than merely counting which system attracted which users. [“Agent Arena: Causal Evaluation of Agents in the Real World”](https://arena.ai/blog/agent-arena-methodology).

For an outcome signal $k$, the basic treatment effect is:

$$
\hat{\tau}_k = \mathbb{E}[Y_k \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_k \mid T=0]
$$

Here, $Y_k$ might record successful completion, a verified factual error, recovery after a tool failure, or a user correction that the agent handled properly. $T=1$ denotes assignment to the system being evaluated, while $T=0$ denotes assignment to a defined baseline. Randomization makes the contrast more credible, but it does not solve every statistical problem.

Repeated users create dependence between observations; task mix may change over time; exposure to one agent may alter later behavior; and a treatment can change when its prompts, tools, or model version are updated. A production analysis should therefore record the treatment version, cluster related sessions where appropriate, and report uncertainty rather than presenting a point estimate as a permanent ranking.

The outcome also needs careful definition. A completion button is valuable because it reflects the user's judgment, but it may reward an easy-to-accept answer rather than a correct one. Complaints and praise expose friction, yet quiet users may leave without either.

An automated fact checker can inspect extracted claims, but its verdict inherits the coverage and quality of its sources. Tool-error recovery reveals resilience, although repeated recovery can also hide an inefficient plan.

No single signal should stand in for agent quality. The stronger approach combines explicit feedback, trace-derived behavior, deterministic verification, and longer-term outcomes while preserving each measure separately enough to diagnose disagreement.

Using live traffic also creates obligations that an offline benchmark does not. Sensitive traces need access controls, retention rules, redaction, and a clear purpose. Randomized assignment must respect the risk of the task: testing two drafting assistants is different from silently experimenting with systems that influence medical, legal, financial, or employment decisions.

High-stakes deployments need stronger authorization, review, and rollback mechanisms. Evaluation is part of governance, not an exemption from it. [“A Practical Guide to Building AI Agents”](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-ai-agents/).

### Evaluate the Model, Harness, and Environment Together

Even a carefully randomized comparison can misidentify the object being measured. An agent's behavior depends on the combination of model, system instructions, tool descriptions, memory, retrieval, orchestration, permissions, and environment.

Changing the harness can alter performance without changing the underlying model; changing the model can expose assumptions embedded in the harness. Reporting “model quality” when only one convenient pairing was tested conceals that interaction.

A more informative evaluation spans the combinations that might actually be deployed. Exgentic's unified-protocol approach treats task, context, and actions as shared primitives, then uses adapters to connect otherwise incompatible agent and benchmark interfaces.

The precise protocol matters less than the experimental discipline it enables: test prioritized model-harness combinations across representative environments, preserve a privacy-controlled execution record sufficient for reproduction, and record both success and cost. An organization can then see whether a model is robust across harnesses or whether a particular pairing fails catastrophically on one class of tasks. [“General Agent Evaluation”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22953).

<details><summary>This represents the adapter boundary:</summary>

```python
class UnifiedProtocol:
    """Mediates between heterogeneous agent and benchmark protocols."""

    # Three universal primitives across all agents and benchmarks
    PRIMITIVES = ["task", "context", "actions"]

    def agent_to_unified(self, agent_message: AgentNativeFormat) -> UnifiedMessage:
        """Transform agent's native output into unified format."""
        return UnifiedMessage(
            task=self.extract_task(agent_message),
            context=self.extract_context(agent_message),
            actions=self.extract_actions(agent_message)
        )

    def unified_to_benchmark(self, unified: UnifiedMessage,
                              benchmark: Benchmark) -> BenchmarkNativeFormat:
        """Transform unified format into benchmark's expected format."""
        return benchmark.format_adapter(unified.task, unified.context, unified.actions)

    def run_evaluation(self, agent: Agent, benchmark: Benchmark, task: Task) -> float:
        # Agent runs in its native protocol
        agent_output = agent.run(task)  # Agent unchanged

        # Mediation: agent → unified → benchmark
        unified = self.agent_to_unified(agent_output)
        benchmark_input = self.unified_to_benchmark(unified, benchmark)

        # Benchmark evaluates in its native protocol
        outcome = benchmark.evaluate(benchmark_input)  # Benchmark unchanged
        return outcome.success_score
```

</details>

The classes are schematic, but the separation is useful: agents and benchmarks keep their native behavior while adapters translate task, context, and action semantics into a common contract.

This prevents two common procurement mistakes. The first is buying the model with the highest isolated benchmark score even though a cheaper model performs equally well inside the organization's harness. The second is assuming that an open or self-hosted model is automatically less expensive without accounting for serving, fine-tuning, observability, security, and operational labor.

Model selection is a configuration problem, not a brand decision. The relevant candidates lie on a cost-quality frontier: a configuration is non-dominated when no alternative is at least as capable and no more expensive for the target workload while strictly improving at least one of those dimensions.

The tasks themselves must also match the level of delegation. A coding benchmark made of well-specified, local fixes can measure implementation skill while missing the judgment expected from a senior engineer. Senior SWE-Bench moves toward underspecified requests, runtime-intensive bugs, and review criteria that include compatibility with repository conventions and unnecessary code, not only test-suite correctness.

The same principle applies outside software. A legal agent should face realistic ambiguity, source requirements, and escalation rules.

A support agent should encounter missing records, policy conflicts, and actions that require authorization. Difficulty is useful only when the task and its verifier remain valid; an opaque or unstable grader turns additional difficulty into noise. [Snorkel AI, Princeton, and UW–Madison, “Senior SWE-Bench”](https://senior-swe-bench.snorkel.ai/).

Task construction therefore deserves the same engineering care as the agent. Automated checks can detect non-determinism, broken fixtures, false passes, and false failures.

Domain experts must still judge whether the request is meaningful, whether the reference solution is acceptable, and whether the evaluation rewards the intended behavior. A benchmark with substantial unused difficulty is valuable only if that headroom reflects real capability rather than flaws in the test.

### Connect Diagnostics to Business Outcomes

Once the evaluation surface is representative, the next challenge is connecting low-level improvements to the result the organization values. A better retrieval score is not automatically useful.

It matters if it improves the completed task, and the completed task matters if it changes an operational outcome. Teams often optimize the easiest metric to measure, then discover that it has little causal relationship to the reason the agent was built.

A practical framework separates four levels of evidence. At the top are business outcomes such as resolution rate, processing time, cost per completed case, or review-ready documents produced. Beneath them is end-to-end task success: whether the agent completed the job correctly under realistic conditions.

Component evaluations inspect retrieval, extraction, field selection, tool use, or record updates. Diagnostics explain failure through measures such as grounding, terminology precision, recall, latency, or repeated tool calls.

The levels should remain connected but distinct. Diagnostics help find a change; component tests localize its effect; end-to-end evaluation decides whether the change improved the task; production outcomes reveal whether the task metric was worth optimizing.

This hierarchy should not be mistaken for a guaranteed mathematical chain. Relationships can be delayed, non-monotonic, or confounded. Higher retrieval recall may add distracting context and reduce task success.

A faster agent may cause users to submit more requests, changing the apparent cost per case. A task-success gain may fail to improve the business outcome because a downstream human queue is now the bottleneck. Teams need experiments and longitudinal evidence, not an assertion in code, to establish that an improvement propagates upward.

Evaluation should consequently be designed before capability work begins. The team first defines the business outcome, the end-to-end task, acceptable failure boundaries, and the evidence required for release. It then builds component tests and diagnostics that explain those outcomes.

This order prevents a familiar failure mode: developing an impressive agent and inventing a metric afterward that makes it look ready. Trace grading, reproducible datasets, and continuous evaluation tools make the discipline easier to operationalize, but the rubric still has to encode the organization's actual definition of quality.

Release criteria should reflect both average performance and the shape of failure. A system that succeeds on 98 percent of cases may still be unacceptable if the remaining two percent contains unauthorized payments, fabricated citations, or silent data loss. Before an agent receives write access, it can run in shadow mode: observe the same events as the live process, propose actions, and compare those proposals with actual outcomes without changing state.

A staged rollout can then limit permissions, transaction size, customer exposure, or task category while monitoring predefined rollback signals. This produces evidence about the deployed environment without making every early mistake consequential.

It also reveals where the evaluation suite is incomplete: systematic disagreement between shadow proposals and trusted operations should become new test cases, not merely a dashboard statistic. Risk-sensitive release gates turn evaluation from a one-time score into a controlled expansion of authority.

### Match the Grader to the Claim

Different outputs support different forms of verification. A database field, arithmetic result, schema, or program execution can often be checked deterministically. A summary's completeness or a recommendation's relevance may need a model judge working from a calibrated rubric.

Risk is a separate dimension: a deterministic result can still be consequential enough to require expert review. The goal is not to maximize automation; it is to use the least expensive base grader that remains trustworthy for the claim, then add human review where risk requires it.

The allocation can be stated compactly:

```math
G_{base}(r)=
\begin{cases}
Det(r), & r \in R_{det} \\
Judge(r, \rho), & r \in R_{semantic}
\end{cases},
\qquad
G_{final}(r)=
\begin{cases}
HumanReview(r,G_{base}(r)), & r \in R_{high\mbox{-}risk} \\
G_{base}(r), & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
```

The output class and risk threshold are governance decisions. A model judge should be compared with expert labels on representative examples, tested for positional and stylistic bias, and recalibrated when either the judge model or production distribution changes. Human review is not automatically ground truth: experts disagree, become fatigued, and can approve fluent errors.

High-risk labels may need adjudication, explicit confidence, and an audit trail. Deterministic checks are strongest when their specification is complete; a test suite can still certify the wrong behavior if it omits an important case.

The same logic applies at runtime. Hard constraints belong in authorization, schemas, policy engines, transaction boundaries, and other enforcement surfaces rather than in a paragraph that asks the model to comply. Human approval is most useful after the agent has assembled a decision-ready packet but before it performs an irreversible action.

The reviewer should see the proposed action, supporting evidence, auditable events, and evaluation result rather than supervise every reasoning step. Approval does not replace policy enforcement: final execution still checks permissions and hard constraints. This division lets the agent do useful cognitive work while preserving accountable control over state-changing behavior.

## Diagnose the Trajectory, Not Just the Answer

An agent can arrive at the right answer for the wrong reasons. It may read the same file repeatedly, call overlapping tools, wander through irrelevant documents, exhaust its context, and then recover by chance. A final-answer grader records success, but the trajectory predicts whether that success is repeatable, affordable, and safe.

In long-running systems, inefficient reasoning is not merely a cost problem. Every unnecessary branch consumes context and creates another opportunity to forget a constraint, act on stale information, or select an inappropriate tool.

### Expand the Failure Taxonomy

Final artifacts support three familiar questions: Is the answer correct? Is it complete? Is it useful?

Agent traces add a fourth: Was the path efficient and controlled? That category includes redundant retrieval, avoidable retries, tool loops, premature actions, failure to use available evidence, and recovery that depends on luck.

Two agents may produce indistinguishable reports while one uses a short, grounded path and the other spends ten times as many tokens navigating dead ends. Treating those runs as equal encourages brittle systems.

Trajectory analysis should retain enough structure to reconstruct decisions without requiring indiscriminate storage of sensitive reasoning. Useful events include tool calls and results, retrieved document identifiers, state transitions, validation outcomes, policy checks, latency, token consumption, and human interventions.

The trace should also preserve the version of the model, prompts, tools, and data indexes used during the run. Otherwise, a regression cannot be reproduced after one of those components changes.

Not every detour is waste. Exploration is necessary when the task is genuinely uncertain, and repeated checks may be appropriate before a consequential action. The evaluator must distinguish productive branching from avoidable churn.

A trajectory rubric can ask whether each tool call had a clear information need, whether later steps used the result, whether the agent recognized failure promptly, and whether a shorter valid path was available. Comparing successful and unsuccessful traces on matched tasks often reveals architecture problems that no prompt rewrite can fix.

### Reduce Discretion Where It Adds No Value

One recurring source of inconsistency is an oversized tool catalog. When several tools provide similar information, the model must make an arbitrary selection.

That choice can vary across otherwise identical runs, and each branch creates different downstream context. More tools may increase nominal capability while reducing predictability.

The remedy is not simply to minimize the tool count. Universally required context should be gathered during a deterministic setup phase, leaving the agent to choose among a smaller set of genuinely distinct tools. In a diagnostic system, topology, index metadata, and active connections may always be required; loading them automatically removes a pointless decision.

Later tools can remain discretionary when they answer different questions, such as examining a query plan, memory pressure, or a slow-query log. Tool descriptions should state preconditions, outputs, side effects, and failure behavior so the agent can choose based on semantics rather than names.

Knowledge architecture deserves the same treatment. Documentation written for humans often distributes one diagnostic path across tutorials, reference pages, release notes, and support articles. An agent forced to search that corpus freely can enter long, irrelevant branches.

An agent-oriented playbook first maps a user concern to a normalized symptom, then routes the agent to a focused handbook with ordered checks and explicit stopping conditions. The model still interprets the situation; the playbook constrains where it searches and how it confirms resolution.

Redis reported that this combination of narrower tools and diagnostic playbooks reduced token use and errors in its Spectre case study. Those figures should be read as results for one system and task distribution, not as universal constants. The transferable mechanism is more important than the percentage: fewer dead ends preserve context, and preserved context lowers the chance that relevant evidence or instructions disappear before the final decision.

### Treat Efficiency as a Reliability Signal

Cost and reliability are often presented as competing objectives, but trajectory waste can make them reinforce one another. Repeated sampling, verification, and stronger models may legitimately increase cost when they buy measurable accuracy.

Dead-end search is different: it spends tokens without increasing evidence, raises latency, and makes failure more likely through context pressure. A cost dashboard that records only total tokens cannot distinguish the two.

Useful accounting separates productive work from retries, abandoned branches, duplicated retrieval, and tool errors. Teams can then ask whether a more expensive run earned its cost through additional verification or merely wandered.

The comparison should include fully loaded latency and infrastructure cost, not only input and output tokens. It should also account for the downstream cost of error: a cheaper run that creates manual cleanup or an incorrect system update may be the more expensive operating choice.

Efficiency signals are especially useful for regression detection. A model or prompt update may leave final-answer accuracy unchanged while doubling tool calls or increasing recovery loops. That is an early warning that the system has become less stable.

Conversely, a playbook that shortens trajectories without reducing task success creates both economic and reliability headroom. The saved budget can fund verification where it actually changes outcomes.

### Make the Evaluation Suite a Living Asset

An evaluation set begins with expert examples, but it cannot remain frozen. Production introduces new vocabulary, rare edge cases, integration changes, adversarial inputs, and tasks that no original designer anticipated.

Traces should feed a governed process that selects representative failures and novel successes for review. Experts can turn them into regression tests, clarify rubrics, and identify missing tool or policy coverage.

Synthetic generation can expand a small expert seed set, but generated cases should not be treated as self-validating ground truth. The useful pattern is to vary an expert example, then require expert validation before admitting the result to a high-confidence suite.

Diversity matters as much as volume. Hundreds of paraphrases of an easy case do less for reliability than a few carefully chosen examples that exercise different constraints, environments, and failure consequences.

The suite should include end-to-end tasks, component checks, trajectory rubrics, safety tests, and action-boundary tests. It should run before releases and continuously on sampled production-like traffic.

Results need slices by task type, language, customer configuration, tool availability, and risk level; an aggregate average can hide a severe regression in a small but important group. When the production distribution shifts, the evaluation distribution should change deliberately, with old cases retained where they protect previously learned behavior.

This living corpus becomes part of the organization's proprietary intelligence. It records not just what good output looks like, but which failures matter, which evidence is required, where humans must intervene, and how the operating environment evolves. Models and harnesses can be replaced; a mature definition of quality is much harder to buy.

### Govern the Feedback Loop

Evaluation can itself be gamed. If a model repeatedly sees the same tests, it may learn the surface form without gaining the underlying capability. If a judge rewards confidence, the agent may become more persuasive rather than more accurate.

If user satisfaction is the only reward, the system may tell users what they want to hear. Independent checks, rotating holdouts, adversarial cases, and separation between development and release gates reduce those risks. Organizations should also monitor the evaluators. Deterministic checks need coverage audits. Model judges need periodic agreement studies against experts.

Human review needs sampling for inconsistency and fatigue. Business metrics need causal scrutiny when other processes change. No layer is permanently trustworthy merely because it was labeled an evaluation layer.

The resulting discipline is less like administering a final exam and more like operating a control system. Production supplies evidence; traces reveal mechanisms; layered graders test different claims; policy gates constrain action; and reviewed failures update the suite. Reliability emerges from the interaction of these parts, not from one benchmark number or a promise embedded in a prompt.

That systems view becomes even more important at the frontier of mathematics. There, trajectories can extend across thousands of dependent steps, rewards may remain sparse until a proof is complete, and a plausible final answer has almost no value unless it can be checked. Advanced mathematical reasoning is therefore a useful stress test for the same principles: structured search, efficient exploration, independent verification, and a feedback loop that cannot be satisfied by fluency alone.

## Mathematics as the Stress Test

Mathematics exposes both the promise and the limits of agentic reasoning. A final proof can be checked against exact rules, giving mathematical agents a stronger oracle than most enterprise systems possess.

Discovery remains difficult because the useful idea may lie behind a vast search space, distant concepts must be combined, and a single unnoticed error can invalidate everything that follows. Mathematics therefore tests the full architecture: decomposition, inference-time search, tool use, reward design, formalization, and independent review.

### Long Horizons and Sparse Rewards

Long reasoning chains are fragile even when each local step appears reliable. If a trajectory has $H$ dependent steps and $p_t$ is the conditional probability that step $t$ is valid given the preceding work, then

$$
P_{\text{success}}(H)=\prod_{t=1}^{H}p_t=p_{\text{step}}^{H}
$$

under the simplifying assumption that every step has the same independent fidelity. Real proofs violate that assumption: errors are correlated, some lemmas carry much more weight than others, and checking an intermediate result can reset part of the risk. The product nevertheless captures the core danger.

A small local error rate becomes unacceptable when thousands of dependencies remain unexamined. Longer free-form generation is not, by itself, a strategy for reliable research.

Sparse feedback makes the problem harder. In elementary exercises, a solver may receive useful signals after each calculation. At the research frontier, an attempted construction can look promising for a long time before one invariant fails, and a proof may receive no decisive reward until its final statement is accepted.

Naive sampling then spends most of its budget on trajectories that provide little information about how to improve. The system needs intermediate objects that are independently testable: lemmas, candidate constructions, bounded computations, proof obligations, or counterexamples to restricted cases.

A long horizon is also different from a large batch. Solving a thousand unrelated one-hour exercises can be parallelized without preserving much shared state; completing one investigation in which each lemma changes the next search decision cannot. The second task demands durable memory, provenance for intermediate results, and a way to revise downstream work when an early assumption fails.

Local verification becomes a form of fault containment. Instead of trusting a million-token trajectory as a single object, the system commits only checked intermediate claims and records the dependencies that would need to be reopened after a correction.

METR's Time Horizon 1.1 offers a useful but limited way to think about this difficulty. Its horizon is the human-expert completion time of tasks at which an agent is predicted to succeed at a specified rate; it is not the amount of clock time the agent runs autonomously.

The September 2026 version uses a larger suite drawn mainly from software engineering, machine learning, and cybersecurity, and METR warns that estimates above sixteen hours are unreliable with the current tasks. The organization also stresses that clean, automatically scored tasks do not represent all intellectual labor or entire jobs. [“Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier AI Models”](https://metr.org/time-horizons/).

That distinction matters for mathematics. A six-hour human baseline on a self-contained problem does not imply six hours of autonomous research, and a trend measured on software tasks cannot simply be extrapolated to an open conjecture.

Research difficulty depends on prior context, the distribution of promising ideas, access to the right literature, and whether progress can be verified before the end. Time horizons are evidence about increasing task complexity, not a universal law of discovery.

### Hierarchy, Test-Time Search, and Reward Hacking

Hierarchy turns a nearly invisible endpoint into reachable subgoals. Instead of searching directly for an object satisfying several rare properties at once, a system can first enter a broad class satisfying one property, then explore changes that preserve it while seeking the next.

A proof search can similarly separate conjecture generation, lemma selection, local derivation, and final checking. Each level narrows the decision space and creates feedback before the entire theorem is complete.

<details><summary>This illustrates hierarchical reward shaping with a two-stage monomial-ideal search:</summary>

```python
class MonomialIdealSearchHRL:
    """Hierarchical RL framework for discovering combinatorial objects satisfying dual invariants."""

    def __init__(self, property_A_checker, property_B_checker):
        self.agent_generator = SubgoalGeneratorPolicy()  # Optimizes for Property A
        self.agent_refiner = RefinementPolicy()         # Searches constrained manifold for Property B
        self.check_A = property_A_checker
        self.check_B = property_B_checker

    def search(self, search_budget: int) -> MonomialIdeal:
        for iteration in range(search_budget):
            # Step 1: High-level agent produces candidate satisfying Property A
            candidate_ideal = self.agent_generator.sample_candidate()
            if not self.check_A(candidate_ideal):
                self.agent_generator.update_reward(-1.0)
                continue

            # Property A satisfied: compute intermediate shaped reward
            self.agent_generator.update_reward(+1.0)

            # Step 2: Low-level agent performs targeted mutations preserving A while hunting for B
            refined_ideal = self.agent_refiner.explore_subspace(
                seed_ideal=candidate_ideal,
                constraint_fn=self.check_A,
                target_fn=self.check_B,
                max_steps=500
            )

            if refined_ideal is not None and self.check_B(refined_ideal):
                # Discovered the needle in the haystack
                return refined_ideal

        raise TimeoutError("Failed to discover ideal satisfying both properties within budget.")
```

</details>

The policies and mathematical checkers are abstract, but the decomposition is concrete: obtain a verified candidate in region $A$, then search within the constraint-preserving neighborhood for property $B$.

Inference-time computation makes that hierarchy operational. A capable system can pursue several hypotheses, call symbolic or numerical tools, test intermediate claims, abandon failed branches, and allocate more effort to approaches that survive checking. The important resource is not a longer monologue but a controlled search process with memory and verifiers.

Parallel attempts help when plausible strategies are diverse; deeper search helps when later steps depend tightly on earlier choices. Both are useful only if the system can recognize progress rather than reward fluent repetition.

Search budgets should therefore be observable and reproducible. A research claim is easier to assess when reviewers know how many problems were attempted, how many independent runs were made, which tools and literature were available, how candidates were selected, and whether failed branches were retained.

More inference-time compute raises the probability of finding rare ideas, but it also raises the number of opportunities to exploit a faulty evaluator or to select a lucky-looking artifact after many attempts. Reported success must be interpreted together with the search procedure, not as though the final proof appeared in one unaided pass.

The May 2026 result on the planar unit-distance problem demonstrates what cross-disciplinary search can produce. The classical question asks for the maximum number of pairs at exactly unit distance among $n$ points in the plane. For decades, the prevailing conjecture was that rescaled-grid constructions were essentially optimal, with only a vanishing improvement in the exponent over linear growth.

An internal OpenAI model produced an infinite family with a fixed polynomial improvement: for infinitely many values of $n$, the number of unit-distance pairs is at least $n$ raised to one plus some fixed positive exponent. The proof drew unexpectedly on algebraic number theory, including class-field-tower ideas, and was checked by external mathematicians who supplied companion remarks. OpenAI describes the system as a general-purpose reasoning model rather than one specialized or scaffolded for this problem. [“An OpenAI Model Has Disproved a Central Conjecture in Discrete Geometry”](https://openai.com/index/model-disproves-discrete-geometry-conjecture/)

This corrects a common misdescription of the result. It was not merely a counterexample to an unspecified linear bound or a constrained variant of the problem.

It refuted the longstanding near-linear-growth conjecture for the classical planar unit-distance function. The result is important not only because the conclusion changed, but because the construction connected mature ideas from one field to a central question in another.

Later announcements require a more cautious status label. In October 2026, OpenAI released AI-generated work claiming results in harmonic analysis, complexity theory, and quantum field theory, including a Lean formalization associated with a proposed refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis.

OpenAI itself described the manuscripts as raw research data and organized different forms of external stewardship and open examination. As of November 16, 2026, those claims should be presented as ongoing community investigations, not settled theorems merely because artifacts or formalizations have been published.

This is where reward design becomes a safety issue for science. A mathematical agent can exploit a weak checker by proving a vacuous variant, dropping a hypothesis, changing a quantifier, triggering a parser edge case, or optimizing a proxy that correlates with rigor only on familiar examples. Formal syntax makes some loopholes easier to detect, but it also creates a sharp target for specification gaming.

Robust systems need independent statement review, adversarial tests, sandboxed tools, and multiple checks whose blind spots do not coincide. The objective is not to produce something a verifier accepts. It is to preserve the intended problem while making every inferential step checkable.

## Close the Loop with Formal Semantics

Formal methods convert a proof from persuasive prose into an object a small trusted kernel can check. In a theorem prover such as Lean, definitions, assumptions, quantifiers, and inference rules must be explicit.

Acceptance establishes that the conclusion follows from the encoded premises within the formal system. It does not establish that the premises are empirically true, that the theorem statement matches the mathematician's intention, or that a translation from natural language preserved every qualification.

That semantic boundary is now central. An auto-formalizer can generate syntactically valid code while weakening the claim it was supposed to encode. A proof of the wrong theorem may be perfectly machine-checked.

Human experts therefore remain responsible for statement design, correspondence with the source problem, and the significance of the result. Formalization reduces one class of uncertainty—whether the derivation follows—but it cannot, on its own, eliminate ambiguity about what was asked.

Even derivational certainty is relative to a trusted base. Reviewers must know which axioms, library theorems, definitions, compiler semantics, and kernel implementation the artifact depends on. A proof can import a lemma with stronger assumptions than its informal name suggests, or rely on a model that omits behavior present in the physical system.

Good formal practice makes these dependencies inspectable, minimizes the trusted base, and separates generated proof material from the small components that must be accepted without proof. This is analogous to enterprise governance: confidence increases when authority and assumptions are explicit rather than buried in a large opaque system.

The November 2026 AlphaProof Code report shows how far this architecture can extend beyond pure mathematics. Google DeepMind reports using a human-guided specification process, a formal semantics of AArch64 machine code, and autonomous proving agents to verify key security properties of the compiled Signal implementation. The system generated about 6.5 million lines of Lean proof resting on roughly 26,000 lines of trusted specifications and processor semantics.

The checked properties cover the PQXDH handshake and Triple Ratchet, including authentication, forward secrecy, post-compromise security, and handshake secrecy. The formal artifacts were released for inspection. [“AlphaProof Code: Verifying Signal’s Security at the Machine-Code Level”](https://deepmind.google/documents/36/Formal_Verification_of_Signal_4.pdf).

The achievement is substantial precisely because it connects approved security statements to unmodified compiled code rather than stopping at a high-level protocol sketch. Its trust boundary is still visible.

Experts must judge the specifications, the formal processor model, environmental assumptions, and the set of observable behaviors. The Lean kernel can reject an invalid proof term; it cannot decide whether the approved property omitted a threat that users care about.

The same distinction governs program translation. The ideal equivalence condition is

$$
Equiv(P_{\mathrm{src}},P_{\mathrm{tgt}})\iff
\forall \sigma\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{admissible}},\;
Obs(P_{\mathrm{src}},\sigma)=Obs(P_{\mathrm{tgt}},\sigma).
$$

Here, the source and target programs must have the same observable behavior for every admissible initial state. A finite test suite, even one generated by symbolic execution, establishes only agreement on the paths and observations it covers. Databases, files, concurrency, timing, undefined behavior, and external services can all sit outside that boundary.

Full equivalence therefore requires either exhaustive coverage under a precise environment model or a deliberately narrower claim. Formal semantics is most trustworthy when it makes that scope explicit rather than disguising testing as proof.

## Autonomy Must Be Earned

The path from deployment to proof is a sequence of expanding obligations. An enterprise first has to define the work, capture the context that experienced people use, and grant agents only the authority their evidence supports. It then has to measure the complete system under representative conditions: model, harness, tools, memory, environment, cost, latency, and human escalation.

Mathematics adds the hardest test. Long horizons punish small errors, sparse rewards make search inefficient, and a formal checker can validate the wrong statement with perfect rigor.

The answer is not to choose between human judgment and machine verification. Each covers a different failure surface. People decide which problem matters, whether a specification reflects the intended goal, what consequences are acceptable, and when an apparent result has earned scientific or operational trust.

Machines can explore more branches, preserve detailed traces, enforce deterministic constraints, and check derivations at a scale people cannot inspect line by line. The strongest systems place those capabilities in a loop: explicit intent guides search; search produces inspectable artifacts; evaluation exposes failure; formal or deterministic checks constrain action; and human review governs meaning and consequence.

This architecture also explains why autonomy cannot be inferred from an impressive result. A model that discovers a proof may still be unreliable on a nearby problem. An agent that succeeds in a sandbox may fail when permissions, data quality, or incentives change.

A machine-checked theorem may faithfully follow premises that do not capture the intended claim. Trust comes from bounded claims, reproducible evidence, clear ownership, and the ability to stop or reverse the system when those conditions fail.

Enterprise governance, production evaluation, and formal proof are therefore not separate disciplines. They are layers of the same contract between capability and consequence. Governance defines the permitted space; evaluation measures behavior inside it; formal methods close the parts of the loop that can be stated exactly.

Autonomy should expand only as all three mature. The durable goal is not an AI system that acts without people, but one whose increasingly independent actions remain legible, testable, constrained, and accountable to them.
