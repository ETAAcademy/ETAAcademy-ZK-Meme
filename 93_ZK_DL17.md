# Building Physical Intelligence: Humanoid Foundation Models, Generative Control, Morphological Design, and Human Data

_Robots will not become generally capable through data scale alone. Progress depends on aligning training data, policy architecture, real-time control, and physical morphology into a coherent embodied system._

Physical AI is entering a phase in which stronger hardware and larger models are no longer sufficient explanations for progress. A useful robot must connect perception, language, locomotion, manipulation, and contact-rich control while operating under strict latency and stability constraints. Improvements at one layer can easily fail at another: more demonstrations do not prevent compounding control error, a capable policy can still produce discontinuous actions, and a sophisticated controller may be solving a problem that better morphology could have removed.

Four research directions make this systems problem concrete. It combines egocentric pre-training, whole-body action generation, and latency-aware deployment in an open foundation model for humanoid loco-manipulation. The theory of the algorithmic Moravec's paradox explains why action chunking and iterative generative policies change the stability of behavior cloning rather than merely increasing model capacity. Work on morphological intelligence shows that legs, body geometry, and passive dynamics can serve as physical forms of computation. Finally, EgoMimic, EgoBridge, EMMA, EgoScale, and EgoVerse outline how natural human experience can become a scalable source of robot supervision.

A foundation model for humanoid loco-manipulation needs more than a larger action head. Egocentric pre-training narrows the observation and morphology gap, MM-DiT Hat gives vision-language and action features a shared computational path, and inpainting-based chunking makes the output executable in real time. The algorithmic Moravec's paradox explains why these deployment choices are fundamental: without open-loop stability, temporal structure, and a mechanism for returning predictions toward the expert manifold, supervised accuracy can coexist with catastrophic rollout error.

Morphological intelligence adds another layer to the same argument. Redundant legs, compliant dynamics, body proportions, and contact patterns determine how much uncertainty software must absorb. The relevant optimization target is therefore not controller performance in isolation, but the joint mechanical and computational cost of reliable behavior.

Human experience supplies the route to scale, but only after alignment. Viewpoint, reference frame, timing, embodiment, and contact geometry all mediate whether an egocentric demonstration becomes useful robot supervision. EgoBridge's temporal alignment, EMMA's kinematic retargeting, EgoScale's staged training, and multi-embodiment pre-training each solve a different part of that translation problem.

Together, these efforts support one thesis: embodied capability emerges from co-design. Data must match the robot's viewpoint and action space; learning algorithms must remain stable under their own predictions; deployment must reconcile slow inference with fast control; and morphology must be treated as part of the intelligence budget. Once those conditions are in place, scale becomes productive rather than merely expensive.

## Part I: Open Foundation Model for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

Modern robotics is advancing rapidly, driven by converging progress in hardware, algorithms, and data. Yet humanoid robots still cannot perform real-world loco-manipulation — the unified capability of locomotion and manipulation — because these skills have not been integrated into a single model. An open foundation model for humanoid loco-manipulation that addresses this gap, introduces a scalable data pipeline built on egocentric human video for pre-training, a novel MM-DiT Hat architecture for action generation, and a real-time chunking deployment system designed to eliminate action jitter. The project reports a 40% improvement over GROOT 1.6 while using only 10% of its training data.

Three converging forces are propelling the explosive growth of modern robotics: **hardware**, **algorithms**, and **data**. Commercial successes span the spectrum — from Waymo's self-driving vehicles to Unitree's agile G1 humanoid and dexterous robotic manipulators — all driven by recent Vision-Language Action (VLA) models. VLA architectures take multi-modal inputs (camera images and language instructions) and output robot action commands, effectively extending large language models with a perception backbone and an action-output head.

Despite this progress, a critical gap remains. Humanoid robots must simultaneously perform **locomotion** (moving through space), **manipulation** (interacting with objects), and **intelligence** (language-driven reasoning about what to do). These capabilities have not been unified into a single model. The core quantitative challenge is the dimensional mismatch in action spaces: the Unitree G1 humanoid requires approximately 43 degrees of freedom (DoF) for whole-body control — 7 per arm, 3 per wrist, 6 per leg, plus torso — while standard VLA models handle only about 18 DoF for bimanual manipulation. This gap means existing VLA models cannot be deployed on humanoids off the shelf.

Ci0's mission is to bridge this gap with a large-scale VLA whose Stage 2 action expert generates whole-body robot joint trajectories rather than a manipulation-only output. The teleoperation system used to collect its training data still relies on hierarchical upper- and lower-body control, as described later. The project draws on the established **π (Pi) practice**: a two-stage training blueprint where a vision-language backbone is first pre-trained on large-scale data, then fine-tuned with an action expert on in-domain robot demonstrations. Ci0 departs from this baseline in three fundamental ways: (1) it replaces internet video pre-training with egocentric human video, (2) it introduces the MM-DiT Hat architecture for richer cross-modal feature interaction, and (3) it implements a training-time inpainting technique for smooth real-time deployment.

### The Data Pyramid and Open Challenges

#### The Three-Layer Data Hierarchy

Robot training data can be organized into a hierarchical taxonomy ordered by quality and cost:

| Layer  | Source                     | Pros                                            | Cons                                 |
| :----- | :------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- |
| Bottom | Internet video             | Abundant, free, contains manipulation knowledge | Viewpoint mismatch; no action labels |
| Middle | Simulation (Isaac, MuJoCo) | Infinitely scalable, no physical risk           | Sim-to-real gap (physics + visual)   |
| Top    | Real-world teleoperation   | High quality, directly usable for imitation     | Expensive; limited scale             |

The insight that drives Ci0's data strategy is that **for manipulation**, the top layer (teleoperation) is the only reliable source, but it does not scale easily, while **for locomotion**, the middle layer (simulation) is sufficient. The challenge is finding a pre-training data source that sits between these extremes — scalable like internet video, but with the observation and action alignment of teleoperation data.

#### Open Questions in the π Practice

The standard π pipeline (pre-train VLM on internet data → post-train action expert on teleoperation) leaves several crucial questions unanswered:

1. Can an alternative pre-training data source outperform internet video?
2. Can alternative training stages (beyond the two-stage paradigm) be explored?
3. Should actions be tokenized as discrete tokens or kept as continuous signals?
4. Is continuous flow matching always optimal for large action spaces?

#### The Deployment Bottleneck

Naïve VLA deployment on a humanoid produces two failure modes: **action pause** (the robot freezes while waiting for VLA inference) and **action jitter** (discontinuous joint-angle jumps when switching from one predicted action chunk to the next). Both stem from the inference delay of large models, which creates a training/test-time mismatch. The problem is not model quality — it is the inference latency gap between observation capture and action execution.

#### Flow Matching for Action Generation

Action expert is trained with a flow matching objective. Unlike diffusion models that use stochastic noise processes, flow matching learns a deterministic velocity field that transports samples from a simple prior to the target action distribution:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, x_0, x_1} \left[ \left\| v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \right\|^2 \right]
$$

where $x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1$ is the linear interpolation between source sample $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and target action $x_1$. At inference, numerically integrating $v_\theta$ from $t=0$ to $t=1$ generates a clean action from noise. The action vector $x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{43}$ for humanoids versus $x_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{18}$ for standard VLA represents a fundamental incompatibility that must overcome.

### Egocentric Human Data for Pre-Training

#### Why Egocentric Video?

Instead of internet video, Ci0 uses egocentric human videos captured via Apple Vision Pro or a custom four-camera headset. This choice is motivated by two forms of alignment:

**Observation alignment**: The egocentric (first-person) viewpoint is geometrically close to the camera placement on a humanoid robot's head, creating a natural domain match between pre-training and deployment observations.

**Action alignment**: Human hand keypoints and humanoid dexterous hand keypoints share a similar morphological structure — both have five fingers with roughly similar joint configurations. This enables a shared keypoint representation across human and robot hands.

#### Scalability

The data collection pipeline is designed for scale:

| Collection device       | Scale                           | Key property                       |
| :---------------------- | :------------------------------ | :--------------------------------- |
| Apple Vision Pro        | Existing dataset (EgoDex, 829h) | High-quality 3D hand tracking      |
| Custom 4-camera headset | 5 hours/person/day              | Doesn't disrupt human productivity |
| Current collection      | ~1,000 hours (≈20 devices)      | Ongoing                            |

The custom headset is cheap and unobtrusive: two stereo pairs (forward-facing and downward-facing) connected to a Raspberry Pi. The person simply wears it while doing daily tasks. At 5 hours per person per day with 20 devices, the system can collect 100 hours per day, reaching 1,000 hours in just 10 days.

#### Hand Representation

Each hand is represented as a 24-DoF action vector:

```math
\mathbf{a}_{\text{hand}} = \left[ \underbrace{\mathbf{r}_{\text{wrist}}}_{\in \mathbb{R}^9} \,\Big|\, \underbrace{\mathbf{p}_{\text{tip},1}, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_{\text{tip},5}}_{\in \mathbb{R}^{3\times5}} \right]
```

where $r_{\text{wrist}} \in R^9$ is the wrist rotation (a $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix flattened) and $p_{\text{tip},k} \in R^3$ is the 3D position of the $k$-th fingertip. This yields $\dim(a_{\text{hand}}) = 9 + 3 \times 5 = 24$ DoF per hand, and 48 DoF for bimanual hand action. This representation is designed to remain compact enough for tokenization while retaining manipulation-relevant hand poses.

The dimensionality is stage-specific. The 48-DoF vector is the bimanual human-hand representation used during egocentric pre-training; the Stage 2 action expert described later generates 43-DoF robot whole-body joint trajectories. The two spaces should not be treated as interchangeable.

During pre-training, Ci0 uses **one-step action prediction** only — not long-horizon prediction. The rationale is that even single-step prediction can give the model a strong understanding of the task while saving substantial computation.

### Teleoperation System and the HumEveryday Dataset

#### Full-Body Teleoperation Rig

To collect high-quality humanoid loco-manipulation data, the team built a full-body teleoperation system combining VR, IMU, and data glove technologies:

| Component                         | Purpose                                         |
| :-------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
| VR Headset (Pico)                 | Head pose tracking → camera viewpoint for robot |
| Wrist motion trackers (IMU-based) | Wrist position for upper-body IK                |
| Full-body motion trackers (IMU)   | Torso + leg pose for lower-body commands        |
| MANUS data glove                  | High-precision finger motion capture            |

#### Hierarchical Control Mapping

The control system is split into two sub-systems. For the **upper body** (arms and hands), finger motion from the MANUS glove maps directly to the humanoid hand through joint mapping, while wrist position from trackers and head position from the VR headset are fed into a multi-target inverse kinematics (IK) solver to compute arm joint angles. The IK problem is formulated as:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{q}} = \arg\min_{\mathbf{q}} \sum_{k} \left\| f_k(\mathbf{q}) - \mathbf{T}_k^{\text{target}} \right\|^2 + \lambda \left\| \mathbf{q} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{ref}} \right\|^2
$$

where $f_k(\mathbf{q})$ is the forward kinematics of end-effector $k$, $\mathbf{T}_k^{\text{target}}$ is the target pose from the trackers, and the regularization term keeps the solution close to a reference pose to prevent large joint jumps.

For the **lower body** (legs and base), torso motion is converted into a velocity command and foot yaw, which is fed into an AMO-style (Agile Motion Omni-directional) RL locomotion policy that handles low-level leg joint control. This is the hierarchical teleoperation and data-collection stack: upper-body retargeting produces manipulation commands, while a separately trained RL policy handles low-level legged locomotion. It is distinct from the Stage 2 action expert's 43-DoF learned output representation.

#### Control Loop Optimization

A critical systems-engineering contribution was reducing the control loop delay from 500ms to 20ms — a 25× improvement — by offloading I/O operations from the control loop. At 500ms, a human teleoperator must "lead" the robot's motion by half a second, which is highly unnatural. The project reports that 20ms made the latency essentially imperceptible and halved operator effort.

#### The HumEveryday Dataset

The resulting dataset, HumEveryday, contains over 10,000 trajectories, approximately 3 million frames, 260 tasks, and captures RGB, depth, LiDAR, joint states, tactile, odometry, and IMU modalities across both Unitree G1 and H1 robots. The reported HumEveryday benchmarks show VLA models outperforming the evaluated imitation-learning baselines (Diffusion Policy and 3D Diffusion Policy), with GROOT 1.5 achieving the best overall performance. However, all evaluated methods fail on the most challenging fine-grained tasks, leaving substantial room for improvement.

#### Cloud Evaluation Platform

To lower the barrier to entry, it provides a cloud evaluation service: users upload their policy to a server, and the team evaluates it on a real robot and returns the success rate. This enables fair, reproducible comparison without requiring robot ownership.

### Pre-Training with EgoDex and the FAST Tokenizer

#### Pre-Training Pipeline

Stage 1 pre-trains a Vision-Language Model (VLM) on 829 hours of EgoDex egocentric video. The pipeline is:

```text
EgoDex egocentric video (829h)
     ↓ (input: video frames + text descriptions)
VLM backbone
     ↓ (output: discrete action tokens)
FAST tokenizer (fine-tuned)  ←→  48-DoF hand action ground truth
```

The VLM backbone is trained with a standard cross-entropy objective over discrete action tokens, predicting only one step at a time. The key enabler is the FAST (Frequency-space Action Sequence Tokenizer), which compresses the 48-DoF continuous action space into a compact sequence of discrete tokens.

#### The FAST Tokenizer Pipeline

FAST converts continuous action sequences into discrete tokens through three stages:

**Stage 1 — Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)**. The action sequence is projected onto cosine basis functions:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbf{a}_t \cdot \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{T}\left(t + \frac{1}{2}\right)k\right), \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, T-1
$$

Since robot actions are smooth (low-frequency), most of the signal energy concentrates in the first few DCT coefficients. Retaining only the first $K \ll T$ coefficients dramatically reduces dimensionality with minimal information loss. This is analogous to JPEG compression applied column-wise to the action matrix.

**Stage 2 — Vector Quantization (VQ)**. Each retained DCT coefficient is mapped to the nearest entry in a learned codebook:

$$
z_k = \text{VQ}(\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k) = \arg\min_{e \in \mathcal{C}} \|\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_k - e\|
$$

This converts the continuous representation into discrete integer tokens.

**Stage 3 — Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)**. Frequent consecutive token pairs are merged into super-tokens, further reducing sequence length.

The off-the-shelf FAST tokenizer was originally trained on lower-dimensional bimanual manipulation data. Ci0 fine-tunes it on 500,000 randomly sampled 48-DoF actions from EgoDex. The fine-tuned version achieves lower reconstruction loss but produces longer token sequences (~13 tokens vs. the original's shorter sequences), since more tokens are needed to represent the richer 48-DoF action space. The overall compression from 48 continuous dimensions to approximately 13 discrete tokens represents a ~3.7× reduction in dimensionality, with additional compression over the temporal dimension.

<details><summary>The tokenization pipeline can be expressed:</summary>

```python
# FAST tokenizer pipeline
def fast_tokenize(action_sequence):
    # action_sequence: shape [T, 48]

    # Stage 1: DCT (applied per action dimension)
    dct_coeffs = dct(action_sequence, axis=0)   # [T, 48]
    dct_truncated = dct_coeffs[:K, :]           # [K, 48], K << T

    # Stage 2: Vector Quantization (flatten and quantize)
    flat = dct_truncated.flatten()              # [K*48]
    tokens = [codebook.nearest(v) for v in chunks(flat)]

    # Stage 3: Byte-Pair Encoding
    compressed_tokens = bpe_encode(tokens)

    return compressed_tokens  # ~13 tokens
```

</details>

This converts a continuous 48-DoF action trajectory into approximately 13 discrete integer tokens. Line 2 implements the DCT transform, line 3 performs the K-coefficient truncation, line 6 implements the VQ step, and line 9 applies BPE compression.

### Post-Training with the MM-DiT Hat

#### Stage 2 Setup

In Stage 2, the pre-trained VLM backbone is frozen and a new action expert is trained from scratch on 30 hours of HumEveryday teleoperation data. The action expert uses flow matching to generate continuous 43-DoF whole-body joint-angle trajectories, conditioned on features from the frozen VLM backbone. The post-training loss is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{post}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I),\, x_1 \sim p_{\text{data}}} \left[ \left\| v_\theta\!\left(x_t,\, t,\, \mathbf{c}_{\text{VLM}}\right) - (x_1 - x_0) \right\|^2 \right]
$$

where $\mathbf{c}_{\text{VLM}}$ encodes the visual and language context from the frozen VLM backbone. Only the action expert parameters $\theta$ are updated.

#### The MM-DiT Hat Architecture

The central architectural innovation is the **MM-DiT (Multi-Modal Diffusion Transformer) Hat**, which replaces the standard late-stage cross-attention mechanism with early-stage joint self-attention between vision-language tokens and action tokens.

In a **naive DiT Hat**, action tokens self-attend among themselves through multiple transformer layers, and vision-language features enter only at the end via cross-attention. The information flow is one-directional (action tokens query VL, but VL cannot query action tokens) and occurs late in the computation.

In the **MM-DiT Hat**, all tokens — both vision-language and action — are concatenated at the very beginning. Every transformer layer then runs joint self-attention over the combined sequence:

$$
\mathbf{H} = [\mathbf{H}_{vl} \,;\, \mathbf{H}_{a}], \quad \text{JointAttn}(\mathbf{H}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{(\mathbf{H}W_Q)(\mathbf{H}W_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)(\mathbf{H}W_V)
$$

This means action features are informed by vision-language context from the very first layer, and vision-language tokens can also attend to action tokens — enabling deeper, earlier, and symmetric cross-modal interaction. The MM-DiT architecture is standard in state-of-the-art video generation models (Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX, CogVideoX), but its application to VLA models is novel. The analogy is comparing writing an essay first and then reading the brief (naive cross-attention) versus having the brief in front of you from the start (MM-DiT joint attention).

<details><summary>Each MM-DiT Hat block operates as follows:</summary>

```python
# MM-DiT Hat block (conceptual, per-layer)
def mm_dit_hat_block(h_vl, h_action, timestep_emb):
    # Concatenate all tokens
    h = concat([h_vl, h_action], dim=1)  # [N_vl + N_a, d]

    # (Optional) Each modality has its own layer norm params
    h_vl_normed = adaLN(h[:N_vl], timestep_emb)
    h_a_normed  = adaLN(h[N_vl:], timestep_emb)
    h_normed = concat([h_vl_normed, h_a_normed], dim=1)

    # Joint self-attention (all tokens attend to all tokens)
    h_attn = self_attention(h_normed)  # [N_vl + N_a, d]

    # FFN (feed-forward network)
    h_out = ffn(h_attn)

    # Return updated representations
    return h_out[:N_vl], h_out[N_vl:]  # (h_vl_updated, h_action_updated)
```

</details>

Crucially, both vision-language and action token representations are updated in each block — the `self_attention` call implements the joint self-attention formula, and `adaLN` handles the timestep/conditioning injection standard in DiT architectures.

### Real-Time Deployment with Action Chunking

#### The Temporal Overlap Problem

When a VLA predicts action chunks sequentially, there is always an overlap between the executing portion of the current chunk and the predicted next chunk. If the model predicts a new chunk while the robot is still executing the previous one, the discontinuity at the handoff point causes abrupt jumps in joint angles — **action jitter**.

A naive solution is temporal ensembling: take a weighted average of overlapping predictions from successive chunks. However, if the two chunks disagree (e.g., one predicts moving left, the other moving right), averaging causes the robot to move toward an obstacle. The problem is fundamentally one of independence: the second chunk prediction is made without knowledge of which actions the robot is currently executing.

#### Training-Time Inpainting

Ci0 solves this by treating action chunk prediction as an **inpainting problem**. During training, a portion of the beginning of each action sequence is revealed as a conditioning signal, and the model must predict the remaining (masked) portion. At inference, the already-executing actions from the previous chunk serve as this inpainting context:

$$
p_\theta(a_{t:t+H} \mid \mathbf{o}_t, \,\mathbf{l},\, \underbrace{a_{t-s:t}}_{\text{inpainting context}})
$$

where $a_{t:t+H}$ is the future action sequence to predict, $o_t$ is the robot observation, $l$ is the language task description, and $a_{t-s:t}$ are the last $s$ actions from the previous chunk that are already committed. The inpainting context anchors the new prediction to the old trajectory, encouraging continuity at the handoff.

The training procedure simultaneously synthesizes an inference delay by adding a time offset $\tau$ to simulate that the first $s$ actions were executed during the delay. This ensures the model learns to handle the exact delay conditions it will encounter at deployment.

#### Client-Server Deployment Architecture

The deployment system uses a client-server architecture with two parallel loops:

- **Control loop** (high frequency, 30–50 Hz): Continuously reads the latest available action from a shared buffer and sends it to the robot. This loop is deterministic and never blocks.
- **Inference loop** (asynchronous): Runs VLA inference with real-time chunking. At each step, it retrieves the last $s$ actions from the action buffer as the inpainting context, runs the model, and updates the buffer with the new chunk.

The two loops run in parallel, decoupling the high-frequency control requirement from the lower-frequency inference speed.

<details><summary>The implementation of this architecture is:</summary>

```python
# SERVER: Two parallel loops

# === Control Loop (high frequency: 30Hz) ===
def control_loop():
    while running:
        action = action_buffer.get_latest()   # Latest available action
        robot_client.send(action)             # Execute on robot
        sleep(1/30)

# === Inference Loop (asynchronous) ===
def inference_loop():
    while running:
        obs = robot_client.get_observation()       # RGB + proprioception
        task = current_task_description            # Language goal

        # Get the last s actions already sent (inpainting context)
        inpainting_ctx = action_buffer.get_last(s)

        # Run VLA inference with real-time chunking
        new_chunk = vla_model.infer(
            observation=obs,
            language=task,
            inpainting_context=inpainting_ctx     # Key: condition on past actions
        )

        # Update the action buffer with the new chunk
        action_buffer.update(new_chunk)
```

</details>

The control loop always sends the latest available action, ensuring continuous control. The inference loop uses `inpainting_ctx = action_buffer.get_last(s)` to implement the conditioning $a_{t-s:t}$, anchoring the new prediction to the old trajectory. The `action_buffer` is shared between the two loops.

### HumDex: IMU-Based Whole-Body Teleoperation

#### System Overview

HumDex extends the framework by replacing VR-based hand teleoperation with an IMU-based system for controlling a 20-DoF five-finger dexterous hand (the Wuji hand). Instead of a MANUS data glove, five fingertip-mounted IMU sensors capture the orientation of each finger segment. This eliminates the need for camera-based hand tracking, making the system fully portable and free of line-of-sight constraints.

| Component     | VR System                 | IMU System (HumDex)        |
| :------------ | :------------------------ | :------------------------- |
| Hand tracking | MANUS glove               | **5 fingertip IMUs**       |
| Robot hand    | 3-finger or Wuji 5-finger | **Wuji 5-finger (20 DoF)** |
| Retargeting   | Direct IK                 | **Learned MLP**            |

#### Learning-Based Hand Retargeting

Controlling 20 DoF from 5 IMU sensors is fundamentally underdetermined. HumDex solves this with a learned retargeting pipeline. First, a bootstrap dataset of 20,000 frames is collected in 20 minutes using a slow optimization-based IK solver. Frames with abnormal joint angles or implausible configurations are automatically filtered out. Then, a small MLP is trained to map the 5 fingertip IMU readings directly to the 20 hand joint angles:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{\text{hand}} = f_\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\text{IMU}}), \quad \mathcal{L}_{\text{retarget}} = \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{q}^*) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{filter}}} \left[ \left\| f_\phi(\mathbf{x}_{\text{IMU}}) - \mathbf{q}^*_{\text{hand}} \right\|^2 \right]
$$

The MLP generalizes better than the IK solver because it learns a smooth mapping from the data distribution rather than solving a non-convex optimization per frame. It also runs orders of magnitude faster — a single forward pass takes approximately 1ms, enabling real-time retargeting at 30+ Hz.

<details><summary>The retargeting pipeline proceeds in three stages:</summary>

```python
# HumDex retargeting pipeline

# === Step 1: Bootstrap data collection ===
retargeting_data = []
for frame in live_capture(duration_minutes=20):
    imu_reading = frame.get_fingertip_imu()        # 5 × d_orient
    hand_joints = ik_solver.solve(imu_reading)     # 20 DoF (slow, per-frame)
    if is_valid(hand_joints):                       # Step 2: Filtering
        retargeting_data.append((imu_reading, hand_joints))

# === Step 3: Train MLP ===
mlp = MLP(input_dim=len(imu_reading), output_dim=20)
for epoch in range(num_epochs):
    for x, q_star in retargeting_data:
        loss = MSE(mlp(x), q_star)
        loss.backward()
        optimizer.step()

# === Deployment: real-time retargeting ===
def teleoperate():
    while running:
        imu = sensors.read()           # Real-time IMU
        joints = mlp(imu)              # Single forward pass (~1ms)
        robot.set_hand_joints(joints)
```

</details>

The `ik_solver.solve` call implements the slow optimization-based IK that fails on edge cases. The `is_valid` function removes abnormal angles and pinch failures from the bootstrap data. At deployment, `mlp(imu)` replaces the iterative IK solver with a single forward pass, implementing the learned retargeting function $f_\phi$.

#### Human Data Augmentation

With the IMU + egocentric camera setup, a human performing a task directly (no robot needed) can generate training data, because the egocentric viewpoint aligns with the robot's camera and the retargeted hand states align with the robot's joint states. When human demonstration data is mixed with robot teleoperation data for ACT policy training, the model trained with human-augmented data consistently outperforms pure robot teleoperation data, especially under unseen scenarios and as the amount of human data increases. This confirms that egocentric human data can serve as a low-cost, complementary data source for robot policy learning.

### Future Directions

**Data scale** called for growth of egocentric data through crowdsourcing, industry partnerships, and better capture hardware. **Model scale** requires both larger models to consume the increased data volume and more data-efficient models that extract more signal per sample. The ultimate goal is to incorporate reasoning, planning, and generalization into the robotics foundation model.

Several important open questions remain. The degree to which the model learns human behavioral priors (how humans naturally perform tasks) versus simply following task specifications is unknown. Stereo cameras are not strictly required for pre-training — monocular data can work as long as 3D hand representations are scale-consistent over time — but existing large egocentric monocular datasets like Ego4D lack hand tracking annotations, making them hard to use directly. The Ci0 demos are not fully zero-shot; the model is evaluated in the same environment where teleoperation data was collected, with object location and type variation representing only partial generalization. And perhaps most critically, force and torque sensing integration remains an unresolved limitation: the current position-based control system cannot handle tasks requiring compliant manipulation, force feedback, or contact-rich interaction, which are fundamental to many practical applications.

Ci0 shows why a humanoid foundation model cannot be separated from its data and deployment pipeline. Its use of action chunks also raises a deeper question: why do chunked and generative policies work so much better than ordinary step-by-step behavior cloning? ## Part II — Generative Action Models and the Algorithmic Moravec's Paradox

Moravec's Paradox — the observation that sensorimotor tasks trivial for humans are hard for AI — has traditionally been attributed to data scarcity. This part develops a deeper, algorithmic formulation: for the smooth Markovian policy class used in the lower-bound construction, behavior cloning in continuous control can remain intractable even with abundant data because error compounds exponentially with the trajectory horizon. Action chunking addresses this barrier under open-loop stability, while the evidence presented for generative control policies (diffusion/flow-based) points to off-manifold error projection rather than multimodality as the main mechanism. The Minimal Iterative Policy (MIP) isolates stochasticity injection and iterative computation from distributional learning, achieving flow-competitive performance with two forward passes. Together, these results argue that specific algorithmic choices must precede data scaling to unlock the "bitter lesson" in continuous control.

### Moravec's Paradox and the 2023 Inflection Point

It was observed that high-level symbolic reasoning — chess, mathematics, theorem proving — proved easier to automate than low-level sensorimotor tasks such as walking, grasping, and manipulating objects. The conventional explanation has been pragmatic: symbolic tasks benefit from internet-scale data, while physical task data requires instrumented environments and human operators, making it historically scarce.

The period around 2022–2023 witnessed a sudden inflection point in robot manipulation capabilities. Behavior cloning (BC), which had existed since the late 1980s, suddenly began producing reliable policies for bimanual manipulation, folding, and other dexterous tasks. This inflection coincided with the widespread adoption of two algorithmic innovations: **action chunking** (predicting and executing multi-step action sequences open-loop) and **generative control policies** (using diffusion or flow-matching models to produce action distributions).

The central thesis of this work is that these innovations are not merely engineering conveniences. Within the stated assumptions, continuous control imitation learning suffers from a **quantitative Moravec's paradox**: a lower-bound construction in which smooth Markovian algorithms incur exponential-in-horizon compounding error regardless of data volume or reward access. The two interventions change the closed-loop dynamics between robot and learner, moving the system into a regime where data-based scaling becomes effective.

### The MDP Formalism and the Curse of Horizon

#### The Behavior Cloning Setup

In the Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework, a robot learning problem is defined by a state space $S$, an action space $A$, transition dynamics $P(s' \mid s, a)$, a reward function $R(s, a)$, an initial state distribution $\rho_0$, and a horizon $H$ (the number of timesteps per episode). A Markovian policy $\pi: S \to \Delta(A)$ maps the current state to a distribution over actions. The objective is to maximize expected cumulative reward:

$$
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{H-1} R(s_t, a_t)\right], \quad s_0 \sim \rho_0, \quad a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_t), \quad s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)
$$

Behavior cloning trains a policy $\hat{\pi}$ by minimizing supervised loss on expert demonstration data $\mathcal{D} = \{\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_n\}$, where each trajectory $\tau_i$ contains state-action pairs from the expert policy $\pi^*$:

$$
\hat{\pi} = \arg\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\|\pi(s) - a\|^2\right]
$$

#### Covariate Shift and Compounding Error

The fundamental tension in behavior cloning is the mismatch between training and evaluation distributions. At training time, the policy minimizes error on states visited by the expert ($d^{\pi^*}$). At deployment, the policy encounters states under its own distribution ($d^{\hat{\pi}}$). Every small prediction error takes the policy to a slightly different state, which leads to a different observation, which leads to a different action — errors compound.

This compounding can be formalized through the lens of control-theoretic stability. A closed-loop system $s_{t+1} = f(s_t, \pi(s_t))$ is **incrementally stable with rate $\lambda < 1$** if, for any two initial states $s_0, s_0'$:

$$
\|s_t - s_t'\| \leq C \lambda^t \|s_0 - s_0'\| \quad \forall t \geq 0
$$

This means two nearby trajectories converge exponentially to each other. If the learned policy $\hat{\pi}$ is stable, errors made at one step contract rather than amplify. If it is unstable, errors grow exponentially with the horizon $H$.

The gap between discrete and continuous settings is stark:

| Setting                     | Error Growth                                 | Reason                                     |
| :-------------------------- | :------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| Discrete BC (0/1 loss)      | $O(H \cdot \epsilon)$ (polynomial)           | Errors are independent across steps        |
| Continuous BC (square loss) | $\Omega(e^{cH} \cdot \epsilon)$ (worst case) | Continuous errors amplify through dynamics |

### The Quantitative Moravec's Paradox: A Formal Lower Bound

#### Theorem Statement

The negative result is constructed in the best possible setting to make it as strong as possible: the dynamics are deterministic and smooth, the expert policy is deterministic, smooth, and Markovian, the expert dynamics are open-loop and closed-loop exponentially stable, the system is one-step controllable (any state reachable from any other), and the learner is restricted to smooth, Markovian, deterministic policies — exactly the conditions one would want for BC to succeed.

**Theorem (informal)**. Fix any desired supervised learning rate $\epsilon_n = n^{-\alpha}$ (any $\alpha > 0$). There exists a set of problem instances satisfying all the above assumptions such that:

1. **Supervised learning succeeds**: The BC algorithm achieves $\epsilon_n = n^{-\alpha}$ supervised loss — generalization is not the issue.
2. **Cumulative reward fails**: Any smooth Markovian algorithm must suffer suboptimality:

$$
J(\pi^*) - J(\hat{\pi}) = \Omega\!\left(e^{c \cdot H} \cdot \epsilon_n\right)
$$

for some constant $c > 0$, regardless of the algorithm or sample size. This lower bound applies to all offline algorithms, including offline RL — even reward access cannot help.

#### The Geometric Intuition

The hard instance exploits an **identifiability failure** in the state space. The state space $S = \mathbb{R}^d$ is decomposed into two orthogonal subspaces:

$$
S = V_{\parallel} \oplus V_{\perp}
$$

On $V_{\parallel}$ (the subspace visible in training data), two different policy-dynamics pairs are identical — the learner cannot distinguish them from demonstrations. On $V_{\perp}$ (the orthogonal complement never visited by the expert), any policy that stabilizes System 1 must destabilize System 2, and vice versa. Since the learner cannot tell which system generated the data, any learned policy will destabilize at least one of them, and the resulting error grows exponentially in $H$ as the instability propagates through the orthogonal subspace.

This is the **quantitative Moravec's paradox**: a formal mathematical separation between discrete and continuous imitation learning. Adding more data alone cannot fix the problem — algorithmic intervention is necessary.

### Action Chunking: Escaping the Lower Bound

#### How Chunking Breaks the Markovian Constraint

Action chunking modifies the policy to predict a sequence of $k$ consecutive actions from a single observation, then executes them open-loop (without re-observing the state during execution):

```math
\hat{\pi}^{\text{chunk}}: S \times \{0, \ldots, k-1\} \to A, \quad (s_t, j) \mapsto \hat{a}_{t+j}
```

The policy commits to future actions $â_{t+1}, \cdots, â_{t+k-1}$ based on the stale observation $s_t$ rather than the actual states $s_{t+1}, \cdots, s_{t+k-1}$. This correlates actions across time steps, making the effective policy non-Markovian — precisely the restriction assumed by the lower bound above.

#### The Positive Theorem

Under the conditions that the dynamics are open-loop stable (rate $\lambda_{\text{OL}} < 1$) and smooth, and the learned policy is $L$-Lipschitz, there exists a critical chunk length $k*$ such that for all $k \geq k^*$:

```math
J(\pi^*) - J(\hat{\pi}^{\text{chunk}}) \leq C^* \cdot \epsilon_n
```

where $C*$ and $k*$ are **horizon-independent constants**. The critical chunk length is:

$$
k^* = O\!\left(\frac{\log(L \cdot C)}{\log(1/\lambda_{\text{OL}})}\right)
$$

Unlike the exponential lower bound $\Omega(e^{cH} \epsilon_n)$ for standard BC, this bound does not grow with $H$. Adding more data ($\epsilon_n \to 0$) directly improves deployment performance.

#### Why It Works

The mechanism operates chunk by chunk. During Chunk 1 ($t = 0$ to $t = k-1$), the policy has been trained on these states, so supervised error is small. Between chunks, open-loop stability means that despite small errors in Chunk 1's actions, the system stays close to the nominal trajectory (exponential contraction with rate $\lambda_{\text{OL}}^k$). When Chunk 2 begins at $t = k$, the state is close to where the expert would have been, and because the policy is Lipschitz-smooth, its predictions at the actual state track the predictions at the nominal state. This argument repeats inductively — the error in each chunk is bounded by a constant rather than growing with $H$.

The requirement $k \geq k*$ ensures that the open-loop contraction has enough steps to bring the state sufficiently close to the nominal trajectory before the next chunk begins. Critically, $k*$ does not grow with $H$, so action chunking genuinely eliminates the horizon problem rather than hiding it.

#### The Role of Position Control

Position control — commanding target joint angles rather than raw torques, with a low-level PD controller tracking them — provides open-loop stability by design. The high-level policy outputs target positions, and the low-level controller brings joints to those positions with exponentially decaying error. This layered architecture is a prerequisite for the chunking theorem: without open-loop stability, the chunking guarantee does not apply.

Experimental validation confirms that longer execution chunks dramatically improve success rates, and that execution chunk length dominates supervision length (the number of actions used as training targets). The improvement is a control-theoretic effect, not merely better representation learning.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
# Training: policy maps (state, chunk_index) → action
def train_chunking_policy(dataset, chunk_length=k):
    policy = NeuralNetwork(input_dim=state_dim + 1)  # +1 for chunk index j

    for episode in dataset:
        for t in range(0, len(episode) - k, k):
            state = episode[t].state
            chunk_targets = [episode[t+j].action for j in range(k)]

            predicted_chunk = [policy(state, j) for j in range(k)]
            loss = sum(MSE(pred, target)
                       for pred, target in zip(predicted_chunk, chunk_targets))
            loss.backward()

    return policy

# Inference: execute k actions open-loop per observation
def deploy_chunking_policy(policy, env, chunk_length=k):
    state = env.reset()
    while not done:
        chunk = [policy(state, j) for j in range(chunk_length)]
        for action in chunk:
            next_state, reward, done, _ = env.step(action)
            # state is NOT updated during open-loop execution
        state = next_state  # only re-observe at chunk boundaries
```

</details>

The `policy(state, j)` learns to predict the $j$-th action $j$ steps into the future. The open-loop execution loop — iterating through the chunk without re-observing — is what creates the non-Markovian behavior that escapes the lower bound.

### Generative Control Policies: Motivation and Taxonomy

#### The Original Motivation

Generative control policies (GCPs) — policies that use diffusion or flow-matching models to produce a distribution over actions — were originally motivated by the need to capture **multimodal action distributions**. The argument was: as robot demonstration data scales, multiple strategies for the same task will appear in the data. For example, given a state with an obstacle, the robot could go left or right — two distinct modes. A regression policy that predicts the mean would output the midpoint, potentially causing a collision. A distributional model could capture both modes.

A flow-based policy is trained with the objective:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{flow}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, z, a^*}\!\left[\left\|v_\theta\!\left((1-t)z + t a^*,\, s,\, t\right) - (a^* - z)\right\|^2\right]
```

where $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is the initial noise, $a^*$ is the target action, and $v_\theta$ is the predicted velocity field. At inference, the ODE $\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x, s, t)$ is integrated from $x(0) = z$ to $x(1) = \hat{a}$, typically using 10–50 Euler steps.

#### Three Bundled Properties

GCPs bundle three distinct properties:

| Property                    | Description                                                            | Potential Benefit                                                   |
| :-------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Distributional learning** | Training to fit the full distribution $p(a \mid s)$, not just the mean | Captures multimodal action distributions                            |
| **Stochasticity injection** | Random noise $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ as additional input            | Randomizes coverage of action space                                 |
| **Iterative computation**   | Multi-step ODE integration; supervised at every step $t$               | Provides more expressive generation; each step can correct previous |

The critical question is: which of these three properties is responsible for the performance gains? A fair comparison requires controlling for architecture — using the same neural network backbone and changing only the loss function and generation mechanism.

### Ablating Multimodality: The Real Mechanism

Three complementary experiments indicate that distributional learning is **not** the main mechanism responsible for the observed GCP performance gains.

#### Experiment 1: Visualizing Action Distributions

Sampling multiple actions from a learned flow policy and coloring them by Q-value reveals no distinct modes — Q-values are uniformly dispersed rather than clustering into high-reward modes. The visual appearance of a task being "multimodal" does not translate into actual multimodality in the learned distribution.

#### Experiment 2: Monte Carlo Averaging

Drawing $K = 64$ independent noise seeds $z^{(1)}, \ldots, z^{(64)}$, computing the full ODE for each to get $\hat{a}^{(k)}$, and averaging $\bar{a} = \frac{1}{64}\sum_{k=1}^{64} \hat{a}^{(k)}$ destroys any multimodality that might exist. If the distribution were truly bimodal with modes $a_L$ and $a_R$, the average would be the physically invalid midpoint $(a_L + a_R)/2$. The performance drop is only **2%**, confirming that multimodality preservation is not necessary.

#### Experiment 3: Deterministic Expert Data

Using a deterministic neural network as the expert (eliminating human teleoperator variability and thus any data-level multimodality) narrows the gap between flow and regression, but a statistically significant advantage for flow remains. Even with zero multimodality in the training distribution, flow policies outperform regression.

#### An Alternative Mechanism: Off-Manifold Error Projection

The true mechanism is revealed through a comparison to noisy data collection. If one collects data by injecting noise into actions ( $a_t + \epsilon_t$, $\epsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$ ), records clean expert actions at the resulting perturbed states, and trains BC on this noisy data, then even on unstable dynamical systems, the compounding error becomes polynomial rather than exponential in $H$. The injected noise excites the controllability Gramian — it visits every direction in state space that is potentially unstable and susceptible to BC error. Training the policy to correct from these perturbed states teaches it to be robust to exactly the directions where instability can occur.

The evidence suggests that flow policies implicitly approximate this noisy data collection effect **without actually collecting noisy data**. The key empirical finding is that flow policies have significantly lower **off-manifold error** than regression policies. The prediction error decomposes as:

$$
\hat{\pi}(s) - a^*(s) = \underbrace{e_\parallel}_{\text{on-manifold (safe)}} + \underbrace{e_\perp}_{\text{off-manifold (dangerous)}}
$$

where $e_\parallel$ is the projection onto the local tangent space of the action manifold and $e_\perp$ is the orthogonal component. For an insertion task, error along the insertion direction is recoverable (just push further), but error perpendicular to the insertion direction is catastrophic (the robot wedges against the wall). Flow policies learn to minimize the dangerous perpendicular error, sometimes at the cost of slightly larger on-manifold error.

<details><summary>The off-manifold error can be measured empirically:</summary>

```python
def estimate_action_manifold_tangent(policy_expert, state_s, sigma=0.01, n=20):
    perturbed_actions = []
    for _ in range(n):
        eps = np.random.randn(action_dim) * sigma
        perturbed_state = dynamics.step(state_s, policy_expert(state_s) + eps)
        perturbed_actions.append(policy_expert(perturbed_state))

    # Local tangent space = column span of action perturbations
    tangent_basis = np.linalg.svd(
        np.array(perturbed_actions) - policy_expert(state_s)
    )[2][:k]
    return tangent_basis

def compute_off_manifold_error(learned_policy, expert_policy, state_s, tangent_basis):
    error = learned_policy(state_s) - expert_policy(state_s)
    proj_onto_manifold = tangent_basis @ (tangent_basis.T @ error)
    e_parallel = proj_onto_manifold
    e_perpendicular = error - e_parallel
    return np.linalg.norm(e_perpendicular)
```

</details>

The `estimate_action_manifold_tangent` function uses noise perturbations to approximate the local tangent space. The `compute_off_manifold_error` function decomposes the prediction error into safe (on-manifold) and dangerous (off-manifold) components.

An additional theoretical argument weighs against the "expressivity" hypothesis — that flow models benefit from higher Lipschitz constants due to depth. If the action distribution is log-concave, the score function $\nabla_a \log p(a \mid s)$ is contractive, meaning the flow's velocity field does not need to grow with depth. Under that condition, flow policies do not gain additional expressivity from multiple steps and may learn smoother functions than regression.

### MIP: The Minimal Iterative Policy

#### Architecture

The Minimal Iterative Policy (MIP) isolates stochasticity injection and iterative computation from distributional learning, using only two regression steps:

**Stage 1 (Regression)**:

$$
\hat{a}_0 = \pi_\theta(s, \mathbf{0}, t=0)
$$

**Stage 2 (Self-Correction)**:

$$
\hat{a}_{\text{final}} = \pi_\theta(s, \hat{a}_0 + \epsilon, t=1), \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)
$$

Both stages use the same neural network with different time embeddings ($t=0$ for Stage 1, $t=1$ for Stage 2). The time embedding acts as a "mode selector," allowing the same weights to learn two distinct computational circuits.

#### Training Objective

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{MIP}} = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\pi_\theta(s, \mathbf{0}, 0) - a^*\|^2\right] + \mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\pi_\theta(s, a^* + \epsilon, 1) - a^*\|^2\right]
```

A key subtlety: Stage 2 is trained on the **ground truth action plus noise** ($a^* + \epsilon$), not on Stage 1's output plus noise. At inference, Stage 1's prediction substitutes for the ground truth, and the gap is small enough that Stage 2 still corrects effectively.

#### Why Stochasticity and Iteration Must Co-occur

Ablation experiments reveal that neither property alone is sufficient:

| Method                    | Stochasticity | Iterative computation | Performance            |
| :------------------------ | :------------ | :-------------------- | :--------------------- |
| Regression                | ✗             | ✗                     | Baseline               |
| Straight Flow (1-step)    | ✓             | ✗                     | No improvement         |
| Iterative-only (no noise) | ✗             | ✓                     | No improvement         |
| **MIP**                   | ✓             | ✓                     | **≈ Flow performance** |
| **Full Flow**             | ✓             | ✓ (N steps)           | Best (but slower)      |

Stochasticity injection mimics the noisy data collection that excites controllable directions, and iterative computation provides the mechanism to correct toward the manifold using the noise as a probe of off-manifold directions. Without stochasticity, there is nothing to correct from, making iteration useless. Without iteration, the noise merely adds randomness without projecting toward the manifold.

<details><summary>Code</summary>

```python
class MIP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, state_dim, action_dim):
        self.encoder = TransformerEncoder(state_dim)
        self.action_head = MLP(hidden_dim + action_dim + 1)  # +1 for time t

    def forward(self, state, action_input, t):
        state_features = self.encoder(state)
        t_embed = positional_encoding(t)
        return self.action_head(cat([state_features, action_input, t_embed]))

def train_mip(dataset, model, sigma=0.1):
    optimizer = Adam(model.parameters())
    for state, a_star in dataset:
        # Stage 1 loss
        a_hat_0 = model(state, zeros(action_dim), t=0)
        loss_1 = MSE(a_hat_0, a_star)

        # Stage 2 loss: denoise (a* + noise) → a*
        eps = randn_like(a_star) * sigma
        a_hat_final = model(state, a_star + eps, t=1)
        loss_2 = MSE(a_hat_final, a_star)

        (loss_1 + loss_2).backward()
        optimizer.step()

def infer_mip(model, state, sigma=0.1):
    a_hat_0 = model(state, zeros(action_dim), t=0)
    eps = randn_like(a_hat_0) * sigma
    a_hat_final = model(state, a_hat_0 + eps, t=1)
    return a_hat_final
```

</details>

The `t=0` and `t=1` time embeddings force the network into distinct modes. Stage 2 is trained on `a_star + eps` (ground truth with noise), while inference uses `a_hat_0 + eps` (Stage 1's prediction with noise). MIP requires only 2 forward passes versus 10–50 for a full flow model — a $5\times$– $25\times$ speedup.

#### Practical Guidance

MIP is recommended when fast inference is needed, as an ablation tool to diagnose whether a flow model relies on multimodality, or for high-precision single-task fine-tuning where MIP outperforms consistency models. MIP is not recommended for large-scale pre-training (where genuine multimodality may exist) or for RL fine-tuning (where flow models' additional plasticity from multiple steps provides more stability).

### Implications and Future Directions

#### Synthesis of the Algorithmic Moravec's Paradox

For the problem class captured by the lower bound, continuous control imitation learning remains hard because state sensitivity and closed-loop instability make errors grow exponentially with the horizon; offline BC cannot overcome that construction through sample size alone. Action chunking uses open-loop stability and breaks the Markovian constraint, producing a horizon-independent bound under the positive theorem's assumptions. The experiments suggest that generative control policies provide a complementary off-manifold error projection mechanism, analogous to noisy exploration data collection, that improves robustness to unvisited state perturbations.

#### Test-Time Feedback Beyond Robotics

The compounding error phenomenon is not unique to robotics. Any system where model outputs are fed back as inputs — autoregressive language model generation, sequential image or video generation — suffers from **test-time feedback shift**. Small errors accumulate when models are executed back on themselves, analogous to the compounding error in continuous control. New optimization methods specifically designed to mitigate test-time feedback show performance gains across robotics, language modeling, and image generation, without altering training or validation loss. This suggests a unified theoretical framework connecting control-theoretic stability to deep learning architectures with feedback loops.

#### Open Questions

Several important questions remain. The theoretical connection between flow policies and noisy data collection is empirically compelling but not yet formally proven. The noise level $\sigma$ in MIP requires careful tuning — too low and Stage 2 learns nothing, too high and Stage 2 cannot recover the clean action. At extreme data scales, position control may no longer be necessary as an inductive bias, potentially allowing direct torque-level policy learning. And the conversion of epistemic uncertainty (model capacity limitations) into aleatoric output variance in flow models offers a novel perspective on why generative modeling succeeds in high-dimensional regression, but the precise mechanism warrants further investigation.

Algorithmic stability changes how a learned controller behaves when its predictions feed back into the world. Yet control software is only one source of stability. A robot's body can also reject disturbances, average over terrain noise, and reduce the amount of feedback the controller must supply. That observation shifts the analysis from policy design to morphology.

## Part III — Morphological Intelligence in Locomotion: From Information Theory to Asymmetric Optimal Gaits

Mesoscale robots (~1 kg) occupy a crucial but under-explored niche between microrobots and macrorobots, operating in a noise-dominated regime where terrain forces are inherently unpredictable. This part presents a unified framework for understanding locomotion through the lens of morphological intelligence — the idea that physical body structure can substitute for computational feedback. Drawing on Shannon's noisy-channel coding theorem, the framework maps morphological redundancy (leg count) to error-correcting codes and derives an asymptotic prediction of zero-error locomotion over stochastic terrain without sensor feedback. A dimensionless Coasting Number describes a friction-dominated regime analogous to low-Reynolds-number fluid mechanics, where reported "terrestrial swimming" gaits triple speed without mechanical changes. Experiments also identify system-specific trade-offs between leg count and feedback bandwidth, and show how sensing can migrate from leg torque to body torque as morphology changes. Finally, graph optimization over approximately $2 \times 10^{89}$ possible contact-state sequences produces an asymmetric 3-leg/1-leg configuration reported to move 50% faster than the symmetric hexapod baseline. Together, these results propose a quantitative foundation for co-designing morphological and computational intelligence.

### The Mesoscale Research Gap

Nature exhibits remarkable diversity in body size, with organisms moving adeptly across habitats spanning orders of magnitude in mass. Modern robotics, however, clusters bimodally: microrobots below 1 gram and macrorobots above 10 kilograms. The mesoscale — robots around 1 kilogram — remains under-explored despite compelling applications. Disaster response requires robots that can cross obstacles too large for microrobots while accessing spaces too tight for macrorobots. CBRN (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear) threat detection represents a projected $21.5 billion market by 2028. Precision agriculture, projected at $21.9 billion by 2031, demands robots that can navigate crop rows without soil compaction. Indoor service robots must maneuver through cluttered human environments.

The mesoscale is not merely an engineering convenience gap — it is a fundamentally distinct physical regime. The key insight is captured by the number of active terrain objects $N_{\text{active}}$ that simultaneously interact with the robot:

```math
N_{\text{active}} \approx \begin{cases}
1 & \text{Microrobots } (m < 1\text{ g}) \quad \text{[Single-contact dynamics]} \\
\sim 10 & \text{Mesoscale } (m \approx 1\text{ kg}) \quad \text{[Noise-Dominated Regime]} \\
> 100 & \text{Macrorobots } (m > 10\text{ kg}) \quad \text{[Continuum Limit]}
\end{cases}
```

At the macroscale, granular media behaves like a continuum — dynamics are smoothed and analytically tractable. At the microscale, single-contact dynamics suffice. But at the mesoscale, each terrain particle has mass comparable to the robot itself. Stochastic particle collisions and terrain flow create a noise-dominated regime where deterministic contact models fail. The central thesis of this work is that reliable locomotion in this regime, without heavy computational overhead, requires **mesoscale embodied intelligence**: the integration of physical and morphological design with computational feedback.

### An Information-Theoretic Theory of Locomotion

#### Step-Driven vs. Inertia-Driven Locomotion

Locomotion strategies can be broadly classified into two paradigms. **Inertia-driven** locomotion — wheels, gliders, continuous thrust — is governed by momentum ($\sum F = ma$) and achieves high displacement per unit thrust in frictionless environments, but is highly sensitive to terrain perturbations. **Step-driven** locomotion — legged gaits — discretizes thrust into distinct spatial-temporal footsteps. While this caps maximum displacement per step, it enables foot placement selection over heterogeneous terrain, making it analogous to digital rather than analog signal transmission.

#### The Shannon Equivalence

The central theoretical contribution is a formal mapping between Claude Shannon's Noisy-Channel Coding Theorem and multi-legged locomotion. Shannon proved that reliable communication over a noisy channel does not require feedback (retransmission); introducing sufficient structural redundancy into the code allows error-free transmission at rates below channel capacity. The locomotion analogue is profound:

| Signal Transmission (Shannon)     | Legged Locomotion (Morphological Theory)                    |
| :-------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Message $W$                       | Desired Destination $D$                                     |
| Encoder (Bits)                    | Spatial-temporal Stepping Map (Discretized Thrust Steps)    |
| Code Redundancy                   | Morphological Redundancy (Number of Legs $N_{\text{legs}}$) |
| Noisy Transmission Channel        | Heterogeneous / Noisy Terrain Landscape                     |
| Received Contaminated Bits        | Deviated Stepping Execution                                 |
| Retransmission Request (Feedback) | Computational Intelligence (Sensor-based Feedback)          |
| Decoded Message $W'$              | Actual Destination $D'$                                     |

Within the framework's terrain and locomotion assumptions, this mapping yields an asymptotic principle: as morphological leg redundancy increases, destination error and arrival-time variance converge to zero without feedback:

$$
\lim_{N_{\text{legs}} \to \infty} \|D' - D\| = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{N_{\text{legs}} \to \infty} \text{Var}(T_{\text{arrival}}) = 0
$$

#### Experimental Validation

The theoretical prediction was tested on centipede-like robots with 6, 8, 12, and 16 legs traversing complex rough terrain. On flat ground, 6-legged and 16-legged robots take equal time to travel 60 cm — the extra legs provide no benefit when the terrain is trivial. On rough ground, however, 6- and 8-legged robots experience high variance in arrival times, frequently getting stuck. In the reported tests, increasing the leg count to 16 collapses the observed arrival-time range to a single value without sensor feedback.

This result refutes the conventional assumption that closed-loop sensing is strictly necessary for navigating complex, non-uniform environments. Redundancy trades hardware complexity (more legs and actuators) for control simplicity (open-loop stability), a principle that parallels error-correcting codes in digital communication.

### Morphology, Speed, and Terrestrial Swimming

#### The Speed-Leg Trade-off

Having more legs provides terrain robustness, but multi-legged robots are traditionally slow. Intuitively, higher speed requires longer legs, not more legs. The question is whether a multi-legged morphology can achieve higher speed purely by altering body-leg coordination without mechanical changes.

Biological centipedes provide the answer. On rough terrain, they use wave-walking — a slow, robust gait where legs contact the ground in a propagating wave. On flat ground, they transition to **terrestrial swimming** — undulating body waves that generate continuous propulsive thrust against terrain friction — achieving approximately three times higher velocity. The key insight is that the same morphology can support multiple behavioral strategies, a concept known as many-to-many morphology-performance mapping.

#### The Coasting Number

To understand why terrestrial swimming works, it introduces a dimensionless **Coasting Number**:

$$
C_{\text{time}} = \frac{t_{\text{coast}}}{t_{\text{cycle}}}
$$

where $t_{\text{coast}}$ is the time required for the locomotor to come to a complete stop after thrust ceases, and $t_{\text{cycle}}$ is the stride or wave cycle period. This ratio quantifies whether momentum carries the robot between strides.

Measurements show $C_{\text{time}} \ll 1$ for centipedes: inertial coasting is negligible. The robot stops almost immediately when thrust ceases. This means locomotion is governed strictly by the instantaneous balance of frictional thrust versus drag — a terrestrial analogue of low-Reynolds-number fluid mechanics. In fluid regimes dominated by viscosity rather than inertia ($Re \ll 1$), Purcell's Scallop Theorem states that net displacement depends purely on the sequence of body postures (the geometry of motion), independent of execution speed.

The equivalence is:

$$
C_{\text{time}} \propto Re = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} \quad \text{(in fluid media)}
$$

In terrestrial friction media, when $C_{\text{time}} \ll 1$:

$$
F_{\text{thrust}}(t) - F_{\text{drag}}(v(t)) = m \frac{dv}{dt} \approx 0
$$

The inertial term vanishes, and locomotion is purely kinematics-driven. This means body undulation — changing the sequence of body postures to generate continuous propulsive contact — can triple speed without any mechanical leg lengthening, simply by exploiting the friction-dominated dynamics.

### Combining Morphological and Computational Intelligence

#### The Greed Dilemma

Pure morphological intelligence presents a dilemma. Open-loop control with 16 legs is robust on rough terrain but slow on flat ground. Terrestrial swimming with 16 legs is fast on flat ground but gets stuck on rough terrain. Neither pure strategy suffices. The solution is to add computational intelligence for adaptive mode switching.

#### Adaptive Contact Sensing

The implementation uses binary contact switches on each foot. At each time step, the stepping map records the actual versus intended spatio-temporal footfall matrix. The difference vector estimates terrain complexity $\hat{C}_{\text{terrain}}$, and a linear feedback controller adjusts the gait mode — switching between wave-walking for rough terrain and body-swimming for flat ground. The feedback requires only low-frequency, binary contact information, not high-bandwidth visual or inertial sensing.

#### The Functional Equivalence Principle

The most striking result is the quantitative trade-off between physical and computational resources. It benchmarked terrain traversal performance across different leg counts and feedback update frequencies, discovering an iso-performance contour:

$$
\text{Performance}(\text{16 legs}, 0\text{ Hz feedback}) \equiv \text{Performance}(\text{12 legs}, 0.1\text{ Hz feedback}) \equiv \text{Performance}(\text{8 legs}, 0.5\text{ Hz feedback})
$$

This can be formalized as:

$$
\text{Embodied Intelligence} = \alpha \cdot N_{\text{legs}} + \beta \cdot f_{\text{ctrl}} = \text{Constant}
$$

where $N_{\text{legs}}$ is the morphological leg count and $f_{\text{ctrl}}$ is the sensor feedback update frequency. The coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are system-dependent constants. In the reported operating points, 16 legs with no feedback, 12 legs with 0.1 Hz feedback, and 8 legs with 0.5 Hz feedback reach similar performance. These points motivate a morphology-control exchange, but they do not establish a universal linear conversion rate.

This functional equivalence provides a concrete framework for robot co-design. Hardware designers can trade off onboard compute and sensing against physical leg density based on payload, power, and cost constraints. A robot with limited computational resources can compensate with more legs; a robot constrained by weight or actuator count can compensate with more frequent sensing and control updates.

### Co-Designing Embodied Intelligence: The Skink Robot

#### Morphology-Driven Sensing

As robot morphology shifts from long-legged quadrupeds to short-legged, elongate skink-like bodies, the optimal sensing strategy changes fundamentally. The skink morphology — an elongated flexible torso with tiny quadrupedal limbs — bridges snake-like limbless robots and conventional quadrupeds, and its control requirements reveal a general principle of sensor placement.

The skink robot uses two distinct gaits. On flat ground, a **standing wave** undulation — where nodes and antinodes remain fixed in space — yields maximum speed. In deep granular media such as sand or loose soil, a **traveling wave** — where body curvature propagates backward along the spine — maximizes speed and prevents sinking. In shallow granular media, an intermediate phase-shifted wave is optimal. The challenge is classifying terrain depth to trigger the correct gait transition.

#### Torque Distribution as a Depth Sensor

The key insight is that internal body joint torques encode terrain depth information. On flat ground, body joint torques are evenly distributed along the spine. In deep granular media, resistive forces concentrate body torques toward the center of the body. A simple $K$-Nearest Neighbors classifier processing internal body joint torques $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{body}}$ achieves 90% classification accuracy for granular media depth — without any visual or optical sensors.

The classification rule can be expressed as:

$$
\text{Media Depth } d_{\text{granular}} \propto \frac{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{mid-body}}\|}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\text{ends}}\|}
$$

When mid-body torques spike relative to the ends, the robot is in deep granular media and should switch to traveling wave gait. When torques are uniform, the robot is on flat ground and should use standing wave.

#### The Sensing Modality Transition Principle

This reveals a general principle: optimal sensor placement is fundamentally constrained by morphology. For conventional quadrupeds with long legs, leg torque distribution $\tau_{\text{leg}}$ senses terrain depth. For skink robots with tiny legs and long bodies, body torque distribution $\tau_{\text{body}}$ senses terrain depth. The transition is governed by the morphological aspect ratio:

$$
\text{Optimal Sensor Location} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{s} \in \{\text{Legs}, \text{Body}\}} \mathcal{I}(\mathbf{s} ; d_{\text{terrain}}) \propto \frac{L_{\text{body}}}{L_{\text{leg}}}
$$

As the spine-to-limb length ratio increases, the mutual information between leg sensors and terrain depth decreases while the mutual information between body sensors and terrain depth increases. Shrinking limb length requires migrating sensing modalities from limbs to the central body trunk. This principle has direct implications for robot design: modifying body proportions requires altering the internal sensing architecture, not just the control algorithm.

### Transcending Biology: Physics-Based Contact Graph Optimization

#### Breaking Biological Symmetry

Biological animals exhibit left-right and diagonal gait symmetries — the tripod gait in hexapods, the trot and gallop in quadrupeds. While evolutionarily robust, symmetry constrains the solvable gait space. To discover gaits beyond what biology has explored, it formulates multi-legged locomotion as a graph optimization problem.

For an $N$-legged robot, there are $2^N$ instantaneous contact patterns (each leg is either in contact with the ground or not). Over a sequence of switches between these patterns, the discrete state space grows combinatorially. For a hexapod ($N = 6$), there are $2^6 = 64$ vertices. The total number of possible sequence trajectories reaches approximately $2 \times 10^{89}$ combinations — far beyond what biological evolution or human intuition can search.

#### The Graph Optimization Formulation

The problem is formulated as a directed graph $\mathcal{G}(V, E)$ where vertices $V$ represent the $2^N$ discrete contact states, and edges $E$ represent the displacement vectors $\Delta \mathbf{x}_{i \to j}$ achieved by switching from state $i$ to state $j$, evaluated via physics simulation or experiments. A periodic gait corresponds to a closed cycle $\mathcal{C}$ in this graph. The optimal gait maximizes average velocity:

$$
\text{Gait}^* = \arg\max_{\text{cycle } \mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{G}} \frac{\sum_{(i, j) \in \mathcal{C}} \Delta \mathbf{x}_{i \to j}}{\sum_{(i, j) \in \mathcal{C}} \Delta t_{i \to j}}
$$

A fundamental theorem constrains the search: in isotropic Coulomb friction environments, body wiggling without changing foot contact states yields zero net forward displacement. Contact switching is the fundamental generator of locomotion — only transitions between different contact patterns can produce net motion.

#### The Asymmetric Discovery

The graph optimizer, unconstrained by biological symmetry assumptions, discovers a gait that no animal uses. The optimal cycle consists of a $3/4$ cycle clockwise turn followed by a $1/4$ cycle rapid counterclockwise turn — an asymmetric rotational phase distribution. Biological gaits assume equal clockwise and counterclockwise phases per stride cycle; the optimizer reveals this assumption is suboptimal.

Remarkably, the optimizer also discovers a hardware simplification. Two of the six leg motors remain fixed and inactive throughout the entire optimized trajectory. These can be replaced by passive rigid sticks, reducing the robot from 6 active legs to an asymmetric 4-legged configuration: 3 active legs on one side, 1 on the other. This asymmetric robot, executing the optimized asymmetric gait, moves **50% faster** than the standard symmetric hexapod with symmetric tripod gait.

This result shows that strict bio-mimicry can limit performance within the tested design space. By removing biological symmetry constraints and searching the full contact state space, the framework discovers an unintuitive morphological and behavioral configuration that outperforms the symmetric baseline.

### Quantifying Embodied Intelligence

#### A Formal Definition

Responding to terminology concerns about the phrase "embodied intelligence," the framework proposes a formal decomposition:

$$
\text{Embodied Intelligence} = \text{Morphological Intelligence} + \text{Computational Intelligence}
$$

Morphological intelligence is the passive physical response to environmental perturbations governed by physical laws and structural constraints — leg density, compliance, joint geometry, body aspect ratio. Computational intelligence is the active feedback processing — sensor sampling, control algorithms, gait switching logic.

#### The Unified Cost of Transport

This decomposition is quantified through a unified Cost of Transport (COT) metric:

$$
\text{COT}_{\text{total}} = \text{MCOT} + \text{CCOT} = \frac{E_{\text{mechanical}}}{m g d} + \frac{E_{\text{computation}}}{m g d}
$$

where $E_{\text{mechanical}}$ is the energy consumed by actuators for physical locomotion, $E_{\text{computation}}$ is the energy consumed by CPUs, GPUs, and sensors for feedback processing, and $m$, $g$, and $d$ are the normalizing mass, gravitational acceleration, and distance. The Mechanical Cost of Transport (MCOT) is governed by morphological intelligence; the Computational Cost of Transport (CCOT) is governed by computational intelligence.

This proposes a scalar benchmark for comparing passive mechanical stabilization with onboard algorithmic feedback. A robot designer can evaluate whether investing in additional legs is more efficient than increasing sensing and control frequency. The reported functional-equivalence points motivate the trade-off, while the unified COT supplies the optimization objective.

Morphological intelligence broadens the meaning of a robotics model: some useful priors live in weights, while others live in the body itself. The remaining scaling challenge is to acquire the sensorimotor experience that neither morphology nor hand-designed control can provide. Natural human activity offers that experience, provided its observations and actions can be aligned with a robot embodiment.

## Part IV — Robot Learning from Human Experience: Scaling Egocentric Data for Physical AI

Modern language models succeeded because the internet made human semantic knowledge trivially scalable. Robotics, however, faces a fundamentally different challenge: transferring human sensorimotor knowledge — the non-verbal, motor-control intuition used during physical interaction — to robots. Teleoperation, the current standard for collecting robot training data, imposes a severe dual bottleneck: it scales only linearly with physical hardware units and strips away natural human compliance, speed, and unconscious motor strategies. This part presents a comprehensive framework for bypassing the teleoperation bottleneck by capturing egocentric human experience directly through wearable sensors, then aligning it with robot execution through a series of algorithmic innovations. EgoMimic converts eye-level egocentric video into stable robot action supervision via SLAM-based reference frame stabilization. EgoBridge uses Joint Optimal Transport to align human and robot latent representations across a 10× speed discrepancy, enabling reported zero-shot cross-domain policy transfer. EMMA extends the framework to mobile manipulation, retargeting holonomic human locomotion to non-holonomic mobile bases. At scale, EgoScale reports log-linear scaling of action-prediction error across 20,000 hours of pre-training data and presents a three-stage recipe — pre-training, mid-training alignment, and one-shot fine-tuning — that mirrors the training paradigm of large language models. Complementary work with Physical Intelligence suggests that multi-embodiment pre-training can produce human-to-robot transfer without an explicit alignment stage. The EgoVerse Consortium provides an open infrastructure for scaling this research, while future directions point toward integrating tactile sensing, dynamic scene-graph memory, and counterfactual contact synthesis for the "last inch" of dexterous manipulation.

### Three Hypotheses and the Teleoperation Bottleneck

The success of large language models rests on a simple insight: the internet made human semantic knowledge trivially scalable. Web scraping transferred the collective textual output of humanity into training data. Robotics faces an analogous challenge — transferring human **sensorimotor knowledge**, the non-verbal, motor-control intuition used during physical interaction — but the data collection mechanism is fundamentally different. Where text can be scraped passively, robot training data has historically required active human teleoperation.

The current standard for robot data collection — teleoperation via VR controllers or leader-follower rigs — imposes a severe dual bottleneck. The **scalability bottleneck** is straightforward: data volume scales only linearly with the number of physical robot units and operator hours, making it orders of magnitude more expensive than internet scraping. The **fidelity bottleneck** is more subtle but equally important: teleoperation is a lossy, unintuitive interface that strips away natural human compliance, speed, and unconscious motor strategies. A human teleoperating a robot arm moves slowly and cautiously, simplifying actions to fit the limits of low-level controllers. Subtle behaviors — kneading dough, sliding a card to the edge of a table to pick it up, the compliant force modulation of an experienced cook — are stripped out entirely.

The core proposal is to capture authentic, unconstrained egocentric human video and multi-modal signals directly at the source, transforming human experience into robot-compatible action supervision without physical robots in the loop during primary data collection. This rests on three hypotheses:

- **Hypothesis 1**: Robot physical capabilities scale predictably with human data volume.
- **Hypothesis 2**: Human sensorimotor data will soon become abundant through wearable sensors and crowd collection.
- **Hypothesis 3**: Scientific progress in physical AI requires scaling both open data infrastructure and learning algorithms simultaneously.

The scaling contrast is stark:

$$
\text{Data}_{\text{teleop}} = N_{\text{robots}} \cdot t_{\text{human}} \quad \ll \quad \text{Data}_{\text{egocentric}} = \sum_{k=1}^{M_{\text{crowd}}} t_{\text{daily}, k}
$$

Teleoperation data volume is bottlenecked by capital-constrained physical robot units. Egocentric data collection, by contrast, scales with the human population wearing sensors — no robot hardware required during capture.

### EgoMimic and EgoBridge: Aligning Egocentric Human and Robot Data

#### The EgoMimic Framework

EgoMimic is an imitation learning system that trains robot policies directly from egocentric human video captured via eye-level smart glasses, specifically Meta Project Aria glasses with an integrated camera and IMU. The robot setup mirrors the human geometry: a custom bimanual robot matching human hand-eye configuration, with Project Aria glasses mounted directly on the robot head to eliminate sensor hardware domain shift between human data collection and robot deployment.

The first technical challenge is **action reference frame stabilization**. Because human heads move continuously during natural tasks, raw hand actions recorded in camera-relative coordinates undergo severe ego-motion noise. A hand trajectory that appears to move erratically in the camera frame may actually be executing a smooth, deliberate motion in world coordinates — the apparent noise comes entirely from head movement.

EgoMimic solves this using Project Aria's built-in Visual Inertial Odometry (VIO) and SLAM pipeline. Future 3D hand poses are re-projected from the global world frame into the camera reference frame at the current observation time:

$$
\mathbf{a}_t^{(k)} = \mathbf{T}_{\text{world} \to \text{cam}(t)} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_{\text{hand}}^{\text{world}}(t+k) \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

This cancels the effect of head motion between the observation time $t$ and the future action time $t+k$, producing stable action trajectories that are usable as policy training targets.

#### The Speed Discrepancy Discovery

Naively adding 1 hour of raw human data to 2 hours of teleoperation data caused a sharp performance drop. The root cause was a **temporal distribution mismatch**: human hands execute tasks up to 10× faster than physical robot manipulators. A human can slide a card off a table in a fraction of a second; a robot arm executing the same trajectory requires much longer, constrained by joint velocity limits and safety margins. Simply mixing these datasets without temporal alignment creates a policy that receives conflicting supervision signals.

#### EgoBridge: Joint Optimal Transport for Representation Alignment

Co-training human and robot data with standard cross-entropy or mean squared error loss fails to align their latent representations. t-SNE visualizations reveal distinct, separable clusters for human and robot data, even when both are performing the same task. The policy learns to treat them as fundamentally different domains.

EgoBridge addresses this by adding a Joint Optimal Transport (JOT) loss that explicitly aligns the latent spaces. The optimization solves for a coupling matrix $\mathbf{\Pi}$ that maps human samples to robot samples while preserving the marginal distributions of each domain:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{JOT}} = \min_{\mathbf{\Pi} \in U(\mathbf{p}_h, \mathbf{p}_r)} \langle \mathbf{\Pi}, \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{z}_h, \mathbf{z}_r, \mathbf{a}_h, \mathbf{a}_r) \rangle_F
$$

The cost matrix uses Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance as the ground metric to compensate for the 10× speed gap:

$$
\mathbf{C}_{ij} = d_{\text{DTW}}(\mathbf{z}_{h,i}, \mathbf{z}_{r,j}) + \lambda \|\mathbf{a}_{h,i} - \mathbf{a}_{r,j}\|^2
$$

This finds the optimal alignment between human and robot trajectory features that minimizes both feature-space and action-space distances without warping the marginal distributions of either domain. The result is a shared embedding space where human and robot representations for the same underlying task overlap.

A striking demonstration of EgoBridge's effectiveness is **zero-shot spatial transfer**: policies successfully execute manipulation tasks in spatial regions — such as opening a drawer in a cabinet area — that were demonstrated only by humans, with no robot teleoperation data in that region. The aligned latent space allows the policy to generalize human demonstrations to new spatial contexts.

### EMMA: Full Humanoid Retargeting for Mobile Manipulation

#### The Mobile Manipulation Challenge

Extending egocentric human data transfer from static tabletop manipulation to mobile manipulation introduces new challenges. Teleoperating mobile manipulators — such as an ALOHA system mounted on a mobile base — is extremely tedious and prone to operator error. The operator must simultaneously control the base, both arms, and the task, while managing the cognitive load of mode switching between navigation and manipulation.

Humans, by contrast, perform mobile manipulation naturally. We navigate while reaching and interacting without conscious mode switching. The EMMA (Egocentric Mobile Manipulation) framework captures this natural behavior and retargets it to robot platforms.

#### Non-Holonomic Retargeting for Navigation

A fundamental kinematic mismatch must be resolved: humans walk holonomically — we can move sideways, turn in place, and change direction seamlessly — while standard mobile bases are non-holonomic differential drives that can only move forward and rotate. EMMA retargets human 2D planar walking paths extracted from SLAM into differential-drive velocity commands:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} v_t \\ \omega_t \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \|\dot{\mathbf{p}}_{h,xy}(t)\| \cos(\theta_h - \theta_r) \\ K_p \cdot \text{wrap}(\theta_{h,\text{path}} - \theta_r) \end{bmatrix}
$$

The linear velocity $v_t$ is the projection of the human's walking speed onto the robot's current heading, and the angular velocity $\omega_t$ uses proportional control to align the robot's heading with the human's path direction. A key finding is that pure navigation transfers **zero-shot** from human video to mobile robots without needing any robot navigation teleoperation data. For manipulation-heavy mobile tasks, mixing static robot teleoperation with mobile human video achieves high task success while preventing navigation drift.

#### High-Frequency Humanoid Retargeting

EMMA has been deployed on full humanoid systems, including the Rainbow Robotics wheeled humanoid. An analytical inverse kinematics solver running at 2,000 Hz maps full-body human motion — torso, arms, and head — directly onto humanoid joint configurations in real time. This closed-form analytical approach avoids the latency and local-minima issues of iterative optimization-based IK, enabling responsive whole-body retargeting.

### EgoScale: Scaling Egocentric Pre-Training

#### From Lab to Industrial Scale

EgoScale, a collaboration between Georgia Tech, NVIDIA, and Physical Intelligence, scales egocentric human pre-training to 20,000 hours. This scale is enabled by commercial data collection ecosystems: consumer apps such as DoorDash's Task.app pay individuals to collect egocentric video while performing everyday chores, wearing smartphones or smart glasses. The data is captured in the same EgoMimic format — eye-level egocentric video with hand tracking — creating a direct pipeline from crowd-sourced human activity to robot training data.

The robot platform used for evaluation is correspondingly advanced: dual 7-DoF arms with 22-DoF dexterous hands, creating a highly human-like upper body that can directly exploit the rich hand motion data captured from humans.

#### The Log-Linear Scaling Law

The central reported empirical finding is a log-linear relationship between pre-training data volume and action-prediction validation error:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{action\_val}}(H) = A - B \cdot \log_{10}(H_{\text{human}})
```

Across the measured range from 1,000 to 20,000 pre-training hours, validation action-prediction error follows a log-linear decline. The result resembles scaling laws observed in large language models, but here it is an empirical fit over the evaluated physical sensorimotor data range rather than a universal law.

Importantly, at small scales common in robotics research (tens to hundreds of hours), validation loss often fails to correlate with task success — a well-known frustration in the field. At 20,000-hour scale, this relationship stabilizes: validation loss becomes a reliable predictor of downstream real-robot task success. Scale transforms a noisy, unreliable metric into a trustworthy signal.

#### The Three-Stage Training Recipe

EgoScale establishes a training paradigm that mirrors the pre-train → SFT → RLHF structure of modern LLMs:

1. **Pre-Training**: Train a 3-billion-parameter VLA backbone on 20,000 hours of unlabelled egocentric human data. The model learns general sensorimotor priors — how hands move, how objects are grasped, how tasks unfold temporally.

2. **Mid-Training (Alignment)**: Train on approximately 300 paired human-robot tasks. This stage bridges the gap between general human priors and specific robot execution modes, teaching the model how human hand trajectories translate to robot joint commands.

3. **Post-Training / Fine-Tuning**: Deploy on a specific target task with as few as 1 robot demonstration plus 100 human demonstrations. Tasks demonstrated include rolling shirts, operating syringes, and bottle capping. Without pre-training, success drops to near zero. With the full three-stage pipeline, the model achieves high success rates from minimal robot-specific data.

### Multi-Embodiment Emergence and the OpenX Collaboration

#### Two Competing Paradigms

Collaborative work with Physical Intelligence reveals an alternative path to human-to-robot transfer that does not require explicit mid-training alignment. The two paradigms represent different philosophies about where the "intelligence" in physical AI should reside:

| Dimension                   | EgoScale Paradigm                                  | Multi-Embodiment Emergence Paradigm                 |
| :-------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------- |
| **Pre-training Foundation** | Super-sized human egocentric data (20,000 h)       | Super-sized multi-robot datasets (OpenX Embodiment) |
| **Alignment Mechanism**     | Explicit mid-training on aligned human-robot pairs | Emergent embodiment-agnostic latent space           |
| **Role of Human Data**      | Primary foundation for pre-training                | Post-training stringing together robot primitives   |

#### Emergent Transfer via Diversity

The multi-embodiment paradigm trains a single VLA model across datasets collected from dozens of distinct physical robot platforms — Franka, UR5, ALOHA, WidowX, and others — as found in the OpenX Embodiment dataset. The key empirical finding is that downstream robot task performance shows a **drastically larger jump** when human post-training data is added, and this jump grows as pre-training robot diversity increases.

Latent-space measurements support the proposed mechanism: increasing robot embodiment diversity during pre-training decreases the measured distance between human and robot feature clusters:

$$
d_{\text{latent}}(\mathcal{Z}_{\text{human}}, \mathcal{Z}_{\text{robot}}) \propto \frac{1}{\text{Diversity}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{pretrain}})}
$$

Multi-robot pre-training forces the VLA to learn primitive motor skills — picking, placing, pushing — across varied kinematics, link lengths, and joint configurations. The model learns that these skills are embodiment-agnostic: "picking up a cup" has the same semantic and spatial structure regardless of whether the arm has 6 or 7 degrees of freedom. Post-training human video then acts as a high-level planner, "stringing together" these primitive robot skills to solve complex long-horizon tasks. The human data provides the task structure; the multi-robot pre-training provides the execution capability.

The reported experiments therefore suggest an emergent property: cross-domain human-to-robot transfer is weak at small dataset scales or with single-robot pre-training, then becomes substantially stronger as embodiment diversity grows. In this paradigm, that transfer is not directly supervised by an explicit alignment stage.

### EgoVerse: Open Data Infrastructure for Physical AI

#### The Need for Open Scale

Historical precedents demonstrate the transformative power of open, standardized datasets: ImageNet for vision, COCO for object detection, Atari for reinforcement learning, Common Crawl for language models. Physical AI currently lacks an equivalent. The work estimates that open-source usable human egocentric data is capped at approximately 10,000 hours and argues that frontier applications may require 1 million to 10 million hours.

The EgoVerse Consortium — spanning Georgia Tech, Stanford, UCSD, ETH Zurich, Meta, Scale AI, and Mecka — addresses this gap by building a multi-institutional, open-source research ecosystem. The data architecture combines two complementary streams:

- **Flagship Tasks**: Standardized semantic task specifications (shirt folding, saucer placement) collected across different labs, environments, and objects. These provide a controlled baseline for scientific comparison.
- **Free-Form Data**: Unconstrained, long-tail human activity captured in the wild by commercial partners and crowdsourcing. This provides the diversity and scale needed for generalization.

#### Demonstrator Diversity

A benchmark study comparing 4 demonstrators with 12 under fixed total data volume suggests that human diversity can be as important as total volume. With 4 demonstrators, t-SNE visualizations place held-out human data at the edge of the learned distribution; with 12, held-out data integrates more closely with the core distribution. The following expression summarizes the observed decrease in outlier distance as the subject pool grows:

$$
\text{Dist}_{\text{latent}}(\mathbf{z}_{\text{test}}, \mathcal{Z}_{\text{train}}) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\text{demonstrators}}}}
$$

This has direct implications for data collection strategy: scaling the number of distinct human demonstrators — with their varying body dimensions, movement habits, and visual perspectives — is at least as important as scaling the total hours of data. A dataset of 1,000 hours from 10 people may generalize worse than 500 hours from 50 people.

### Future Frontiers: Force, Memory, and the "Last Inch"

#### Beyond Pure Visual Observation

The current framework, while powerful, has clear limitations. First-person camera views capture what a human sees, but fail to capture how hard a human squeezes, how they detect slip through tactile feedback, or how they modulate compliance when reaching into a bag blindly. Electromyography (EMG) muscle sensors and tactile gloves attempt to capture force proxies, but the hardware remains noisy and immature. A promising direction is using tactile-sensorized human data as dense supervisory labels to train vision-only models to infer contact forces from visual cues alone — learning to "see" force.

#### Spatial Memory and Decision Context

Current VLA models predict actions purely from instantaneous image inputs. But humans make motor decisions based on long-horizon memory and background knowledge — remembering where ingredients were placed a minute ago, tracking which objects have already been manipulated, maintaining spatial awareness of the entire workspace. The next generation of models requires **dynamic 3D scene graphs** that continuously track, segment, and integrate point clouds of objects as they are manipulated across time. The extended policy should condition on:

```math
\mathbf{a}_t = \pi_\theta\!\left( \mathbf{o}_t, \, \mathcal{G}_{\text{scene}}(t), \, \mathbf{F}_{\text{tactile}}(t), \, \mathbf{l}_{\text{instruction}} \right)
```

where $G_{\text{scene}}(t)$ is a dynamic 3D scene graph memory tracking object displacements, $F_{\text{tactile}}(t)$ is an inferred or measured contact force vector, and $l_{\text{instruction}}$ is the language task description.

#### The "Last Inch" Problem

A fundamental geometric challenge arises at the final contact phase. Human hands and robot grippers have different morphologies — different numbers of fingers, different joint axis orientations, different finger length ratios. For macro-trajectories (approaching an object, transporting it), real-world human data is sufficient. But for the final inch of interaction — the precise finger configuration needed to grasp an irregular object, the compliant force modulation needed to insert a plug — the kinematic discrepancy between human and robot hands becomes critical. Synthetic counterfactual data, generated through simulation, is required to model these contact trajectories tailored to specific robot hardware.

#### The Teleoperation End Game

The long-term vision is to phase out teleoperation entirely in favor of unconstrained human capture. Teleoperation, by its nature, cannot capture reactive behaviors — catching a thrown object, flinching from an unexpected collision, the instinctive recovery from a slip. These behaviors are fundamental to robust manipulation and cannot be teleoperated because they are unconscious and instantaneous. The goal is to "dump humans.zip" into large foundation models: capture the full richness of natural human sensorimotor behavior through passive wearable sensing, and let the model learn to extract the robot-executable subset.
